# (I) Concept
The post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), also known as the post-anesthesia recovery room, is a unit for the intensive observation and monitoring of patients after anesthesia, as well as for continued treatment until the patient's vital signs stabilize.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# (I) Concept
The PACU is an important part of the modern anesthesiology department and one of the important indicators of the advancement of modern hospitals. The establishment of the PACU aims to closely monitor patients after anesthesia, allowing postoperative patients to smoothly transition through the anesthesia recovery period. It is also a way to accelerate the turnover of operating rooms and improve the utilization rate of operating rooms.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# (II) Background and history of establishment
Patients, due to various factors such as anesthesia, surgical procedures, and underlying diseases, may experience changes in condition within a few hours after surgery. The application of anesthetic drugs has not yet ceased, and various protective reflexes have not yet recovered, making this a high-risk period for patients. Complications such as respiratory and circulatory systems are likely to occur, and patients need to be carefully observed by medical staff to prevent any unexpected events. Since 1863, Western countries have started to establish PACUs, which are managed by well-trained medical staff to care for patients recovering from anesthesia. Due to the effective work of PACU medical staff, the incidence of early postoperative complications and mortality has been greatly reduced.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
# (II) Background and history of establishment
The establishment of PACUs in China began in the late 1950s, but only a few large hospitals nationwide had them, with small scale and irregular management. In recent years, the safety management of patients in the post-anesthesia recovery period has been increasingly emphasized, and PACUs have been established in major hospitals across China, with expanding scale and increasingly standardized management.
According to statistics, 50% of deaths occurring within 24 hours after surgery could be prevented through intensive monitoring.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# (III) Significance of establishing PACU
Rapidly detecting and treating changes in the condition of patients during the peri-anesthesia period, greatly reducing postoperative complications and mortality.
Safely and effectively controlling postoperative pain.
Increasing the efficiency of operating room utilization.
Relieving the tension of ICU beds and reducing the workload of ward nurses.
Alleviating the panic and psychological and spiritual stress of patients' families.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# Functions of PACU
1. Recovery and early recovery of patients after anesthesia.
2. Early postoperative treatment, including the detection and treatment of early postoperative complications of anesthesia and surgery.
3. Improving the patient's condition to facilitate further treatment in ICU, special care units, or general wards.
4. Assessing and determining the indications and timing for transferring patients to ICU, special care units, or general wards.
5. In special cases (such as the need for emergency reoperation), preoperative treatment and preparation of the patient's condition.

# Criteria for the Evaluation of Three-level General Hospitals
(I) Implement a grading and authorization management system for anesthesiologists and a mechanism for regular ability evaluation and reauthorization.
(II) Implement a patient pre-anesthesia condition assessment system, formulate treatment plans/schemes, and record risk assessment results in medical records.
(III) Informed consent of patients before anesthesia, including treatment risks, benefits, and other possible options.
(IV) Record the entire process of anesthesia operations in medical records/anesthesia sheets.
(V) Have a PACU with effective management measures, implement standardized whole-process monitoring, record the recovery status of patients after anesthesia, and implement measures to prevent anesthetic complications.
(VI) Establish management specifications and procedures for postoperative, chronic pain, and cancer pain patient analgesic treatment, and effectively implement them.
(VII) Establish effective communication between the anesthesiology department and the blood transfusion department, actively carry out autologous blood transfusion, strictly control the indications for intraoperative blood transfusion, and transfuse blood rationally and safely.
(VIII) The department head, head nurse, and qualified personnel form a quality and safety management team, use anesthetic work quality and safety regulations, job responsibilities, various anesthetic technical operation procedures, and quality and safety indicators to ensure patient anesthetic safety, regularly evaluate quality, and promote continuous improvement.

Location

# (1) Staff Management
1. Doctors: The PACU Director should divide necessary responsibilities among the team, responsible for removing tracheal tubes or other artificial airway devices in PACU. Anesthesiologists in PACU should not have anesthesia tasks outside of PACU. It is necessary to notify surgeons to be present to identify and early treat possible surgical complications; other departmental physicians may be requested for emergency consultations as needed.
2. Nurses: In large hospitals, PACU can be established as an independent nursing unit, with nurses configured according to work conditions, with a nurse-to-patient ratio of 1:1, 1:2, or 1:3 based on the severity of the condition. The work of PACU nurses should focus on bedside care. When patients with tracheal tubes are admitted to PACU, they require corresponding medical staff supervision; if patients admitted to PACU have removed artificial airways and are conscious or can be awakened, the number of beds managed by nurses can be appropriately increased.

The anesthetic nurse should measure and record the blood pressure, heart rate, heart rhythm, SpO2, respiratory rate, and recovery of consciousness at least every 15 minutes to judge the degree and speed of recovery. # Awakening of Patients Under General Anesthesia
The awakening of patients under general anesthesia refers to the process from the end of anesthesia surgery to the patient's awakening, which is the process of the patient's transition from an unconscious state to consciousness and the recovery of complete protective reflexes. It usually takes 30-50 minutes, and more than 3 hours is considered prolonged awakening. The following should be noted:

<!-- Slide number: 47 -->
1. Strengthen respiratory management: respiratory function gradually recovers during the awakening period, and improper management is prone to hypoxia. Antagonize the residual muscle relaxants to restore spontaneous breathing. The standard for judging whether the recovery of spontaneous breathing function is satisfactory is that the patient's pulse oxygen saturation remains above 95% for more than 15 minutes in a quiet state (elderly or special patients should reach the level before anesthesia). The removal of the endotracheal tube after general anesthesia is a critical moment of awakening and should be removed after the recovery of spontaneous breathing is satisfactory.

<!-- Slide number: 48 -->
2. When the patient develops respiratory failure, hypothermia, delayed awakening, significant hemodynamic instability, or airway inflammation damage (such as extensive oral surgery), the endotracheal tube should be retained after surgery until these conditions improve before extubation.
3. Promptly handle complications: arrhythmias, hypertension, hypotension, myocardial ischemia, and respiratory depression are common complications during the awakening period and should be handled promptly.
4. Application of general anesthesia awakening drugs: generally, anesthetic awakening drugs should be used as little as possible. If necessary, use targeted awakening drugs and start with a low dose.

<!-- Slide number: 49 -->
# Awakening and Recovery of Patients Under Inhalation Anesthesia
The awakening of patients under inhalation anesthesia can be considered as the washing out process of inhalation anesthetics. Low-flow anesthesia should be turned off earlier than high-flow anesthesia at the end of the operation. After the entire operation is completed, high-flow pure oxygen is used to rapidly flush out the residual anesthetics in the patient and the circuit.

<!-- Slide number: 50 -->
# Precautions for Awakening of Patients Under Inhalation Anesthesia
1. The cleaner the inhalation anesthetic is washed out, the more conducive it is to the smooth process of awakening and the patient's recovery. Excessive residual anesthetics can not only cause the patient to be restless and vomit but may also suppress the state of consciousness and respiration.
2. Reduce the inhalation volume before the end of the operation, and intravenously administer certain analgesics, which is conducive to the early excretion of inhalation anesthesia, increases the patient's tolerance to the endotracheal tube, and reduces the stress response during extubation. There is still a certain analgesic effect after the operation, and the consciousness is relatively quiet and stable.
3. The anesthetic depth should be sufficient when extubating, the muscle relaxant effect should be completely eliminated, the patient's spontaneous breathing should recover to a sufficient ventilation volume, and there must be a certain anesthetic depth to avoid "asphyxia" or active laryngeal contraction. With the shallowing of anesthesia, the following respiratory signs appear in sequence: spontaneous breathing, irregular breathing, swallowing movements, and respiratory end stoppage, and extubation should be performed immediately. Extubation too early due to shallow anesthesia can lead to asphyxia, tachycardia, hypertension, and other conditions.
4. Some antagonists have a certain effect on reversing the inhibitory effect of anesthetics, but due to the side effects of antagonists, routine application is not recommended.

<!-- Slide number: 51 -->
# Indications for Extubation After General Anesthesia
(1) After the end of surgery and the cessation of anesthesia, the patient's consciousness recovers, there are directive actions, and the circulatory function is stable.
(2) Spontaneous breathing is restored, the respiratory rate reaches 14-20 times/min, and the SpO2 is >95% when breathing air.
(3) The residual effect of muscle relaxants has disappeared, respiratory movements are normal, both sides of the respiratory are symmetrical, and chest and abdominal breathing are symmetrical. The time from the last administration of muscle relaxants is more than 1 hour, and muscle relaxant antagonism has been performed.
(4) If necessary, measure tidal volume (VT) and arterial blood gas analysis. After breathing air for 10 minutes, PaO2 and PaCO2 are within the normal range or close to the preoperative level.

<!-- Slide number: 52 -->
# Conditions for Extubation
1. Recovery of consciousness and muscle strength, the ability to open eyes, open mouth, protrude the tongue, shake hands according to instructions, and raise the upper limbs for more than 10 seconds.
2. Good recovery of spontaneous breathing, without signs of respiratory distress.
3. Recovery of the pharyngeal reflex.
4. No secretions in the nasal cavity, oral cavity, and trachea.

<!-- Slide number: 54 -->
# Tracheal Extubation Techniques
(1) Pre-extubation preparation: Suction out secretions from the oral cavity, pharynx, and trachea; inhale pure oxygen for 2-3 minutes; prepare oropharyngeal airways and anesthesia masks for artificial respiration; prepare laryngoscope and tracheal tube if necessary.
(2) Tracheal extubation methods: Deflate the cuff. ① Directly remove the tube; ② Remove the tube while the lungs are being inflated by the anesthesia machine; ③ Insert the suction catheter through the tube into the trachea, while suctioning, remove the tube.
(3) Close observation after extubation: Observe for the return of spontaneous breathing, airway patency, ventilation, and oxygenation.

<!-- Slide number: 55 -->
# Indications for Delayed Extubation
(1) Patients with significant respiratory dysfunction before surgery, or those with significant impact on respiratory function from surgery and anesthesia.
(2) Patients with prolonged surgery time and severe surgical trauma.
(3) Patients with unstable circulatory function before or during surgery.
(4) Patients with delayed awakening and difficulty in maintaining airway patency.

# Ensuring Criteria for Patient Transfer Before Discharge
1. Patient's heart rate and blood pressure are stable, and there will be no significant changes during transportation without monitoring.
2. Patient's breathing is restored well, with sufficient tidal volume.
3. Proper handling of any issues that may arise during transportation (such as airway obstruction, vomiting, etc.).
4. Patient's physiological function is stable, and there will be no significant changes every 10 minutes of observation.

<!-- Slide number: 59 -->
# 3. Criteria for Patient Discharge from PACU
Aldret Score Criteria
(1) Muscles: Ability to move limbs and raise the head;
(2) Respiration: Normal breathing and coughing;
(3) Circulation: Stable circulation, with fluctuations of < ±20% compared to before surgery;
(4) SPO2: >92% under room air;
(5) Consciousness: Fully awake.
For patients with a score of less than or equal to 5 who show no signs of improvement after treatment, they should be transferred to the intensive care unit for further treatment.

# Indications for Patient Discharge from the Recovery Room

1. Central nervous system criteria: Patients with normal consciousness before surgery regain consciousness, are alert, and have purposeful movements; orientation is restored, able to recognize time and place; muscle tone is restored, and the ability to keep the head up while lying flat for more than 10 seconds.
2. Respiratory system criteria: Able to maintain airway patency spontaneously, swallowing and cough reflexes have returned; ventilation function is normal, respiratory rate is 12-30/min, able to cough, and respiratory secretions are expelled; PaCO2 is within the normal range or at the preoperative level, SpO2 is higher than 70mmHg under room air, or SpO2 is higher than 95%.
3. Circulatory system criteria: Heart rate and blood pressure are within ±20% of the preoperative values and stable for more than 30 minutes; rhythm is normal, and ST-T changes on ECG or recovery to preoperative levels.
4. After spinal anesthesia, respiratory and circulatory stability, anesthesia level below T6; more than 1 hour after the last dose of anesthesia; sensory and motor nerve block has recovered, sympathetic nerve block has recovered; circulatory function is stable, and no need for pressor drugs.
5. After postoperative use of analgesic or sedative drugs, observe for 30 minutes without any abnormal reactions.
6. No acute complications of anesthesia or surgery, such as pneumothorax, active bleeding, etc. Chest and lung X-rays show no special abnormalities, urine output is >25ml/hour, electrolytes and hemoglobin concentration are within normal ranges.

# 4. Patient Transfer Protocol
Routine for Patient Transfer
1. Inform the ward when transferring the patient out of the recovery room so that the ward can prepare for handover.
2. All patients must have a small monitoring device during transportation, and an oxygen bag should be brought along if necessary for oxygen administration during the journey.
3. When the anesthetist has a concurrent surgery, issue an oral order, and the anesthetist nurse will escort the patient to the ward.
4. Upon arrival at the ward, assist the on-duty staff in safely transferring the patient to the bed.
5. Measure the patient's blood pressure and pulse oxygen saturation, hand over the patient's vital signs and special circumstances during surgery to the on-duty staff, and ensure that the drainage tubes and catheters are patent, and check for any damage or leakage in the drainage bags and urine bags. Only after both parties and the family confirm that the patient is fully awake and vital signs are stable can the departure be made. For patients with analgesic pumps, the family must be informed of the use and precautions of the analgesic pump.

# Handover Protocol
The patient transferred out of PACU is accompanied by the anesthetist, surgeon, and PACU nurse and is handed over to the ward physician and nurse through an oral handover. The handover includes:

(1) Anesthesia method
(2) Surgical method and postoperative diagnosis;
(3) Changes in the patient's condition and special treatment during surgery and recovery;
(4) The patient's use of medications, intravenous fluids, and blood transfusions in the operating room and PACU, and the patient's reaction to various medications.
(5) Effective drainage of various drainage tubes;
(6) Integrity of the patient's skin;
(7) Items and medications brought into the hospital by the patient.

# I. Respiratory System Complications

# I. Respiratory Obstruction

Respiratory obstruction due to respiratory depression or due to issues related to respiration accounts for 70% of all anesthetic complications, of which 40%~70% lead to patient death or disability.

### Notes:

# 1、Posterior Tongue Drop
- **Incomplete**: Snoring (Snore)
- Tongue drop blocking the pharynx
- **Complete**: Only respiratory movements, no gas exchange, SpO2↓
**Causes**:
- Sedatives, analgesics, general anesthetics, and muscle relaxants → Relaxation of the mandible and tongue muscles
- Tongue drops towards the pharynx, blocking the upper airway

**Prevention and Management**:
- When tongue drop occurs, the head can be tilted back, the mandible can be lifted, or an oropharyngeal airway can be placed.

# 2、Secretion and Foreign Body Obstruction in the Oral Cavity
| **Cause** | **Prevention and Management** |
| --- | --- |
| Anesthetic agents that irritate the airway → Increased secretion | Administer adequate anticholinergic drugs preoperatively |
| Bronchiectasis, wet lung, etc. → Large amounts of sputum and blood blocking the airway | Double-lumen intubation, adequate aspiration during surgery |
| Nasopharynx, oral cavity surgery → Accumulation of blood, gauze blocking | Thoroughly aspirate secretions through the tracheal tube |
| Fallen teeth or dentures blocking the airway | Remove dentures, mark and secure loose teeth with sutures or extract them before anesthesia |

# 3、Inflammatory Lesions in the Oral and Pharyngeal Areas, Laryngeal Masses, and Allergic Laryngeal Edema

- **Peritonsillar abscess, post-pharyngeal abscess, laryngeal Ca, vocal cord polyps, epiglottic cyst, allergic laryngeal edema** → Partial upper airway obstruction: Dyspnea, unable to perform oral intubation.
- **Extremely sensitive to the throat** → Thiopental can cause severe laryngospasm → Asphyxiation death. Such patients should first consider tracheostomy.
- **Allergic laryngeal edema** → Antiallergic treatment, pressure oxygen administration → If SpO2 does not improve → Tracheostomy

# 4、Laryngospasm
**Causes**:
- Respiratory protective reflex → Overactive glottis closure reflex

**Clinical Manifestations**:
- **Inspiratory dyspnea, high-pitched inspiratory wheezing**.
- Laryngospasm → Increased excitability of the vagus nerve that支配s the pharynx → Increased pharyngeal stress → Increased glottis closure activity.
- Occurs during general anesthesia stages I-Ⅱ (light general anesthesia), thiopental is prone to induce laryngospasm.

# 4、Laryngospasm
**Management**:
- **Mild**: Inspiratory stridor: Can be relieved spontaneously after removing local irritation.
- **Moderate**: Inspiratory and expiratory stridor: Oxygen administration via face mask is required.
- **Severe**: Glottis closure, complete airway obstruction, use of a large-bore needle to puncture the cricothyroid membrane to aspirate O2 or intravenous muscle relaxant → Oxygen administration under pressure or tracheal intubation.

**Prevention**:
- Avoid气管插管 or surgical procedures under light general anesthesia, prevent oxygen deficiency and CO2 retention.

# 5、Lower Respiratory Tract Obstruction
**Causes**:
- **Mechanical obstruction** is common in twisted tracheal tubes, tubes with excessively long bevels that are tightly adhered to the tracheal wall, aspiration of sputum or vomitus blocking the trachea and bronchi.
- **Functional obstruction** is due to bronchospasm.

# 6、Abnormal Intubation Position and Airway Occlusion
**Causes**:
- ▲ Tube twisting, compression, or overinsertion into one side of the bronchus
- ▲ Too shallow, extruding, and airway occlusion by sputum
- ▲ Twisted threaded tube, breathing valve failure → SpO2↓, abnormal respiratory movements

**Management**: Address the cause

# 7、Reflux and Aspiration
**Causes**:
- After the administration of morphine, general anesthetics, and muscle relaxants → Relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter → Reflux of gastric contents → Severe airway obstruction in the lower respiratory tract → Aspiration mortality of 50%-75%.
- Aspiration of gastric fluid → Sudden bronchospasm, tachypnea, difficulty breathing, diffuse wet rales in the lungs, severe hypoxemia

**Prevention**:
- **For elective surgery**: 8h fasting from clear liquids, 3h fasting, 4h fasting from water
- Prepare suction equipment, nasogastric tube decompression.

**Management**:
- When aspiration occurs → Head in a low position, turn to one side, suction
- Bronchodilator, bronchoscopy if necessary

# 8、Bronchospasm
**Causes**:
- ● Tracheal intubation, reflux aspiration, suction
- ● Surgical stimulation → Reflex spasm
- ● Thiopental, morphine, etc. → Mast cell release of histamine → Induce spasm

**Manifestations**:
- Inspiratory dyspnea, wheezing, difficult expiration, HR↑ or arrhythmia

**Management**:
- ● Mild: Hand-controlled breathing can improve.
- ● Severe bronchospasm: Use bronchodilators or corticosteroids
- ● Hypoxemia or CO2 retention induced by lack of oxygen → Use IPPV ventilator mode
- ● Stimulation-induced bronchospasm under light general anesthesia → Deepen anesthesia and use muscle relaxants

# 9、Insufficient Ventilation
**Causes**:
① Anesthetic agents suppress the respiratory center, muscle relaxants paralyze the respiratory muscles, and auxiliary ventilation is not sufficient.
② Residual suppression of the oxygen-hypoxia ventilation response by inhaled anesthetics.
③ The action of anesthetic analgesics during surgery, especially in the elderly and obese.
④ Insufficient ventilation occurs during the recovery period, in addition to oxygen retention, hypoxemia can also occur, the threat of which is even greater.

# 10、Insufficient Ventilation
**Prevention and Management**:
① Auxiliary or controlled ventilation should be appropriate to avoid insufficient ventilation or prolonged overventilation.
② Strengthen respiratory function monitoring, especially for elderly and obese "high-risk" patients.
③ Strictly control the indications for extubation, that is, the patient can open their eyes, lift their head, and clench their fist, to avoid or reduce insufficient ventilation during the anesthetic recovery period.

# 11、Hypoxemia
**Causes**:
① Anesthetic machine failure, insufficient oxygen supply, etc. lead to low inspired oxygen concentration.
② Tracheal tube displacement
③ Microatelectasis can occur under general anesthesia, and it can last until after surgery.
④ Functional residual capacity can decrease by 15%-20% under general anesthesia, increasing respiratory muscle tension, resulting in further reduction in lung volume.
⑤ During airway obstruction or insufficient ventilation, hypoxemia and hypercapnia can occur simultaneously.
⑥ Upper abdominal surgery is more prone to postoperative hypoxemia than other types of surgery.
⑦ Elderly, obese, and smokers, due to increased closing volume, are more prone to hypoxemia during and after surgery.

# 12、Hypoxemia
**Prevention and Management**:
① Eliminate the cause, such as airway obstruction.
② Monitor blood gas and SpO2 during surgery, and detect and treat hypoxemia early.
③ For hypoxemia caused by atelectasis and reduced lung volume, PEEP (5-10cmH2O) can be used for treatment.
④ Monitor SpO2 in patients during the recovery period from general anesthesia, and administer oxygen via a face mask to maintain SpO2 ≥94%.
⑤ High-risk patients after surgery should undergo prophylactic mechanical ventilation.

## Four: Shivering

Shivering after anaesthesia refers to involuntary muscle contractions in patients
during the awakening period after anaesthesia. Generally, it first manifests as
peripheral vasoconstriction and central body temperature drop. Shivering can not
only increase intraocular pressure and intracranial pressure but can also increase
oxygen consumption and CO2 production by 2~3 times. This increase in metabolism is
extremely detrimental to patients with pulmonary shunt, fixed cardiac output, and
reduced respiratory reserve.
Therefore, effective prevention and treatment of shivering during the perioperative
period is one of the key measures in anaesthetic management. The exact mechanism
of shivering is still unclear.

## Four: Shivering
  Causes
1. Preoperative Medication: Patients who receive preoperative anticholinergic and
  benzodiazepine drugs can reduce the occurrence of shivering, while patients who
  receive preoperative analgesics have a higher incidence of shivering than those who
  do not receive analgesics.
2. Anaesthetic and Surgical Factors: Volatile anaesthetic agents are more likely to
  cause shivering, local anaesthetic toxicity can cause shivering, fentanyl and
  pethidine can reduce the occurrence of shivering. The longer the surgery time, the
  higher the incidence of shivering.
3. Hypothermia: Patients with hypothermia often experience shivering when waking up
  from general anaesthesia, and the mechanism may be that anaesthetic agents inhibit
  the thermoregulatory system, lower the threshold of shivering, and when the
  anaesthetic effect disappears, the threshold of shivering returns to normal, thus
  causing a difference between the body's hypothermic state and the current normal
  body temperature threshold, leading to shivering.
4. Patient Factors: The incidence of shivering in male patients is higher than that in
  female patients, elective surgery patients are higher than emergency patients, and
  young adults are higher than children and the elderly.
5/11/2026

## Four: Shivering
  Prevention and Management
Pay attention to warming, prevent body temperature drop
Medication: Opioids such as pethidine can effectively treat post-anaesthetic
  shivering, with an efficacy rate of more than 73%. Fentanyl is less effective than
  pethidine in treating shivering and has a shorter duration of action. Other drugs such
  as desflurane can also effectively prevent the occurrence of post-anaesthetic shivering.
