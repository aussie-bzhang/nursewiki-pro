# Orthopaedic Perioperative Patient Care

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->
# Introduction Before the Class
Comprehensive perioperative nursing care is an important guarantee for the success of surgery. The focus of preoperative care is to conduct a comprehensive assessment, identify and eliminate factors that threaten surgical safety, meticulously prepare for various aspects, and provide health guidance to ensure that patients can tolerate surgery well. Surgery is a trauma for patients, which can cause a series of physical injuries and even severe complications that may endanger the patient's life. During the surgery, the focus of nursing care is to actively cooperate with the surgeon, closely monitor vital signs, promptly detect and rescue from respiratory and cardiac arrest, and protect patients from unexpected injuries. The focus of postoperative care is to restore the patient's normal physiological function as soon as possible, observe and prevent complications, actively take measures to promote wound healing, and maximize the recovery of joint function.

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->
# I. Definition of Perioperative Period
The perioperative period refers to the time from the decision to undergo surgery to discharge. During this period, the patient's body and spirit will undergo significant changes. Healthcare workers must perform perioperative nursing care to ensure the smooth progress of surgery and the patient's postoperative recovery.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
# II. Orthopaedic Patient Perioperative Care
Orthopaedic patient perioperative care is divided into preoperative, postoperative, postoperative recovery, and discharge periods. Preoperative care includes preoperative care, preoperative education, and preoperative preparation; postoperative care includes postoperative monitoring, postoperative care, and postoperative education; during the postoperative recovery stage, it includes functional exercise, physical rehabilitation, dietary guidance, and prevention of complications; upon discharge, the patient should be educated on the discharge process, health education, functional exercise, and follow-up consultations if necessary.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
# (I) Preoperative Care
Surgery is both an important means of surgical treatment and a traumatic process, which can cure diseases while also causing complications or sequelae. Orthopaedic surgery includes a wide range of procedures, covering operations on limbs and trunk, bones, joints, muscles, tendons, as well as various surgeries on the spinal cord, peripheral nerves, and blood vessels, and some plastic surgeries. Patients undergoing surgery not only have to endure the pain of physical illness but also experience the stimulation of anesthesia and surgical trauma; in addition, patients may also experience psychological burdens. The success of surgery not only depends on the success of the surgery itself but also on the preparation before and the cooperation after the surgery. Preoperative education, as an important part of comprehensive nursing, has received widespread attention.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# 1. Preoperative Education
Preoperative education should inform patients about the surgical method and anesthesia, understand the process of surgery, and understand the impact of surgery on life, thereby alleviating the anxiety and unease of patients and their families caused by surgery, enabling patients to face surgery bravely and recover smoothly after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# 2. Preoperative Care Assessment
Assessing the patient is the first step in preoperative education, and the patient's physical and emotional state, as well as their understanding ability, should be included in the assessment. The assessment stage is an excellent opportunity for nurses to establish a harmonious relationship with patients and their families. The first impression is very important; the nurse's image and self-introduction, treating patients equally, help patients maintain a sense of control and self-esteem.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
# (1) Collection of General Information and Medical History of the Patient
Routine preoperative checks for orthopaedic patients include assessing the overall condition of the body, including inquiring about the patient's medical history, family history, past medical history, genetic history, reproductive history, and current medication status, etc.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# (2) Assessment of Physical Condition
① Comprehensive assessment of the patient's nutritional status. The patient's nutritional status is directly related to their tolerance of surgery, and appropriate nutrition is necessary for wound healing and postoperative recovery, but it is not encouraged to overeat. Nurses should consider the patient's height, weight, triceps skinfold thickness, upper arm muscle circumference, as well as the patient's appetite, mental appearance, and work capacity, and combine them with the condition and laboratory test results to comprehensively evaluate the patient's nutritional status.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# (2) Assessment of Physical Condition
② Assessment of cardiac function. It is necessary to understand whether the patient has any abnormalities in heart rate and rhythm, and if necessary, to undergo ambulatory electrocardiogram monitoring. Arrhythmia patients have reduced tolerance to surgery and anesthesia and are prone to induce heart failure. Patients should be guided to actively accept drug treatment; if the patient has a history of heart disease, the nurse should promptly notify the doctor and assist the patient in undergoing various cardiac function tests, such as stress tests, echocardiography, Doppler, etc. The results of cardiac function tests should be reported to the doctor in a timely manner.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
# (2) Assessment of Physical Condition
③ Assessment of pulmonary function. Patients with pulmonary diseases, such as emphysema and bronchiectasis, may increase the risk of surgery due to impaired gas exchange. Nurses should strengthen the observation of the patient's respiratory rhythm and frequency before surgery, understand whether there are smoking and drinking habits, asthma, cough, expectoration, and observe the color and nature of sputum. Patients with known pulmonary diseases or a smoking history should be reported to the doctor in a timely manner, and assistance should be provided for the patient to undergo pulmonary function tests.

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
# (2) Assessment of Physical Condition
④ Inquiry into past medical history, such as hypertension, diabetes, infectious disease history, and surgical history, etc.

# (2) Assessment of Physical Condition
⑤ Assessment of psychological condition. Orthopaedic diseases, especially trauma, are often sudden events, and patients lack psychological preparation. Surgery often involves pain and other severe discomfort or functional impairment, so the psychological contradictions of patients are very prominent. In addition to fragility, emotional fluctuations, and increased dependence, the most common is worry about the outcome of surgery, fear of misdiagnosis or mistreatment, fear of anesthesia, pain, and postoperative complications. These psychological reactions may worsen as the surgery approaches. Therefore, before surgery, nurses should comprehensively assess the patient's psychological condition, correctly guide and correct adverse psychological reactions in a timely manner, and ensure the smooth progress of surgery.

# 3. Methods and Content of Preoperative Education
(1) Face-to-face conversation. When talking to patients and their families, try to use common words to accurately explain the significance of the diagnosis and the details of surgical treatment, avoiding complex language and medical professional terms. Patients are eager to learn about the treatment methods, effects, and the skills of doctors and nurses for the diseases they have, as well as whether the treatment costs can be borne. At this time, healthcare workers should listen patiently to the patient's questions, provide necessary explanations, and eliminate the patient's doubts.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# 3. Methods and Content of Preoperative Education
(2) Use of promotional brochures. The content listed in the brochures is beneficial for patients and their families to understand. The font used in the brochures should be easy to read, and key words should be printed in bold. These brochures can allow patients to review and consider preoperative education materials. Written materials are good reading materials, but they cannot replace face-to-face conversations with patients.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# 3. Methods and Content of Preoperative Education
(3) Demonstration to patients. Demonstrating the techniques of deep breathing, turning over, brace fitting, specific methods and matters for limb rehabilitation is far more effective than verbal instructions. Use visual aids such as the patient's X-ray films, spinal models, relevant instruments, and braces to educate the patient, as this makes it easier for them to understand and accept.

# Content of Preoperative Education Includes:
① Help patients gain a comprehensive understanding of the treatment plan. The nurse and patient review the preoperative and postoperative X-ray films of a similar patient together, explaining the anatomical structures, surgical steps, issues that can be addressed, and the expected goals. Explain the surgical approach and indicate the location of one or more incisions. Additionally, invite patients with the same disease to share their experiences in cooperating with treatment and nursing, which can help alleviate the patient's concerns and anxiety, and enhance their confidence in the surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# Content of Preoperative Education Includes:
② Teach patients the methods of functional exercise, which is beneficial for postoperative rehabilitation training.
③ Respiratory preparation: According to the patient's surgical site, perform postoperative adaptive exercises, such as demonstrating to the patient how to perform deep breathing and how to cough effectively. Especially for patients undergoing general anesthesia and elderly patients (especially smokers), guide them on effective expectoration. The method is to first cough lightly to loosen the sputum, then take a deep breath and cough forcefully to expel the sputum.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Content of Preoperative Education Includes:
④ The nurse should list the situations the patient may encounter after surgery. The patient and family are most concerned about common issues that directly affect them before and after the surgery, such as the duration of the surgery, the type of anesthesia, and the family waiting area. Inform the patient and family about whether the patient will need to stay in the intensive care unit and for how long. Explain that the patient may need to use a catheter, vacuum drain, intravenous infusion, and postoperative monitoring equipment, so that the family is not overly anxious when they see the patient after surgery. Also, inform the family that there may be facial edema due to the patient lying on their stomach and intravenous infusion during surgery. Discuss with the family how to turn the patient over, how to sit and walk at the bedside, and when to start these activities. If the patient needs to wear a brace after surgery, explain the process and method of making the brace, and detail the use and wearing time of the brace.

# Content of Preoperative Education Includes:
⑤ Guidance for diagnostic tests: Healthcare professionals should inform patients that contraindications should be excluded before undergoing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), such as those with pacemakers, metallic implants in the body, and early pregnant women. Patients who can undergo MRI should remove metallic items such as necklaces, watches, bone traction needles, and magnetic objects to avoid interference with the test results and damage to items.

# Content of Preoperative Education Includes:
⑥ Special treatment checks. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is often used for patients with poor limb blood supply or nerve trophic disorders after trauma. Before entering the oxygen chamber, healthcare professionals should inform the patient and family of the seriousness of the flammability and explosiveness of the hyperbaric oxygen chamber, and strictly prohibit bringing items such as matches, lighters, cigarettes, alcohol, gasoline, firecrackers, and electric toys into the chamber to avoid causing explosions. It is not advisable to wear clothing that can produce sparks, and change to all-cotton clothing as required.

# 4. Preoperative Preparation
(1) Assist the patient in completing preoperative examinations. Preoperative examinations should include chest X-ray, electrocardiogram, blood cell count, blood biochemistry, coagulation time, urine routine, stool routine, liver and kidney function tests. If the patient is not undergoing autologous blood transfusion, check for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) titers, etc.
(2) Prepare for blood transfusion and skin testing before major surgery.
(3) Monitor vital signs, temperature, pulse, and respiration, three times a day the day before surgery. Measure temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure in the morning of the surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
# 4. Preoperative Preparation
(4) Gastrointestinal preparation: ① Guide the patient to train for defecation in bed for three days before surgery to prevent constipation due to the inconvenience of lying in bed after surgery; ② If there is no defecation for three consecutive days, inform the doctor so that medication can be used to relieve constipation or enema; ③ For patients undergoing elective surgery, use soap water or glycerin suppositories for enema the night before surgery to remove accumulated stool in the intestines to prevent contamination and postoperative constipation and bloating due to the relaxation of the anal sphincter after anesthesia during surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
# 4. Preoperative Preparation
(5) Urinary practice: Patients may develop urinary retention after surgery due to anesthesia, trauma, or not being accustomed to urinating in bed, especially in male patients. The nurse should guide the patient in practicing urination and defecation in bed before surgery, and inform the patient of the possibility of having a catheter inserted after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
# 4. Preoperative Preparation
(6) Preoperative skin preparation. The purpose of preoperative skin preparation is to reduce the number of skin bacteria and lower the infection rate of surgical wounds without damaging the integrity of the skin. The focus is on cleaning the skin around the surgical site and removing hair. For orthopedic surgery, the skin preparation needs to extend beyond the range of the upper and lower joints, and it should start three days before surgery because its sterile requirements are stricter than those of other surgeries. The specific methods are as follows: start cleaning the local area around the incision and the surrounding skin three days before surgery, and clean the hair in the surgical area again the day before surgery. Take a bath or sponge bath, wash hair, cut fingernails and toenails, shave facial hair, and after washing, change into a hospital gown. If the patient has tinea pedis or skin ulcers, report to the doctor for treatment as soon as possible. For patients with hand and foot diseases, soak the hands and feet for 20 minutes each day since admission. # 4. Preoperative Preparation
(7) Dietary preparation. Patients should be guided to consume high-calorie, high-protein, vitamin, and pectin-rich foods to enhance their resistance and correct malnutrition; consume high-fiber foods and drink plenty of water to maintain bowel regularity. Patients should be fasting for 12 hours before surgery and 4 hours before drinking to prevent vomiting and aspiration during anesthesia or surgery, which may lead to aspiration pneumonia or asphyxia and other accidents.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# 4. Preoperative Preparation
(8) Preoperative morning preparation. Thirty minutes before surgery, the nurse should administer pre-anesthetic medication to the patient, prepare the patient's medical records, X-ray films, CT, and MRI films, and instruct the patient: ① Remove jewelry, non-fixed dentures, glasses, etc.; ② Remove valuable items and hand them over to the family for safekeeping; ③ Empty the bladder before going to the operating room, and if there is a catheter, use a clip to fasten it to the pants leg to prevent it from falling off.
(9) Preparation of the bed unit. After the patient is taken away, change the clean bedding; prepare the anesthetic bed and items according to the type of surgery and anesthesia. Prepare items such as resuscitation kits, monitors, suction devices, oxygen supplies, inflatable pads to elevate the limbs, sandbags to stabilize the neck, etc.

# (II) Postoperative Nursing
Postoperative nursing refers to the period from the patient's return to the ward after surgery until they are basically recovered and discharged. Firstly, we need to conduct a nursing assessment, including evaluating whether the patient is conscious upon returning from the operating room; understanding the patient's blood loss during surgery, as well as the administration of intravenous fluids and blood transfusions; and the type of drainage tube placed after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 30 -->
# 1. Postoperative nurses should closely monitor the patient's vital signs
① Closely monitor the patient's blood pressure. For major surgery patients, measure the blood pressure every 15 to 30 minutes until stable, then change to once every 1 to 2 hours, and ensure proper nursing records are kept.
② Measure the patient's body temperature every 4 hours postoperatively, as temperature changes are a defense mechanism of the human body after various physical, chemical, and biological stimuli. An elevated temperature often indicates the presence of some stimulus. After surgery, due to the stimulation of the surgical trauma, the body temperature may slightly rise, but not exceed 38°C, and no treatment is required for the patient. If the body temperature remains above 38°C after 3 days, the cause of the fever should be analyzed.
③ Observe the pulse. The pulse changes with body temperature (for every 1°C increase in body temperature, the pulse increases by 10 to 20 beats/min). Loss of blood and fluid can lead to insufficient circulatory volume, causing the pulse to quicken, become weak, the blood pressure to drop, and the pulse pressure difference to decrease; however, a quickening pulse and rapid breathing can also indicate heart failure.
④ The assessment of respiration is particularly important. Prolonged anesthesia and subsequent changes in chest shape due to inadequate lung expansion, iatrogenic hemothorax or pneumothorax, can cause postoperative lung function damage; postoperative pulmonary embolism (such as shortness of breath, chest pain, decreased hemoglobin, and elevated body temperature). Lung function should be checked at each shift for 72 hours or longer postoperatively. Relevant signs should be noted.

# 2. Guidance on Postoperative Positioning
① Patients who have undergone general anesthesia and have not yet regained consciousness should be placed in a supine position for 6 hours, with the head turned to one side to prevent aspiration into the airway due to vomiting; patients who have undergone spinal anesthesia should be kept in a supine position for 6 to 8 hours to prevent headache due to cerebrospinal fluid leakage.
② After limb surgery, elevate the limbs using pillows, supports, etc., so that the limbs are slightly higher than the level of the heart, with the distal end of the limb higher than the proximal end, to facilitate rest and improve blood supply and venous return to the limbs, and to eliminate edema. It should not be too high or too low, as this can affect arterial blood supply or venous return.
③ Elderly or weak patients who are unable to get out of bed after surgery and patients with immobile lower limbs should lie on an air-filled bed. Postoperatively, the patient can be turned and massaged every 4 hours, and then every 2 hours thereafter to prevent pressure sores.
④ For patients who have undergone plaster immobilization, elevate the limbs, and position them in a way that is comfortable,有利于 venous blood return, eliminates swelling, and does not cause the plaster to break or compress local soft tissue. Avoid moving the limbs multiple times before the plaster dries, and do not use fingers to lift the plaster to prevent local skin pressure sores or circulatory disturbances due to plaster indentation.

# 3. Nursing of the surgical area and incisions
① Routine observation of the surgical area postoperatively: Observe for signs of bleeding, exudate, dressing detachment, and redness, swelling, heat, and pain. If any abnormalities are found, report them to the doctor for treatment; if the affected limb shows severe swelling, cold skin, pale nails, no change in color upon pressure, inability to move the limb freely, severe pain upon passive movement, weak or absent distal artery pulsation, and other signs indicating circulatory disturbances, inform the doctor immediately and observe for nerve injury.
② Patients who have undergone amputation and reimplantation should have their overall condition closely monitored, looking for signs of shock, renal failure, bleeding, anemia, vascular crisis, and infection.

# 4. Nursing of Drainage Tubes
For patients with wound drainage devices, ensure proper fixation to prevent twisting, loosening, and slippage of the tubes. Observe the patency of the wound drainage and record the color, quantity, and nature of the drainage material.

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
# 5. Nursing of Plaster and Orthotic Devices
When patients are using plaster and orthotic devices, listen to the patient's complaints. Inform the doctor of any painful bony prominences so that they can be relieved promptly. Observe the tightness of the plaster and orthotic devices, massage the edges of the plaster, and listen to the patient's complaints. Sniff the plaster to check for any foul odors. Observe the method of bleeding within the plaster. Each time the patient's plaster is checked, if bloodstains are observed on the surface of the plaster, circle the edges of the bloodstains. If the bloodstains continue to expand, it is a sign of ongoing bleeding, and the doctor should be reported immediately. If there is excessive bleeding, blood may flow out from the edges of the plaster. Promptly report to the doctor for treatment.

# 6. Observation of Deep Venous Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism Post Orthopedic Surgery
Prevention of venous thromboembolism = deep vein thrombosis + pulmonary embolism. When orthopedic surgery patients (especially those at high risk for venous thromboembolism) present with the following symptoms during the perioperative period due to unknown causes, they should be vigilant about the occurrence of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, and the doctor should be notified for examination. These include asymmetric swelling of the lower limb; dyspnea, increased respiratory rate but the patient can lie flat; chest pain, hemoptysis; hypotension/shock or cardiac arrest; palpitations, tachycardia; syncope. These symptoms are not consistent with the underlying cardiovascular diseases. These symptoms occur after prolonged immobilization and when the patient starts to walk.
The risk of deep vein thrombosis occurring after trauma and surgical procedures can vary with the type of surgery, such as spinal cord injury, with an incidence of 75% to 80%, gradually decreasing to 25% to 20%. Spinal cord injury, hip arthroplasty, amputation surgery, knee fracture surgery/arthroplasty, lower limb fracture, open prostatectomy, general abdominal surgery, surgical procedures, renal transplantation, non-cardiac thoracic surgery, neurosurgical surgery, open meniscus resection can all occur.

# 6. Observation and Prevention of Deep Venous Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism Post Orthopedic Surgery
Observation and prevention of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism post orthopedic surgery. The mechanical preventive method is the use of elastic stockings, which are safe, simple, and non-invasive. They can be used in conjunction with other preventive methods or alone in patients who are contraindicated for anticoagulant drug therapy. Elastic stockings provide graduated pressure from the ankle to the knee, which can significantly reduce post-valvular stasis of blood. Other mechanical preventive methods include intermittent pneumatic compression devices, also known as foot pumps or calf pumps.
Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism can also be prevented by drug therapy. Anticoagulant drug prevention can reduce the incidence of pulmonary embolism after surgical procedures by at least 60%, with only a 5% increase in the risk of bleeding.

# 7. Dietary Guidance
(1) Patients who have undergone general anesthesia or epidural anesthesia can start with liquid diet 6 hours postoperatively, once they are conscious and do not have any obvious symptoms of nausea or vomiting. Gradually transition to semi-liquid or regular food. Brachial plexus anesthesia patients can start eating 4 hours postoperatively.
(2) Patients should consume foods high in zinc and calcium to promote wound healing and bone callus growth; such as high-protein, high-sugar, collagen-rich, trace elements (copper, zinc, calcium, iron) and vitamin-rich foods, such as lean meat, pork skin, liver, egg yolks, soy products, carrots, fresh vegetables and fruits, to supplement sufficient nutrition and promote the recovery of the body.

# 8. Prevention and Management of Postoperative Complications
(1) Bleeding at surgical incisions in orthopedic surgeries is common to varying degrees. In case of severe bleeding, do not panic. Immediately apply pressure to the bleeding site to achieve hemostasis, and inform the doctor for further treatment.
(2) Pain. After the anesthetic effect wears off, patients may experience pain. It is usually most intense within the first 24 hours postoperatively and gradually subsides after 2-3 days. Appropriate analgesics or painkillers can be administered as needed. If the pain persists or worsens after initially improving, be vigilant for incisional infection. Postoperative patients generally can use intravenous analgesia pumps, nerve block analgesia pumps, or intramuscular analgesics.
(3) Urinary retention. If patients are unable to urinate within 6-8 hours postoperatively, it is mainly due to the suppression of the micturition reflex after general anesthesia or subarachnoid anesthesia. At the same time, the pain from the incision can cause reflex spasm of the bladder and the posterior urethral sphincter, as well as the patient's unfamiliarity with urinating in bed. Help patients to induce urination by using methods such as listening to running water sounds, applying heat, and gently massaging the bladder area. If the patient is unable to urinate, urinary catheterization can be performed by medical staff.
(4) Abdominal distension. Abdominal distension is generally due to the suppression of gastrointestinal function and excessive gas in the intestinal lumen. As the stress response of surgery subsides and gastrointestinal peristalsis recovers, symptoms can resolve spontaneously after anal排气. If there is no anal排气 for several days postoperatively and severe abdominal distension is present, along with absent bowel sounds, it may be due to intra-abdominal inflammation or intestinal paralysis. If abdominal distension is accompanied by intermittent sharp pain and hyperactive bowel sounds, be vigilant for the occurrence of mechanical bowel obstruction.
(5) Three major complications in bedridden patients: ① Prevention of pressure ulcers. Strengthen morning and evening care, maintain skin cleanliness and dryness, ensure that underwear and bedding are clean and wrinkle-free. For patients who are bedridden for a long time, turn and change their positions regularly, and perform effective massage. If necessary, use alcohol to massage bony prominences and pressure areas to prevent pressure ulcers. ② Prevention of pulmonary infection. Encourage patients to cough and expectorate effectively, and nurses can assist in protecting the wound to reduce pain caused by coughing. Advise patients to quit smoking, pay attention to keeping warm, and avoid catching a cold to prevent deep respiratory infections. ③ Prevention of urinary tract infection. Keep the perineum clean and hygienic, encourage drinking plenty of water to flush the urinary tract, frequently change positions to prevent the formation of urinary tract stones, and assist patients in training bladder reflex micturition to prevent urethral infection.

# 9. Psychological Nursing
Orthopedic surgery is a stage where patients have a strong psychological response. As the primary disease is resolved and patients successfully go through anesthesia and surgery, they have a certain degree of relief. However, the pain from the postoperative wound, discomfort caused by limb immobilization, and concerns about postoperative complications can lead to anxiety in patients. At this time, medical staff should patiently explain and introduce relevant postoperative knowledge to patients and guide them in early functional exercise.

# (III) Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
Postoperative rehabilitation is the ultimate goal of orthopedic treatment, with functional rehabilitation being the key to surgical success. Early rehabilitation exercise is crucial for the prevention and treatment of joint mobility disorders. For patients undergoing lower limb surgery, guide patients in ankle flexion and extension exercises, leg lifting, and quadriceps muscle function exercises. For lower limb surgery, such as knee replacement surgery, guide patients in passive knee exercises, active knee exercises, knee extension, and sitting knee flexion and extension. Instruct patients on how to use walking aids to walk.

# (IV) Discharge Instructions
After treatment and care, if the patient's condition improves or is cured, and they can be discharged according to medical advice, nursing staff provide a series of discharge instructions. The purpose of the discharge instructions is: ① Guide patients in the process of discharge, inform patients of the procedure for discharge; ② Guide patients to continue functional exercise after discharge; ③ Guide patients in post-discharge living care; ④ Guide patients on the method and precautions for taking medication after discharge; ⑤ Provide psychological care for discharged patients; ⑥ The nurse manager has a conversation with the patient to seek opinions. ⑦ Guide patients to follow medical advice, receive treatment on time, and have regular follow-up visits.
