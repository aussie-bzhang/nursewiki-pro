# Hepatitis B

According to statistics, more than 280 million asymptomatic carriers of hepatitis B virus (HBsAg carriers) are worldwide, with China accounting for about 130 million.
The incubation period of HBV infection is relatively long (about 45-160 days, on average 65-90 days).
It is mainly transmitted through blood, mother-to-child, and sexual contact.

# Significance of Various Indicators
Surface antigen (HBsAg):
     It itself is not infectious, but its presence often indicates the presence of hepatitis B virus, the invasion information of hepatitis B virus, and the marker of hepatitis B virus infection. Those with normal liver function but only HBsAg positive are called hepatitis B virus carriers. The higher the titer of HBsAg, the higher the infectivity, the greater the infectivity.
Surface antibody (HBsAb):
     Immune reactive antibody (protective antibody). It appears 6-23 weeks after the initial infection with hepatitis B and can last for several years. The higher the antibody titer, the stronger the protective effect, and the longer the duration (3-5 years). After re-infection, the titer increases significantly by 90% within 2 weeks, and the positive rate of HBsAb in China can reach about 40%.
e antigen (HBeAg): The virus is actively replicating in the body, and the infectivity is strong.
e antibody (HBeAb): The virus replication decreases or stops, indicating that the infectivity of the patient has significantly or relatively decreased.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
# Significance of Various Indicators

Core antibody (HBcAb)
  High titer indicates that the virus is replicating, and there is infectivity.
  Low titer indicates past viral infection.
  Core antibody can persist in the body for a long time, lasting for several years to several decades.
HBcAb-IgG (+): Past infection with hepatitis B virus.
HBcAb-IgM (+): Acute or recent infection with hepatitis B virus, and virus replication.
   If HBcAb-IgM has a high titer, it indicates a tendency towards chronic hepatitis B. If HBcAb-IgM has a high titer in patients with chronic active hepatitis B, it indicates that the hepatitis B virus is actively replicating in the body, which is one of the indicators of high infectivity.

# Analysis of Test Results
First item positive, the other four items negative. This result of the hepatitis B five items test indicates that it is in the late latent period of acute hepatitis B virus infection;
Fifth item positive, the other four items negative. It indicates that the carrier of hepatitis B virus is in a hidden carrier state or in the window period of infection, and also indicates that the person has been infected with hepatitis B virus;
First and third items positive, the other three items negative. This result of the hepatitis B five items test indicates that it is in the early stage of acute hepatitis B;
First and fifth items positive, the other three items negative. This result of the hepatitis B five items test indicates that the person has been infected with hepatitis B virus and is in the recovery period;
First, third, and fifth items positive, the other two items negative. Commonly known as "big three positive", this result of the hepatitis B five items test indicates acute and chronic hepatitis B;

# AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome)
            It is a highly fatal and severe infectious disease caused by a virus called "Human Immunodeficiency Virus" (HIV), which invades the human immune system, gradually reduces and destroys the immune function, leading to a series of incurable opportunistic infections and tumors, and ultimately resulting in the death of the patient.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# Why is it called Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome?

     For different individuals, the disease often presents with different clinical symptoms and signs. The immune deficiency caused by HIV is different from that caused by other diseases or drug side effects, as it is transmitted between humans through different routes, hence the name Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# What is the Window Period of AIDS?

       HIV is a retrovirus with a strong ability to mutate.
       The time from infection with HIV to the body producing antibodies is called the window period. The general window period is 3 months, with a few individuals extending up to 6 months.

The production of antibodies generally requires 2-12 weeks, with an average of about 45 days.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# What is the Difference Between AIDS Patients and HIV Infected Individuals?

An HIV infected individual is someone who has no clinical symptoms or signs, but the virus can be detected in their body.

An AIDS patient is someone whose immune system is severely damaged by HIV, leading to a variety of clinical syndromes such as opportunistic infections and tumors due to weakened immune function.

    The average incubation period from HIV infected individual to AIDS patient is 8-10 years.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# How Resistant is HIV to External Conditions?

HIV is very sensitive to heat and general chemical disinfectants and is not resistant to acid or alkali.
HIV loses its activity on the surface of objects within 3 hours at 30ºC, and can be quickly killed at temperatures above 60ºC.
HIV can be inactivated by 15W qualified ultraviolet light at a distance of 10cm for 8 minutes.
Common disinfectants such as 10% sodium hypochlorite, 2% glutaraldehyde, 4% formaldehyde, 2% chlorineamine, and 6% hydrogen peroxide can quickly kill it.
It is very sensitive to dry sunlight and dies quickly in a dry environment. The virus cannot be detected after 3 days.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
# Can Mosquito Bites Transmit AIDS?

The amount of blood left on the mosquito's mouth is only 0.00004ml, and it would take 2800 consecutive bites to carry enough HIV to cause infection. Moreover, mosquitoes do not bite again for at least 4 days after a single blood meal.

HIV can survive in the mosquito's stomach for 3-4 days, but it does not replicate within the body. HIV has not been found in the surrounding blood or saliva, so it does not transmit the HIV from the stomach to the human body through mosquito bites.

# Syphilis
     It is a chronic systemic sexually transmitted disease caused by the infection of Treponema pallidum (TP). Syphilis, tuberculosis, and leprosy are listed as the world's three major chronic infectious diseases. It is one of the 8 key sexually transmitted diseases to be prevented and controlled by the Ministry of Health of China.

# COURSE
Primary Syphilis: within 3 months after infection with TP;
    TP enters the human body, about 2-4 weeks later, causing an inflammatory reaction at the entry site, such as the penis, vulva, vaginal orifice, etc., known as chancre;
Secondary Syphilis: within 3 months to 2 years after infection with TP;
    6-8 weeks after the appearance of chancre, TP invades various tissues and organs throughout the body, with skin rash being the most common;
Late Syphilis (Tertiary Syphilis): more than 2 years after infection with TP;
    Skin, bone, internal organs, nerves, sensory organs, and gummas occur about 10 years later;

# Resistance of Treponema pallidum to External Conditions
Treponema pallidum has a very weak resistance to external conditions, is sensitive to chemicals, and is not easy to survive outside the body.
Boiling, drying, soap water, and general disinfectants can easily kill it. Sunlight and a dry environment can quickly kill it.
It can only survive for about 1-2 hours outside the body. In an oxygen-deficient environment, it can survive for several days, and can also survive for several hours on damp clothing. It can survive for about 24 hours in a blood bank.
Treponema pallidum is not resistant to high temperatures, and can be killed in 2-3 minutes at 40℃-60℃, and immediately at 100℃.

# Protection Against Blood-Borne Infections in the Operating Room

     Occupational exposure of medical personnel to blood-borne infectious diseases: Medical personnel may be exposed to blood or body fluids from blood-borne infectious disease carriers or infected individuals during the course of diagnosis, treatment, and nursing, or may be pierced by needles or other sharp instruments contaminated with blood-borne infectious diseases, which may lead to viral infection.

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# Protection Against Blood-Borne Infections in the Operating Room
Increase the awareness of medical personnel, follow the "Standard Precautions" principle.
Increase awareness to prevent occupational exposure - the most proactive, effective, and easy-to-implement measure.
Actively accept knowledge of blood-borne infectious diseases, understand the transmission methods of various viruses, and know the importance of taking necessary protective measures;
Understand the condition of surgical patients, be vigilant about patients diagnosed with infection factors, and strengthen protection;
Treat all emergency or undiagnosed cases as positive.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Protection against Blood-Borne Infections in the Operating Room
Carry out isolation and disinfection protection work
Avoid direct contact with body fluids, and use appropriate protective equipment according to the amount and location of blood or body fluids that may be contacted.

If the hand skin is broken and surgery is required, double gloves must be worn;
If involved in cardiovascular, large artery, abdominal surgery, and other operations where a large amount of blood and body fluids may be splashed, waterproof isolation gowns and protective eyewear should be worn;
If contaminated blood accidentally splashes into the eyes or onto the skin, it should be rinsed immediately with running water.

# Establishing a "Middle Area" for Transferring Sharps

The "middle area" refers to a pre-designated area for placing sharp instruments, where both surgeons and scrub nurses can easily retrieve them, thereby reducing the need for direct hand transfer of sharp instruments.

# Post-exposure Preventive Measures for Hepatitis B Virus Occupational Exposure

Post-exposure preventive measures are closely related to the status of vaccination:
1. For those who have not been vaccinated, measures should be taken to inject hepatitis B immune globulin and receive hepatitis B vaccine.
2. For those who have been vaccinated before and are known to have antibodies, no treatment is required.
3. For those who have been vaccinated before but have not produced antibodies, measures should be taken to inject hepatitis B immune globulin and receive hepatitis B vaccine.
4. For those with unknown antibody response, antigen-antibody detection should be performed; if the results are insufficient, measures should be taken to inject hepatitis B immune globulin and receive hepatitis B vaccine.

# Post-exposure Follow-up and Consultation for Hepatitis C Virus

1. Hepatitis C antibody and alanine aminotransferase baseline and follow-up detection should be performed 4 to 6 months after exposure.

2. If early diagnosis of hepatitis C virus infection is desired, hepatitis C virus RNA detection should be performed 4 to 6 weeks after exposure.

# Post-exposure Preventive Measures for HIV Virus Occupational Exposure

Post-exposure preventive measures should be taken as soon as possible, and prophylactic medication should be implemented within 4 hours after HIV virus occupational exposure, at the latest not exceeding 24 hours. However, even if it exceeds 24 hours, prophylactic medication should still be implemented. Pregnancy testing should be performed for all childbearing women who are not known to be pregnant. Childbearing women should avoid or terminate pregnancy during the period of prophylactic medication.

Prophylactic medication should:
1. If there is an indication for medication, post-exposure prophylaxis should be initiated as soon as possible after exposure.
2. Consider re-evaluating the exposed person within 72 hours after exposure, especially if new exposure information or source patient information is obtained.
3. Provide 4 weeks of post-exposure prophylactic medication, provided that the exposed person can tolerate it.
4. If it is confirmed that the source patient is not infected with blood-borne pathogens, post-exposure prophylactic medication should be discontinued immediately.

<!-- Slide number: 39 -->
# Post-exposure Follow-up and Consultation for HIV Virus Occupational Exposure
1. HIV virus tracing detection should be conducted within six months after exposure, including HIV antibody detection at 4 weeks, 8 weeks, 12 weeks, and 6 months after exposure, monitoring and managing the toxicity of the medication taken, observing and recording early symptoms of HIV infection, etc.
2. If recurrent acute symptoms accompany the disease, HIV antibody detection should be conducted.
3. The exposed person should take preventive measures to prevent re-infection during the follow-up period.
4. The level of post-exposure prophylaxis should be assessed within 72 hours after exposure, and at least 2 weeks of medication toxicity monitoring should be conducted.

# Post-exposure Preventive Measures for Syphilis

Consult a specialist for consultation and provide antibiotic prophylactic treatment for medical personnel exposed to the occupation, recommended long-acting penicillin 2.4 million units, once a week, 1.2 million units per side, injected into each buttock per time, for two consecutive weeks. For those allergic to penicillin, alternatives such as erythromycin can be selected.

# How to Report After Occupational Exposure

Medical staff should report occupational exposure (or occupational exposure) as soon as possible after the event:
1. Immediately report to the department head and nurse manager, and report to the Infection Control Department as soon as possible, and have blood tests conducted.
2. Fill out the "Medical Staff Occupational Exposure (or Occupational Exposure) Registration Form," have the department head sign it, and submit it to the Infection Control Department within 24 hours (with HIV exposure, the report must be submitted within 2 hours).
3. Upon receiving the report of "HIV Contact," the Infection Control Department will immediately report to the AIDS Department of the District Center for Disease Control, and also report to the hospital leadership.
