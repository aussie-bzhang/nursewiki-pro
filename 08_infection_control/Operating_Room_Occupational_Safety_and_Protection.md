# II. Related Definitions
What is occupational exposure?
           Operating room nurses are exposed to blood, body fluids, or other infectious agents or carriers in their daily work, such as during diagnosis, treatment, nursing, and medical waste disposal. If they are contaminated with blood or body fluids through broken skin or mucous membranes, or if they are pierced by contaminated sharp instruments, they face the risk of occupational hazards such as physical, chemical, and biological factors, as well as psychological and physiological factors. They are at risk of contracting a disease at any time. This risk is called occupational exposure.

# III. Risk Factors - Physical Injuries
Sharp instrument injuries are the main risk factor for occupational injuries among operating room nurses, according to statistics, 98% of nurses in China have experienced sharp instrument injuries.
Radiation, radiation, electromagnetic waves, noise, weight-bearing, etc. The ionizing radiation produced by the radiation equipment used in the operating room has attracted attention for its damage to medical staff, and its safety protection must be strictly observed. Operating room nurses stand for a long time, with a relatively fixed posture, and are highly stressed, which can cause lumbar muscle strain, poor local blood circulation, varicose veins of the lower limbs, cervical spondylosis, and other diseases.

# 三、Risk Factors—Chemical Injuries
Chemical Disinfectants    These preparations are commonly used for disinfection of the operating room environment, disinfection and sterilization of surgical items, immersion of pathological tissue specimens, etc. Examples include formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, ethylene oxide, iodine, ethanol, toluene, and chlorine-containing preparations, which are volatile chemical disinfectants. These chemical disinfectants can cause certain damage to the skin, respiratory system, and digestive system of humans.

Anesthetic Waste    The main source of contamination in anesthesia is the overflow of volatile anesthetics, exhaled gases from patients after anesthesia, and gases emitted by anesthesia machines. The toxicity of anesthetic drugs mainly involves the destruction of the liver, kidneys, and immune function. Pregnant staff who have long-term contact with gas anesthetics are prone to abortion and fetal malformation.

Other Harmful Gases    For example, the smoke from surgical electrosurgical units can cause multiple injuries to healthcare workers, including burns, tears, nausea and vomiting, and respiratory system disorders; bone cement used in joint replacement can also cause severe chest tightness, asthma, and allergic symptoms; the concentration of CO2 and various gases released by iodophor gauze in laparoscopic surgery can be too high in the air, causing harm to those in contact.

# 三、Risk Factors—Infectious Injuries
The operating room is a place where surgical patients are highly concentrated and pathogenic microorganisms are relatively concentrated. Medical staff often come into direct and frequent contact with the body fluids, blood, and secretions of patients, making the risk of infectious diseases the highest. Operating room nurses are a high-risk group for hospital-acquired infectious diseases due to occupational factors, with bloodborne pathogens posing the greatest risk to nurses, mainly transmitted through exposure of the skin or mucous membranes.

# 三、Risk Factors—Social and Psychological Factors
Psychological factors are one of the important factors affecting the physical and mental health of operating room nurses. High-intensity work pressure not only causes a sense of fatigue among nurses but also is prone to adverse psychological states such as neurasthenia, neurosis, and mood swings. Long-term night shifts disrupt the biological clock, irregular eating and resting, which can lead to gastrointestinal diseases; studying for academic qualifications during leisure time reduces rest time, leading to physical exhaustion and a series of clinical manifestations such as endocrine function disorders and weakened immune function. Constant worry about making mistakes in patient identification, misplacing surgical sites, leaving surgical instruments or dressings inside patients, etc., can increase psychological stress. The high risk, high intensity, irregularity, and inability to care for family life in operating room work can lead to increased mental burden.

International practice commonly uses sound pressure level to express the size of sound volume, with the unit of sound pressure level being dB (decibels).
Generally, sounds at around 30 decibels will not affect normal living and rest. However, when the level reaches 50 decibels or above, people have a strong sensation and it is difficult to fall asleep.
     Generally, sounds reaching 80 decibels or above are considered noise.
For those engaged in brainwork and highly skilled operations, the ideal sound pressure level in the room is 40 dB, with a maximum not exceeding 60 dB.
The average noise level in the operating room is 60-65 dB, but it often approaches 90 dB, which is the highest level allowed within 8 hours.

# 1. Discoverer of X-rays

# 小故事
In 1861, the British scientist William Crookes discovered that a cathode ray tube emits light when electricity is passed through it. He photographed it, but when the film was developed, he found nothing but a blurred image on the entire plate. He thought the plate was old, so he used a new plate and photographed it three times in a row, but the result was still the same. Crookes' laboratory was very primitive, and he thought it was a problem with the plate, so he returned it to the manufacturer. He had also found that film stored in a dark box in the drawer had mysteriously become exposed and wasted. He found the film manufacturer and criticized their poor product, almost missing a great discovery. It was not until Roentgen discovered X-rays that Crookes realized his mistake.

Five years before Roentgen discovered X-rays, the American scientist Goldsborough accidentally developed an X-ray film in his laboratory, but he attributed it to the developer or processing technique and discarded this "accident" in the trash.

# (1) Three Principles of Radiation Protection
Radiation practice justification: Any radioactive work carried out when the benefits are clearly greater than the total cost incurred is justified and worth pursuing.
Radiation protection optimization: The radiation dose to staff and patients should be kept as low as possible without affecting the therapeutic effect. This can be achieved by shortening exposure time, increasing distance, and using radiation shielding.
Dose limitation: Staff exposed to radiation must undergo dose monitoring. Dose meters can accurately display the radiation dose received by staff, and the dose meter records should be checked monthly. Special attention should be paid to the monthly absolute safe radiation dose. The International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) stipulates that the annual dose equivalent limit for uniform whole-body exposure of staff is 50 mSv.
Three sensitive organs: lens, thyroid, and gonads. The lens is most susceptible to radiation damage.

# (2) Basic Protective Measures
The operating room should have sufficient space, with an area of not less than 30m2, to facilitate the placement and transportation of equipment, without disrupting sterile operation. The operating table should be X-ray transparent.
The main protection of the operating room walls should reach a thickness of 2mm lead content, and the auxiliary protection should reach a thickness of 1mm lead content, to prevent the rays from escaping. The radiation dose outside the operating room should be less than 3μGy, achieving the purpose of safety.
Inside the operating room, X-rays can ionize the air inside, producing harmful ozone and nitrogen oxides, and also disrupt the balance of positive and negative ions in the air. Therefore, in a clean operating room, the working status of the equipment and the operation of the clean system and ventilation system should be checked before surgery to ensure their normal operation and maintain good ventilation.
The radiation dose of the X-ray machine should be kept at a reasonable minimum level, using the smallest cost to achieve the greatest benefit, so that all necessary exposures are kept at the lowest possible standard to reduce the harm of radiation. Regularly invite professional maintenance personnel to inspect the machine, timely handle and solve problems, and maintain the good performance of the machine and protective equipment.

# (3) Use of Personal Protective Equipment
Wear lead aprons, lead-lined gloves, protective goggles, lead collars, and lead screens.

# (4) Personal Protective Measures
Distance protection: The most effective way to reduce radiation dose is to increase distance. If the distance is doubled, the radiation dose can be reduced to one-fourth of the original. Therefore, all personnel should try to stay as far away as possible from the X-ray tube and scattered rays, without violating the principle of sterile operation during surgery.
Time protection: Time protection means shortening the exposure time of X-rays as much as possible. The shorter the exposure time, the smaller the radiation dose received by the operator and the examined person, and the two are proportional. It is required that the operator must be skilled in technique to avoid repeated exposure caused by human error.
Shielding protection: Placing a barrier material that can effectively absorb radiation between the radiation source and the staff can weaken or eliminate the harm of radiation to the human body. Common shielding tools include lead screens, lead curtains, lead aprons, lead gloves, lead goggles, and lead collars.
Dose limitation: Staff wear a badge-type personal dose meter. Placed on the lead apron behind the chest, a personal file is established, and the dose records are checked monthly. However, it should be noted that dose limitation is only a protective measure relative to radiation damage and does not mean there is an absolutely safe radiation dose.

# 3. Hazards of Lasers

The hazards caused by lasers are divided into five levels: Level 1, Level 2, Level 3A, Level 3B, and Level 4. With the increase in levels, the danger also increases, with Level 4 being the most dangerous. Most medical lasers belong to Level 3B and Level 4.
The hazards of lasers are divided into two types: beam hazards and non-beam hazards.
Beam hazards are direct and unexpected laser beam exposure, which may cause eye and skin injuries, fires, or explosions.
Non-beam hazards are the effects of smoke, chemicals emitted during the laser generation process, and electrical accidents on the human body.

# 46
# 4. Hazards of Lasers to the Human Body—Eyes
Since lasers are light, and the human body's most sensitive organ to light is the eyes, the hazards of lasers to the eyes are the greatest. When the laser focuses on the photoreceptor cells, it can cause overheating and protein coagulation and denaturation, resulting in irreversible damage. Once damaged, it can cause permanent blindness in the eyes.
The blink reflex time of the human eye is usually 150-250ms, while the laser pulse can be as short as less than 0.01ms. In addition, the energy is released in a very short time and concentrated in a very small area. Therefore, even low-dose laser exposure can cause serious damage to the eye tissue. Severe cases may result in retinal burns, holes, and bleeding. Therefore, it is strictly forbidden to operate such laser devices without wearing laser protective glasses.

# 47
# 4. Hazards of Lasers to the Human Body—Skin
The skin exposed to the laser will show thermal erythema, blisters, coagulation, thermal carbonization, boiling, combustion, and thermal vaporization with the increase of dose.
Therefore, the mechanism of laser damage to the skin is mainly due to the thermal effect of the laser, and the ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 270-290nm is the most harmful. The degree of harm is relatively reduced for wavelengths greater than 290nm or less than 270nm.

# 3. Causes of Occupational Exposure in Operating Room Nurses
Lack of protective awareness: Lack of knowledge and insufficient attention to oneself.
Opportunities to contact sharp instruments are numerous: such as: drug ampoules, surgical knives, surgical needles, syringes, needles, sharp surgical instruments: scissors, hooks, etc.
Unsafe operations: Inexperienced, busy, incorrect transmission of sharp instruments. Hand-breaking ampoules.
Patient non-cooperation: Patients are unconscious, restless, children do not cooperate, etc.
Not handling sharp instruments in a timely and correct manner: Busy work, not handling sharp instruments after use. Mixing sharp instruments with other items after use.

# 6. Protection Measures for Sharp Instrument Injuries
Prohibition of using both hands to re-screw the needle cap after using a needle. It should be promptly placed in a sharp instrument box.
When passing a knife or suture needle, slow down the speed, place the surgical knife in a bowl or plate for transfer, and avoid direct hand-to-hand contact.
Do not directly use hands to remove blades, bend, or break needles.
Use a needle holder to pick up suture needles. Do not use a handkerchief directly to wipe bloodstains on the surgical blade. Remove all unnecessary sharp instruments promptly after use.
Place electrical scalpels, drills, and other sharp instruments in a fixed protective position or lock them to prevent accidental activation of the switch and injury.
Items and materials in the operating room are limited to use by only one person. After the operation, place the blades and suture needles in a sharp instrument box and dispose of them as medical waste by incineration.
When cleaning and disinfecting instruments, place sharp instruments separately. Use protective sleeves on the sharp ends of instruments during packaging to avoid injury.

# What is Contactless Transfer Technology?
Establish a "middle area" for the transfer of sharp instruments, which is a pre-designated area for placing sharp instruments. Surgeons and scrub nurses can easily pick up sharp instruments from this area, which can reduce the direct hand-to-hand transfer of sharp instruments. The use of the middle area for sharp instruments is also known as contactless transfer technology.
The WHO stipulates that sharp instruments must be transferred using specific colored containers. When transferring surgical knives and scissors, do not pass them directly by hand, but use a bent plate.

# 7. Emergency Management Procedures for Sharp Instrument Injuries
Wound pre-treatment: Immediately and rapidly remove gloves according to routine, immediately use the non-affected hand to squeeze the wound from the proximal to the distal end, preferably to expel blood. Wash the wound with running water for 15 minutes. If there is mucosal injury, rinse with running water and physiological saline. Disinfect the wound with 0.5% iodophor, 2% iodine tincture, or 75% alcohol.
Reporting time: Establish a reporting management system for sharp instrument injuries. Once a nurse is injured, they should immediately report to the relevant department of the hospital and assess the situation.
The infection control department records the occurrence of medical staff's trauma events, including: time, location, process, specific location, and the nature of the injury, and performs relevant examinations and treatments.

# 8. Occupational Exposure Protection System
Preventive medication:

- Hepatitis C occupational exposure medication,
- HIV/AIDS occupational exposure medication;
Serological follow-up:
- Complete relevant serological tests for both the injured person and the patient they have contacted within 24-48 hours. Follow-up for one year, and corresponding treatment is given based on the situation.

# Conclusion
In daily work, medical staff should recognize occupational hazards, make full use of various protective equipment and barriers, reduce various hazardous behaviors, and strengthen the environmental protection and self-protection awareness of medical staff.
Regular inspections and supervision should be carried out, and problems should be addressed in a timely manner. Strengthen the understanding of the hazards of medical waste, manage each link well, and effectively implement occupational protection. Take positive and effective measures to protect medical staff and patients to the maximum extent, reduce the hazards of occupational injuries to the lowest level, and better serve patients.
