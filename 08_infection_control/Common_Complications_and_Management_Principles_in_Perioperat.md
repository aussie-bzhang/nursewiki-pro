# Diagnostic Criteria for Postoperative Pneumonia
Currently, there is no unified diagnostic standard for postoperative pneumonia. The following features may indicate pneumonia in patients:
- Chest X-ray showing new or progressive infiltrates
- Diagnosis based on clinical characteristics (such as fever, purulent sputum, leukocytosis, tissue hypoxia)
The following are reference results from post-mortem examinations:
     – Radiographic opacity combined with 2-3 clinical features (body temperature >38°C, leukocytosis/reduction, purulent secretion), the diagnosis of pneumonia can reach 69% sensitivity and 75% specificity
Gaurav Sachdev , et al . Surg Clin N Am. 2012; 321–344

### Notes:
Note: The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classifies patients according to their physical condition and surgical risk before anesthesia, dividing them into 5 categories. The ASA classification criteria are as follows:
    1st level: Physically healthy, well-developed, and with normal organ function;
    2nd level: Other than surgical diseases, has mild comorbidities, with normal functional compensation;
    3rd level: Severe comorbidities, limited physical activity, but can still cope with daily activities;
    4th level: Severe comorbidities, unable to perform daily activities, frequently facing life-threatening situations;
    5th level: Patients whose lives are difficult to maintain for 24 hours, regardless of surgery or not;
    6th level: Confirmed brain death, whose organs are intended for organ transplantation surgery.

# Pathogenesis of Postoperative Pulmonary Infection (1)

Inhalation of gas containing pathogens: This includes cross-infection (inhaling contaminated air formed by other patients' coughing) and iatrogenic infection (such as inadequate disinfection of nebulizers or oxygen humidifiers). This is the exogenous mechanism of POP.

Decreased cough reflex, swallowing reflex, or other natural defense functions of the respiratory tract, leading to an increased opportunity for aspiration of oropharyngeal secretions or gastroesophageal reflux, and poor drainage of respiratory secretions.

Due to anesthesia, analgesic drugs, or wound pain after surgery, ventilation of low lung tissue is suppressed, leading to atelectasis.

Collaborative Group for the Guideline on the Application of Antimicrobial Agents for the Prevention and Treatment of Surgical Infections. Chinese Journal of Surgery. 2005; 43(17):1158-1160.
Zhu Lei. Chinese Journal of Practical Surgery. 2009;29(12):1064-1066.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
# Pathogenesis of Postoperative Pulmonary Infection (2)

Physiological disturbances caused by thoracotomy: Thoracotomy disrupts the dynamic balance of the chest wall bellows movement and exposes the surgical side lung to open pneumothorax. A series of physical and chemical stimuli during surgery can also interfere with respiration and circulation through neural reflexes. Thoracotomy causes atelectasis of the surgical side lung, leading to a decrease in minute ventilation, a decrease in the ratio of alveolar ventilation to pulmonary blood flow, and an increase in shunting from the pulmonary artery to the vein.

Bacteremia: Relatively rare, with pathogens mainly including Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Enterobacteriaceae, etc.

Collaborative Group for the Guideline on the Application of Antimicrobial Agents for the Prevention and Treatment of Surgical Infections. Chinese Journal of Surgery. 2005; 43(17):1158-1160.
Zhu Lei. Chinese Journal of Practical Surgery. 2009;29(12):1064-1066.

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
# For the above pathogenesis, the main treatment approach is to improve drainage and strengthen respiratory management (1)

Tracheal intubation and aspiration, or non-invasive positive pressure ventilation can be provided, with pressure support ventilation being the first choice. High pressure (generally 20-25 cmH2O, 1 cmH2O = 0.098 kPa) is used for ventilation. After these measures are taken, most patients can quickly improve.

However, for patients with asphyxia, rapid orotracheal intubation should be performed. If poor secretion drainage persists or recurs, tracheotomy may be necessary.

**Improving Drainage**
Zhu Lei. Chinese Journal of Practical Surgery. 2009;29(12):1064-1066.

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
# For the above pathogenesis, the main treatment approach is to improve drainage and strengthen respiratory management (2)

Strengthen deep breathing exercises to keep alveoli fully open. The tidal volume of deep breathing should reach 70%-80% of vital capacity, generally 4-6 times a day, with each breath lasting 10-20 times.

Improve the efficiency of coughing.

Preventive辅助通气: For high-risk patients, the time of extubation can be delayed, continuing mechanical ventilation for 24-72 hours; or non-invasive positive pressure ventilation can be provided after extubation.

Therapeutic mechanical ventilation: Once respiratory failure occurs, it is often necessary to establish artificial airways for mechanical ventilation early. Generally, orotracheal intubation is the first choice. If it is estimated that the tube cannot be removed within one week, tracheotomy should be performed as soon as possible.

**Strengthening Respiratory Management**
Zhu Lei. Chinese Journal of Practical Surgery. 2009;29(12):1064-1066.

Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, anaerobic bacteria, legionella, and others can all cause postoperative pulmonary infections in patients, and mixed infections are also relatively common. In recent years, there has been an increasing trend in infections caused by non-fermenting bacteria such as Acinetobacter, which have or may have multiple drug resistance. The mortality rate of pneumonia caused by these infections has significantly increased, and early treatment should cover a wide range of antibiotics.

Before obtaining a microbiological diagnosis or when it is not possible to obtain a microbiological diagnosis, empirical treatment can only be based on clinical diagnosis. The initial empirical treatment should adhere to the principle of "comprehensive coverage."

# Summary
Postoperative pulmonary infection is one of the most common postoperative pulmonary complications, with a high incidence and mortality rate, posing severe harm. Effective management should be implemented in conjunction with the patient's high-risk factors to improve patient prognosis.

- For the pathogenesis of postoperative pulmonary infection, the main treatment approach is to improve drainage and strengthen respiratory management.
- For the common pathogenic bacteria associated with postoperative pulmonary infection, the initial empirical treatment should adhere to the policy of "comprehensive coverage."

Bifort covers the common pathogenic bacteria associated with postoperative pulmonary infection, effectively penetrating the lung tissue, acting rapidly, and demonstrating excellent efficacy. It is safe, convenient, and has good pharmacoeconomic characteristics, making it an ideal choice for the treatment of postoperative pulmonary infection.
