# Enteral Nutrition
A method of providing nutritional support by administering nutritional fluids through the gastrointestinal tract via oral intake or tube feeding, for patients with functional gastrointestinal tract but unable to intake sufficient oral food
Oral nutrition has been the only choice for several centuries
Tube-feeding enteral nutrition has only been adopted and promoted in the past few decades

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
# Evolution of Nutritional Support Concepts
In the 1970s, parenteral nutrition was the first choice for patients in need of nutritional support
In the 1980s, peripheral parenteral nutrition was the first choice for patients in need of nutritional support
In the 1990s, when the intestine is functional and can be safely used, it should be used
Currently, enteral nutrition support is the first choice, combined with parenteral nutrition when necessary
         Try to carry out enteral nutrition

# Advantages and Disadvantages of Enteral Nutrition
Physiological compatibility
　Protects the integrity and function of the gastrointestinal tract, promotes visceral blood supply
Effective

Safe and economical

# Goals of Enteral Nutrition Support
Maintain intestinal integrity (most important)
　Support intestinal lymphoid tissue, increase intestinal blood flow

　Prevent bacterial translocation, reduce endotoxin response

# Indications for Enteral Nutrition Routes
Patients unable to eat >7-14 days
If the gastrointestinal tract is functional, enteral feeding is the preferred option (compared to parenteral nutrition)
When the expected nutritional needs are < 30 days, nasogastric tube feeding is generally suitable
When the expected nutritional needs are > 30 days, direct enteral routes (through endoscopy, surgery, or radiology) are generally suitable
　The choice of route is determined by the individual disease status of the patient and the experience and choice of the operator

# Recommendations
Nasogastric tube is suitable for patients receiving enteral nutrition for less than 2-3 weeks
    Elevating the head to 30-45° during tube feeding can reduce the occurrence of aspiration pneumonia (C)
Patients undergoing abdominal surgery and requiring long-term enteral nutrition postoperatively
    It is recommended to place a jejunal stoma during surgery (A)
Enteral nutrition through an enteral feeding tube placed distal to the anastomosis of the proximal gastrointestinal tract (B)

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
# Recommended Opinions
For patients undergoing non-abdominal surgery who require enteral nutrition for more than 2-3 weeks, such as patients with severe head trauma:
Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) is the preferred route for tube feeding (C).
Skilled endoscopic manipulation can reduce the incidence of PEG complications (A).
Patients at high risk of aspiration pneumonia should choose jejunostomy tube placement (C).

# Preoperative Application of渣-Free Enteral Nutrition Formulation
Three days before surgery, the application of Nourish can achieve optimal intestinal cleanliness in 95% of patients
The Nourish group required fewer enemas and less enema fluid than the traditional fluid group (P<0.01)
The Nourish group had no adverse reactions during enema, while the traditional fluid group experienced palpitations and dizziness (P<0.05)

Zhao Zonggang, Zhan Wenhua, et al. Application of enteral nutrition formulation in preoperative intestinal preparation for colorectal cancer. Chinese Journal of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition. 2004;11(3):174-176

# Intraoperative Enteral Nutrition
The intestinal fasting state (IS) enhances cytokine response and promotes infection formation
The intestinal fasting state before and during surgery from 12 to 20 hours has not been given sufficient attention
The interruption of gastric nutrition during the perioperative period will lead to gastric atony
  It is very important to prevent the occurrence of intestinal fasting state before, during, and after surgery
  It is recommended to use it routinely in major surgeries such as liver, pancreas, burn, and liver transplantation
Bengmark S (2002), Aggressive peri- and intraoperative enteral nutrition. ASPEN), Chapter 33, pages 365–380

# Early Postoperative Enteral Nutrition
Postoperative gastrointestinal paralysis, nutritional formulation, and nutritional device research
It was not until the end of the last century that it was realized that postoperative gastrointestinal paralysis was mainly localized to the stomach and could be largely prevented
In the 1920s, Rehfuss's duodenal feeding tube was introduced

# Case Sharing 1
Male, 34 years old, admitted on October 25, 2006, for recurrent vomiting after eating for 15 days following radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer 28 days earlier
The patient underwent a gastric resection and esophagojejunal Roux-Y anastomosis for gastric body cancer (T3N1M0) at our hospital on September 27, 2006, and recovered well after surgery. He was discharged from the hospital on the 11th day after surgery, and he could eat semi-liquid food at the time of discharge. On 15 days before the onset, there was no obvious cause for the onset of vomiting after eating, with vomiting material being the food ingested, no retained food, and no bitter taste; vomiting occurred approximately 15 minutes after eating, and the symptoms worsened progressively. Both solid and liquid foods were vomited, and recently, drinking water also caused vomiting. Vomiting was accompanied by abdominal discomfort and abdominal pain, without palpitations, sweating, dizziness, or other discomforts; over the past month, the weight has dropped by about 5kg
He had been treated at a local hospital, where abdominal X-ray and gastrointestinal contrast studies were both normal. Anti-inflammatory and fluid replacement therapy did not alleviate the symptoms, and he was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. The outpatient diagnosis was intestinal obstruction following gastric cancer surgery. Since the onset, sleep and appetite are normal, and bowel movements are regular
