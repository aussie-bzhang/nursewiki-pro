# Purpose of Prophylactic Antimicrobial Use in the Perioperative Period

★ To prevent surgical site infections (SSI), which refer to infections occurring at the incision or in the deep organs or cavities of surgery
★ The concept of SSI is broader than incision infection but narrower than postoperative infection
★ SSI accounts for approximately 15% of all hospital infections
★ It accounts for approximately 35%-40% of hospital infections in surgical patients

 Classification of Surgical Incisions

          Category                       Criteria
Ⅰ Class (Clean) incision   Surgery does not enter the inflammatory area, does not enter the respiratory, digestive, and urinary生殖道, and is closed trauma surgery; surgery that meets the above conditions
Ⅱ Class (Clean-polluted)  Surgery enters the respiratory, digestive, or urinary reproductive tract but without obvious pollution

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->

Ⅲ Class (Polluted) incision  Surgery enters an acute inflammatory area but not a suppurative area; open trauma surgery; gastrointestinal, urinary, biliary tract contents and body fluids with a large amount of overflow pollution; obvious pollution during surgery (such as chest compression during open heart surgery)
Ⅳ Class (Infected)          Old trauma surgery with necrotic tissue; surgery with existing clinical infection or organ perforation

Incision classification is an important basis for determining whether antibiotic prophylaxis is necessary.

 What situations require prophylactic use of antibiotics?

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
    Not all surgeries require antibiotic prophylaxis. General clean incision surgeries, such as superficial surgeries of the head and neck, trunk, and limbs, without artificial implants, such as inguinal hernia repair, thyroid adenoma resection, and breast fibroadenoma resection, generally do not require the use of antibiotics

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
Indications for Prophylactic Antibiotic Use
★ Clean-contaminated incisions and some class III contaminated incisions requiring surgery (such as entering the gastrointestinal, respiratory, or female reproductive tracts)
★ Clean surgeries with long duration, significant trauma, and severe consequences in case of infection (such as craniotomy, cardiac and great vessel surgery, and portal hypertension surgery)
★ Surgeries involving the use of artificial materials or devices
★ Patients with high-risk factors for infection (diabetes, malnutrition, immunosuppression, elderly age)
Class IV incisions and severely contaminated class III incisions should be treated with antibacterial drugs for therapeutic purposes and are not considered for prophylaxis.

# Quality Control Indicators
The proportion of patients undergoing class I incision surgery who receive prophylactic antibiotic use should not exceed 30%.
Among them, patients undergoing inguinal hernia repair (including mesh repair), thyroid disease surgery, breast disease surgery, arthroscopic examination surgery, carotid endarterectomy, craniotomy for tumor resection, and interventional diagnostic procedures via vascular access should not be prophylactically administered antibiotics in principle.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->

How to Select Prophylactic Antibiotics?

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
★ Select relatively broad-spectrum, effective (bactericidal), safe, and inexpensive drugs
★ Cephalosporins are listed as the first choice
★ Cardiovascular, head and neck, thoracic abdominal wall, and extremity surgery - first-generation cephalosporins
★ Surgery entering the gastrointestinal, respiratory, or female reproductive tracts - commonly use second-generation cephalosporins, with a few using third-generation cephalosporins
★ Aminoglycosides have ototoxic and nephrotoxic effects, and attention should be paid when selecting
★ Generally, quinolone drugs are not used (antibacterial spectrum is not balanced, and bacterial resistance is high in China)

★ For patients with an allergy to penicillin and cephalosporins, clindamycin can be used for G+ cocci, and aztreonam for G- bacilli, with the combination of both often used
★ In cases with specific indications, vancomycin can be selected, such as when there is confirmed MRSA-related SSI outbreak, or when patients with rheumatic heart disease complicated with endocarditis require open heart surgery
★ For organ transplant patients, broader-spectrum antibiotics are required, such as β-lactamase inhibitors (cefoperazone/sulbactam, piperacillin/tazobactam), fourth-generation cephalosporins, and even carbapenems (ertapenem)

★Bacteria (endogenous, exogenous) contamination: easily cleared in the early stage
→
★Colonization: bacteria adhere to the surface of tissue cells but do not reproduce in large numbers, not easily cleared quickly
    Mechanism: G-rod bacterium flagella; G+ bacterium cell wall teichoic acid; bacterial surface glycoproteins and polysaccharide complexes; tissue cell surface polysaccharide filaments
→
★Infection: large-scale reproduction of bacteria causing inflammation

# Timing of prophylactic medication
★Prevent contamination before it occurs, be prepared
★Early administration is of no benefit, it is a shot in the dark
★Cephalosporins should be started 30 minutes before the start of surgery (Vancomycin, Clindamycin should be 2 hours), ensuring that the drug has reached an effective concentration in serum and tissues before contamination occurs (> MIC 90)
★Administer in the operating room rather than summoning in the ward
★Antibiotic bowel preparation for colorectal surgery should be given 1 day before the operation, not for 3 days

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
Application methods
★Should be administered intravenously, infused within 30 minutes
★Intramuscular injection, oral administration have individual differences in absorption, cannot guarantee blood and tissue drug concentration, not suitable for use
★Ensure sufficient antibiotic concentration throughout the operation. The half-life of commonly used β-lactam antibiotics is 1-2 hours, if the operation exceeds 3 hours, the second dose should be given, and if necessary, the third dose can also be used; no additional medication is required for antibiotics with a longer half-life (such as Ceftriaxone)

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
Scher observed 801 cases of clean-contaminated surgery and found that if the operation time is longer than 3 hours, the addition of one dose or the use of antibiotics with a longer half-life can significantly reduce the incidence of infection (Am Surg, 1997, 63:59)

How long should antibiotics be used?

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->
#
★Generally, no need to continue using antibiotics after elective surgery, if used, it should not exceed 24h
★Continuing to use antibiotics for several or several days after surgery does not improve the prophylactic effect
★Kager compared the prophylactic application of cefoperazone once and three times in colorectal surgery and confirmed that there was no difference; those who used three times had a tendency for an overgrowth of intestinal Pseudomonas and fungi

<!-- Slide number: 38 -->
Beijing, Nanjing, Wuhan, Shenyang, and 13 other hospitals used antibiotics (netilmicin) for the prevention of postoperative infections of the abdomen (prospective, controlled)
Infection rate was 0.84% (3/358) for those treated for 1 day
Infection rate was 2.68% (10/373) for those treated for 3 days
[Yang Zhiying et al., 2000]

<!-- Slide number: 39 -->
★ If the patient has obvious risk factors for infection or is using an artificial implant, an additional dose or doses may be administered up to 24 hours, or up to 48 hours in special circumstances
★ Organ transplant patients require medication for several days (3-5 days) postoperatively
★ For severe contamination, existing infection, or organ perforation (Class IV incision), antibiotics should continue to be used for therapeutic purposes postoperatively, not as prophylaxis

<!-- Slide number: 40 -->
For patients who have been contaminated before surgery (such as open trauma), the administration of multiple doses within 24 hours postoperatively may be beneficial, but it is not necessary to continue for several days
Fabian treated 280 cases of abdominal solid organ penetrating injuries (those treated within < 3 hours of injury) with a single preoperative dose, with no infections. In a randomized double-blind observation of 235 cases of hollow organ injuries, the infection rate was 8% (14% for colonic and rectal injuries) for those treated for 1 day; the infection rate was 10% (15% for colonic and rectal injuries) for those treated for 5 days

<!-- Slide number: 41 -->
# Randomized double-blind study of 248 cases of open fractures (Dellinger. Arch Surg, 1988, 123:339) showed that 5 days of medication was not better than a single dose

# Advantages of short-term prophylactic antibiotic use
★ Reduction in toxic side effects
★ Less likely to induce the development of resistant strains
★ Less likely to cause disruption of the intestinal flora
★ Reduction in the patient's economic burden
★ Ability to select a more expensive but effective antibiotic
★ Reduction in nursing workload

<!-- Slide number: 43 -->
# Quality Control Indicators
The prophylactic use of antimicrobial drugs for Class I incision surgery patients should not exceed 24 hours.
If the patient has obvious risk factors for infection, or is using an artificial implant, or has bacterial contamination before surgery (such as open trauma), an additional dose or doses may be administered up to 24 hours, or up to 48 hours in special circumstances.

<!-- Slide number: 44 -->
# Common Errors in Prophylactic Medication
★ Inappropriate timing (medication administered after surgery)
★ Too long duration (prophylactic medication for elective surgery for multiple days)
★ Inappropriate selection of medication (lacking specificity)

<!-- Slide number: 45 -->
# Local Prophylactic Application of Antimicrobial Drugs
★ Local antibiotic irrigation of the wound cavity or wound does not have a definite prophylactic effect and is not recommended
★ Especially, it should not be used to apply routine systemic antibiotics locally (inducing high resistance)
★ Local application of antibiotic sustained-release systems (PMMA-gentamicin bone cement or collagen membrane) may have certain benefits

# Summary
Prophylactic medication during the perioperative period is for the prevention of surgical site infections – purpose
Only 30% of Class I incision surgeries use antibiotics – indications
Medication should be administered 0.5-2 hours before surgery – timing
Class I incisions use a single dose or within 24 hours, and in special circumstances, up to 48 hours – duration
Primarily select first and second-generation cephalosporins – rationality
