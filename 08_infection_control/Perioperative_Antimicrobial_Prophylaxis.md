# Classification of Surgical Incisions
| Class I Incisions (Clean Surgery) | Surgery does not involve inflammatory areas and does not involve organs that are in direct communication with the external environment, such as the respiratory, digestive, and urinary生殖 systems |
| --- | --- |
| Class II Incisions (Clean-Polluted Surgery) | Operations involving the upper and lower respiratory tracts, upper and lower digestive tracts, urinary生殖 systems, or surgery through these organs, such as oral pharyngeal surgery, biliary tract surgery, total hysterectomy, rectal prostate surgery, and open fracture or trauma surgery, etc. |
| Class III Incisions (Polluted Surgery) | Surgeries that cause severe contamination of the surgical site, including: surgery involving acute inflammation but not suppurative areas; obvious overflow and contamination of gastrointestinal contents; fresh open wounds that have not been timely debrided; significant defects in aseptic technique such as thoracotomy, cardiac compression, etc. |
| Class IV Incisions (Contaminated-Infection Surgery) | Surgeries with necrotic tissue in old traumatic wounds; surgeries with existing clinical infection or organ perforation |
Note: 1. This guideline adopts the above classification. Currently, in China, surgical incisions on the first page of medical records are classified as Class I, II, and III, where Class I is the same as in this guideline, Class II is equivalent to Class II and III in this guideline, and Class III is equivalent to Class IV in this guideline. When referring to this guideline, attention should be paid to the distinction between the two classifications.

2. Case history 0 class refers to operations without incisions on the body surface or operations performed through natural body cavities, as well as percutaneous endoscopic operations. The prophylactic use of antimicrobial drugs is referred to in Appendix 3.

# When is Prophylactic Use of Antimicrobial Agents Required

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
# The following surgeries are generally not prophylactically administered antimicrobial agents
            Groin hernia repair (including mesh repair), thyroid disease surgery, breast surgery (breast cancer, breast augmentation, prophylactic use may be considered for breast reconstruction with implants), arthroscopic examination surgery, carotid endarterectomy, craniotomy for tumor resection, cataract (local application), and interventional diagnostic procedures via vascular access (for patients who will undergo vascular intervention again within 7 days, or who need to have catheters or sheaths inserted or left in place for more than 24 hours, prophylactic use should be considered).

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
    Class I incision tumor resection, internal fixation removal in orthopedics, biopsy, etc., are generally not prophylactically administered antimicrobial agents.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# Indications for Prophylactic Use of Antimicrobial Agents in Class I Incisions
⑴ Large surgical areas, long duration (generally >3h), with a high risk of contamination.
⑵ Surgery involving important organs, where infection can cause serious consequences, such as cranial surgery, cardiac surgery, etc.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
#
⑶ Foreign body implantation procedures, such as artificial heart valve implantation, permanent cardiac pacemaker placement, artificial joint replacement, etc.
⑷ Patients with high-risk factors for infection. Such as elderly (＞70 years old), diabetes, immunodeficiency or impairment (especially in recipients of organ transplantation), malnutrition, etc. (disease course should be recorded)

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
#
        Class II incision surgical sites have a large number of human resident flora, and surgery may contaminate the surgical site, leading to infection, so prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents is required for such surgeries.

# Selection of cephalosporin varieties for those allergic to cephalosporins
For patients allergic to cephalosporins, clindamycin can be used for Gram-positive cocci, and vancomycin or norvancomycin can be used if necessary. For gastric and duodenal surgery, hepatobiliary surgery, colorectal and rectal surgery, appendectomy, and II or III class incisions in gynecological surgery, if the patient is allergic to beta-lactam antibiotics, clindamycin + aminoglycosides, or aminoglycosides + metronidazole can be used. For Gram-negative bacilli, aztreonam or fosfomycin can be used.

# Summary of drug selection
Preventive medication for class I incision surgery: Cefazolin, Cefuroxime
For patients allergic to cephalosporins, clindamycin can be used.
Preventive medication for abdominal and pelvic surgery: Cefazolin, Cefuroxime ± Metronidazole, or Cephalosporin
For patients allergic to cephalosporins, clindamycin + aminoglycosides, or aminoglycosides + metronidazole can be used, or aztreonam or fosfomycin can also be selected.

# Only cephalothin can be selected for hepato-biliary and pancreatic surgery or colorectal, rectal, and appendectomy surgery.
Consider anaerobic bacteria for oral pharyngeal mucosal surgery.
Fluoroquinolones can be selected for urological surgery (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin).

# Discussion on the use of aminoglycosides for prophylaxis
Antibacterial spectrum: Appropriate for infections caused by Gram-negative bacilli and staphylococci.
Adverse reactions:
Can cause neuromuscular junction blockage, leading to symptoms such as muscle weakness and respiratory depression. Aminoglycosides and clindamycin can increase the neuromuscular blocking effect of anesthetics and anesthetic adjuvants.
"Antibacterial Drug Guidelines" (2015 edition):
Aminoglycosides should not be used with neuromuscular blocking agents.

# Aztreonam
Antibacterial spectrum: Used for infections caused by Gram-negative bacilli, effective against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, but less effective against other Pseudomonas species and Acinetobacter species. Ineffective against Gram-positive bacteria and anaerobes.
Note: Used under close observation for patients allergic to penicillins and cephalosporins.

# Drug selection for perioperative treatment
Drugs can be selected according to the condition. If the preoperative infection is not completely controlled, the selected drugs can cover the pathogens that may be infected during surgery (such as: class I incision surgery mainly prevents staphylococcal infection, and surgery involving respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts also needs to consider Gram-negative bacilli or anaerobic infections), then no drug change is needed during the perioperative period; if the preoperative infection has been controlled, then the perioperative period should be selected according to the preventive medication. Drug selection.
Administration time: 0.5-1h before surgery.

# Four: Preoperative Medication Timing

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Medication Timing
Intravenous infusion should be administered 0.5-1 hour before the incision of the skin and mucosa or at the start of anesthesia. The operation should begin after the infusion is completed, ensuring that the local tissue at the surgical site has reached a drug concentration sufficient to kill the bacteria that may contaminate the surgical site during the procedure.
Clindamycin, vancomycin, or fluoroquinolones, which require a longer infusion time, should be administered 1-2 hours before the operation.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
#
It is no longer emphasized that preventive medication must be administered after the umbilical cord is clamped during cesarean section.
A second dose should be administered intraoperatively if the operation time exceeds 3 hours or the blood loss is greater than 1500ml. It is recommended to bring the medication into the operating room or the preoperative preparation room to ensure the appropriate timing of preoperative medication.

# Five: Course of Treatment

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
# Course of Treatment
The total preventive medication time for Class I incisions should not exceed 24 hours in principle. For heart surgery, the duration can be extended to 48 hours depending on the situation.
The total preventive medication time for Class II incisions is also 24 hours, and can be extended to 48 hours if necessary. The total preventive medication time for Class II incisions should not exceed 48 hours.
Extending the medication time beyond 48 hours does not necessarily improve the preventive effect and increases the risk of resistant bacterial infections.

# Other Measures for Preventing Surgical Site Infections

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
# Other Measures for Preventing Surgical Site Infections
Preoperatively:
Minimize the preoperative hospital stay;
Perform proper skin preparation;
Infection control, hand hygiene;
Correct patient hyperglycemia, hypoalbuminemia, hypoxemia, cardiac function;

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
# Skin Preparation of the Surgical Area
Shaving the surgical area can cause epidermal injury and bacterial colonization, significantly increasing the incidence of incisional infection. It is better to use depilatory cream or trim the hair than to shave with a razor.
No skin preparation is required for areas with sparse hair;
For areas with dense hair that require skin preparation, it should be done immediately in the operating room before the start of the surgery;

# Other Measures for Preventing Surgical Site Infections
Intraoperatively:
Maintain patient temperature;
Correct hyperglycemia;
Skilled surgical techniques (reduce trauma, shorten operation time)
Proper placement of drains;
Strict control of personnel in the operating room

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->
# Other Measures for Preventing Surgical Site Infections
Postoperatively:
Change dressings promptly; when changing the incision dressings for the patient, strict adherence to aseptic techniques and the dressing change protocol is required.
Maintain unobstructed drainage; remove the drain from the patient as soon as possible based on the condition.

  Antibiotics are not a panacea

# Summary
1  Preventive medication for Class I incisions should be based on the indication, while Class II incisions have indications for preventive medication.
2  Strictly follow the "Guidelines for Antibiotic Use" to select the drug, with Cefazolin and Cefuroxime as the main choices.
3  Medication dose: Use the dose for mild to moderate infections.
4  Medication timing: Generally, medication should be administered 0.5-1 hour before surgery.
5  The principle for preventive medication in Class I incisions is not to exceed 24 hours, and for Class II incisions, not to exceed 48 hours.
