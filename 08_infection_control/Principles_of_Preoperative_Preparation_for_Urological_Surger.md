# Objectives of Urological Surgery
1. To save lives. For example, nephrectomy for renal trauma; renal transplantation for uremic patients.
2. To protect and restore function. For example, nephroureterostomy, penile prosthesis implantation, artificial sphincter implantation.
3. To restore organ morphology. For example, correction of various malformations of the urological male reproductive system, repair of various injuries, and restoration of physiological urination.
4. To clear the focus. For example, tumor resection, removal of calculi or foreign bodies, resection of tuberculosis foci. If malignant disease cannot be removed, other therapies should be supplemented to prolong life.

# Concept and Importance of Perioperative Management
In the three consecutive stages of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative, comprehensive examination and treatment of patients, other than the surgery itself, which are closely related to the surgery, are called perioperative management.
The importance of perioperative management lies in being able to target the treatment effectively; to bring the patient's psychology, physiology, and pathophysiology closer to normal; to improve the expected effect and quality of surgical treatment; to reduce the patient's pain; to reduce the occurrence of complications, and to enable the patient to recover sooner.
Preoperative preparation is particularly important in perioperative management, as it is a crucial guarantee for the smooth implementation of surgery and the patient's smooth recovery after surgery.

# (I) Preoperative Examination
1. Complete medical history and physical examination
2. Screening tests
   (1) Full blood count including red blood cell and hemoglobin values, white blood cell and classification, coagulation time, and platelet count. If necessary, perform prothrombin time and fibrinogen content determination.
   (2) Urinalysis including pH value, specific gravity, sugar, protein, and microscopic examination of sediment.
   (3) Stool examination, routine examination. If necessary, perform occult blood test.
   (4) Electrocardiogram.
   (5) Chest X-ray, anterior-posterior and lateral chest films if necessary. Serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BuN), liver function, and blood glucose level determination.
3. Special examinations According to the need for diagnosis, the following examinations may be performed if necessary, such as ultrasound, various contrast studies, endoscopy, radionuclide scanning, CT, MRI, etc.

# (II) Patient's Psychological Preparation
1. Preoperative conversation Medical staff must explain clearly to the patient and their family the necessity of the surgery, the possible effects, the risks of surgery, potential complications, and the postoperative recovery process, so that they can cooperate well.
2. For palliative surgery that cannot be cured, the medical secrecy system can be implemented for the patient. The true prognosis should be clearly explained to the family or unit leaders.
3. The patient should be explained clearly about the postoperative diet, position, defecation, oxygen therapy, catheterization, and various drains, as well as the possible incision pain and other discomforts, so that the patient can cooperate in treatment.

# (III) Exercises to Adapt to Postoperative Changes
1. Most patients are not accustomed to defecating in bed, so they should be fully trained in bed before surgery.
2. After surgery, patients often do not want to cough due to incision pain, or they worry about possible incision dehiscence. The importance of coughing and expectoration should be explained clearly, and the correct methods of coughing and expectoration should be taught.
3. Smoking patients should stop smoking two weeks before surgery.

# (IV) Estimation of Surgical Tolerance
Clinically, the tolerance of patients to surgery can be relatively divided into three categories:
1. Good tolerance: Good overall condition, limited surgical lesions, no significant impact on the patient's overall health, or minimal impact, no organic lesions in the systemic organs. Such patients can undergo surgery after general preparation.
2. Poor tolerance: Poor overall condition, older or younger age, significant impact of surgical lesions on the overall health, or associated with other organ dysfunction. For these patients, a period of preparation and treatment is required before they can tolerate general major surgery.
3. Very poor tolerance: Very poor overall condition, significant impact of surgical lesions on the overall health, or accompanied by severe lesions in one or more major organs. For these patients, active preparation is required before they can possibly tolerate surgery.

# (V) Estimation of Surgical Risk
The estimation of surgical risk involves a detailed preoperative examination to identify adverse factors and potential risks associated with surgery.

In addition to the risks that may occur from the surgery itself, such as massive hemorrhage, the following points should also be considered:
1. History of bleeding tendency in the individual or family.
2. Allergy history to certain drugs such as disinfectants, anesthetics, analgesics, or antibiotics.
3. Recent medication treatment, such as the use of corticosteroids, diuretics, insulin, digitalis, etc.
4. Anatomical relationship between the surgical site and adjacent organs. The degree of adhesion to major abdominal blood vessels. The relationship with the liver, gallbladder, spleen, pancreas, intestines, and female reproductive organs in the pelvis. The possibility of injury and resection during surgery, and the associated risks.
5. Risk factors for important organs, and possible functional failure caused by the impact of surgery.

# (Seven) Identifying Potential Factors Leading to Infection from Prophylactic Antibiotic Use
   Such as diabetes, obesity, malnutrition, metabolic diseases, abnormal metabolism of glucocorticoids, local infection foci, etc., prophylactic antibiotics should be administered preoperatively. They should be used shortly before and after the surgical field or incision is contaminated. When bacteria侵入 tissue, the concentration of antibiotics in the tissue should reach an effective level, rather than waiting until the patient returns to the ward after surgery.

# Principles of Prophylactic Antibiotic Use
   Administer a single dose of antibiotics a few hours before the start of surgery;
   The duration of prophylactic antibiotic use should be short. Prolonged use does not reduce the infection rate of wounds and may increase the side effects of antibiotics, such as the development of drug-resistant strains or secondary infections;
   Select sensitive antibiotics;
   For sterile surgery without potential factors leading to infection, prophylactic antibiotic use is not generally recommended.

# (Eight) Special Preparations Before Urological Surgery
1. Drug Preparation
   Such as antibiotic treatment for urinary tract infection patients;
   Treatment with α-adrenergic receptor blockers for pheochromocytoma;
   Treatment with spironolactone and potassium salts for patients with primary hyperaldosteronism;
   Treatment with cortisone acetate for patients with Cushing's syndrome;
   Certain chemotherapy before tumor surgery, immunosuppressive therapy before organ transplantation;
   Antihypertensive drug treatment before surgery for renal hypertension;
   For patients who have been taking corticosteroids for a long period (6 months to one year before surgery), adrenal cortex suppression may occur, and consideration should be given to administering exogenous glucocorticoids before surgery.

# (Nine) Other Preparations
   Before major surgery, ensure blood typing and cross-matching tests are performed, and a certain amount of whole blood is prepared.
   If the patient's body temperature is elevated, there is respiratory tract infection, a rash in the surgical area, menses, or infection around the surgical incision, and it is not an emergency surgery, the surgery should generally be postponed.
   The composition of the surgical team and the preparation of special surgical instruments should be arranged appropriately for any type of surgery. The surgeon should personally select some special instruments.
   For more complex cases or surgeries, a preoperative discussion should be organized to perfect the surgical treatment plan.

# (Ten) Preoperative Day Preparation
1. Gastrointestinal Preparation
   Fast for 12 hours before surgery and 4 hours before drinking water; for perineal surgery and patients under general anesthesia, an enema should be administered the night before surgery; for urinary diversion or non-diversion surgery using the intestines, the preoperative bowel preparation for intestinal surgery should be followed.

2. Skin Preparation
   Elective surgery can be performed in the afternoon before surgery. The patient should bathe or shower, wash their hair, trim their nails, and change into clean underwear. 3. Drug Allergy Testing

# Special Preoperative Preparation
(One) Whether patients with heart disease can tolerate surgery needs to be studied together with the Department of Internal Medicine and Anesthesiology, and preoperative preparations should be made to prevent accidents. The estimation of cardiac function mainly refers to the compensatory function of the heart, followed by the type of heart disease.

# Clinically, cardiac function is divided into four grades
Good: Able to hold their breath for more than 30 seconds, able to walk quickly, no palpitations or shortness of breath, no significant difference from general patients.
Poor: Able to hold their breath for more than 20 seconds, unable to run or perform strenuous work, no special difficulty with anesthesia.
Weakened: Able to hold their breath for 10 seconds, must be in bed, mild exertion causes palpitations and shortness of breath, and sufficient preparation is required before surgery.
Severely impaired: Able to hold their breath for less than 10 seconds, unable to lie flat, even sitting breathing, rales are heard at the base of the lungs, and surgery should be postponed except for emergency rescue.

# Clinically, heart disease is divided into three categories
I class: Non-blue congenital heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, hypertension heart disease, which does not increase the risk of surgery and anesthesia.
II class: Coronary heart disease, heart failure, atrioventricular block (especially high-degree block), syphilitic heart disease, which increases the risk of surgery and anesthesia compared to general patients.
III class: Acute myocarditis, acute myocardial infarction, which significantly increases the risk of surgery and anesthesia, and surgery should be postponed except for emergency rescue.

# (II) Key Points for Special Preoperative Preparation
Correction of water and electrolyte imbalances, especially in patients who have been on low-sodium diets and diuretics for a long time, often results in hyponatremia and hypokalemia. During surgery, this can lead to arrhythmias and shock, which should be corrected preoperatively.
Correction of anemia: Anemic patients have poor oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, which affects myocardial oxygen supply. Preoperative blood transfusions can be administered in small, frequent doses.
Control of arrhythmias: For occasional ventricular premature complexes, no special treatment is required; however, if there is atrial flutter with tachycardia, intravenous push of digoxin 0.4 mg in 20 mL of 25% glucose and propranolol 3 times daily can be used.
