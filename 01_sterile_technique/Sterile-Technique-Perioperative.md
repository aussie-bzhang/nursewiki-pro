I. Historical Development of Aseptic Technology

As early as 1846, the obstetrician Semmelweis in Vienna observed that there was a significant difference in maternal mortality rates between two obstetric wards in a general hospital (16% vs 7%). The first ward was attended by doctors, while the second ward was attended by midwives. Observations revealed that doctors performed post-mortem examinations in the anatomical room and then directly delivered to the mothers, thus inferring that the hands of medical personnel played a decisive role. Semmelweis therefore required doctors to wash their hands with chlorinated lime before delivering to the mothers, and over the next few years, the mortality rate in the first ward significantly decreased.

Semmelweis is therefore also recognized as the "pioneer" of hand hygiene!

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->

• In 1867, the British surgeon Lister used carbolic acid solution to disinfect the hands and instruments of medical personnel, reducing the mortality rate of amputation surgery from 45.7% to 15%.

It can be seen that disinfection can significantly reduce the potential pathogenic bacteria carried on hands and instruments, thereby reducing the chance of surgical infection.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->

1. In 1882, the German surgeon Friedrich developed a steam sterilizer.

1. In 1888, the Berlin dermatologist and internist Paul Ehrlich improved the surgical disinfection method, using hot water and soap to wash hands for 10 minutes, followed by alcohol for 5 minutes, and finally disinfectant for 3-5 minutes.

1. In 1890, the Baltimore surgeon Halsted invented extremely thin rubber gloves.

1. In 1908, Grosh used iodine for skin disinfection at the surgical site.

II. Concept of Aseptic Technology

Aseptic technology (asepsis) refers to the operational techniques in medical and nursing care to prevent the entry of all microorganisms into the human body and to prevent the contamination of sterile items and sterile areas.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->

III. Aseptic Principles in the Operating Room

1. Environment: Clean, spacious, closed doors, regular disinfection. Stop cleaning and reduce movement 30 minutes before operation.

2. Standard attire for medical and nursing staff

3. Rational placement of sterile items

   1) Separate placement of sterile items and non-sterile items
   2) The sterile package should be marked with the name and sterilization date, and arranged in order of expiration date.
   3) The shelf life is related to the environmental temperature and humidity and the packaging material, generally 7-14 days (7 days in our hospital), and should be re-sterilized if expired or the packaging is damp.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->

III. Aseptic Principles in the Operating Room

4. Strict implementation of operational specifications

   1) Use sterile forceps (tongs) to pick up sterile items. Once the sterile items are taken out, they cannot be put back into the sterile container (package).

   2) Strictly define the boundaries between sterile and non-sterile areas. During aseptic operations, non-disinfected items, hands, and arms cannot touch sterile items or cross the sterile area.

   3) During aseptic operations, the operator's body should maintain a certain distance from the sterile area, and hands and forearms should be kept within the field of vision below the shoulder and above the waist or operating table.

   4) All aseptic operations should use sterile items, and one sterile item should be used for one patient only.

(A) Management of Building and Environment

1. Reasonable operating room architecture and layout are the prerequisite for strengthening aseptic management and reducing the rate of surgical infection.

1. Strictly divide the restricted area, semi-restricted area, and unrestricted area.

1. Separate clean and dirty flows, design different flows and channels for different personnel and items.

1. According to the actual situation, you can choose the central clean hall type, central supply type, peripheral supply type, peripheral collection type, and one-way through type layout. Among them, the peripheral collection type is more common in China.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->

Environmental management requirements for clean operating rooms:

1. First, do a good job of cleaning and dust reduction, wet wiping disinfection, and use non-woven fabrics that do not produce dust for dressings. All items must be unpacked, cleaned, and other preliminary treatments before entering the laminar airflow operating room. Staff should also go through air shower or other methods to reduce dust as much as possible before entering the laminar airflow operating room.

1. During the operation, strictly control personnel, strengthen the management of visitors, and try to reduce personnel movement.

1. All operations should be performed gently, avoiding unnecessary movements.

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->

Environmental management requirements for clean operating rooms:

1. Reduce the number of door openings during the operation, maintain positive pressure in the operating room (except for negative pressure operating rooms). Perform aseptic techniques on the upwind side of the air flow, and perform operations that produce air pollution on the return air side.

1. After the operation is completed, do a good job of cleaning work, and continue to open the laminar airflow equipment for a certain period of time between consecutive operations (the minimum self-cleaning time for level Ⅰ operating rooms is 10 minutes, level Ⅱ and Ⅲ is 20 minutes, and level Ⅳ is 30 minutes).

1. Noise: ≤51 decibels in level Ⅰ clean operating rooms, ≤49 decibels in level Ⅱ, Ⅲ, and Ⅳ clean operating rooms. The static pressure difference between the clean area and non-clean area should be ≥5pa.

1. The laminar airflow equipment should be regularly inspected by professional maintenance personnel, replace filters, and find the cause according to the biological detection results to maintain the normal operation of the system.

Management of Disinfection and Sterilization of Items (Part Two)

1 The disinfection and sterilization of various items in the operating room are crucial for ensuring surgical safety. It is essential to select the correct disinfection and sterilization methods; at the same time, management should be strengthened in all aspects of the packaging, storage, and use of sterile items. Additionally, the disposal of waste items in the operating room is also an important factor in protecting the environment and preventing nosocomial infections.

1 Autoclaves should be used and maintained by designated personnel, and disinfection staff should receive pre-job training and hold a certificate before taking up their positions. Routine checks and B-D tests should be conducted before sterilization each day. During each sterilization cycle, parameters such as pressure, temperature, and time should be carefully observed and recorded, and strict adherence to operational procedures such as loading methods and capacity should be ensured. The sterilizer should also be subjected to biological testing on a regular basis. The use of large autoclaves in conjunction with small rapid-pressure steam sterilization equipment can effectively improve efficiency, avoid resource waste, and provide strong support for clinical practice.

Management of Disinfection and Sterilization of Items (Part Two)

1 Ethylene oxide sterilization can sterilize items that cannot withstand heat or moisture, ensuring a longer period of sterility. Attention should be paid to the stage of ethylene oxide residual gas exhaust to avoid health damage to staff.

1 Chemical disinfection solution immersion disinfection and sterilization: when selecting chemical disinfectants, the most ideal disinfectant should be chosen based on the type of item and the disinfection and sterilization requirements. Appropriate treatment should be carried out before use to remove residual liquid. Monitoring should be strengthened during the use of chemical disinfectants to ensure the correct concentration and usage method of the drug solution, ensuring the effectiveness of disinfection and sterilization.

1 In addition, there are various other methods of item disinfection and sterilization, such as low-temperature plasma, with certain differences in specific operations and management.

Clean Operating Room

1 The air supply system of the clean operating room
Utilizing air purification technology, the air is highly filtered, and various indicators such as the fresh air flow rate, direction, and velocity are controlled, maintaining a "positive pressure" state in a relatively closed environment, with the purified air always flowing from the high-purity surgical area to the low-purity area, providing a clean, comfortable, and suitable temperature and humidity surgical space environment for surgery.

1 The Technical Code for Clean Operating Rooms of Hospitals GB50333-2013 was officially implemented on June 1, 2014, and the 2002 version was abolished.

1 Clean operating rooms equipped with air supply systems generally do not require physical or chemical methods of air disinfection, providing a safety guarantee for clinical patients and also posing new requirements for environmental management.

Management of Sterile Technique (Part Three)

1 The management of sterile technique is a key aspect of operating room nursing quality management and is one of the important links that directly affect the patient's surgical outcome and even life. 

1 The operation of sterile technique is a dynamic process, involving every detail and every relevant person in the entire surgical process. Any minor step, from entering the operating room, starting surgical preparation, surgery, to every action during the surgical process, can lead to the failure of sterile technique if there is a moment of carelessness, causing surgical infection and affecting the patient's recovery.

1 The effect of sterile technique is not immediate, but often becomes apparent only after surgery.

1 There is a certain probability that multiple related factors work together, making the relationship between the process and the result very complex. All these make sterile technique easily overlooked and affect the strict implementation of sterile technique.

Management of Sterile Technique (Part Three)
In summary, sterile technique is not only a technique but also a system, an idea, and a responsibility, reflected in every link of the work of all personnel, reflecting the professional quality and moral accomplishment of medical staff, and is one of the important links to ensure the success of surgery. Only through the joint efforts of all personnel can the expected goals and results be achieved.

1. Dress Code in the Operating Room

Purpose
The human body is the main source of contamination in the surgical environment. Dressing in the operating room includes hats, masks, clothes, pants, shoes, protective eyewear, and other isolation measures, which provide a two-way anti-pollution barrier for patients and surgeons, providing a highly clean and hygienic surgical environment.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->

give an example

Implementing strict "isolation of body substances" (isolation of body fluids) to reduce the risk of exogenous infection, with gloves, isolation gowns, plastic aprons, masks, protective eyewear, and face shields, etc. When contacting items contaminated with blood or other body fluids, gloves must be worn, and hands should be washed immediately after removing the gloves. When the head and face may come into contact with blood, other body fluids, and bone fragments, masks, eye protective goggles, or face shields should be worn. Plastic aprons or waterproof surgical gowns should be worn when necessary. When caring for patients with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), circulating nurses and anesthesiologists should wear isolation gowns to strengthen their own protection.

(1) All personnel entering the semi-restricted and restricted areas of the operating room should wear specialized operating room clothing.

(2) All head and facial hair, including the front of the ears and the neck, should be covered in the semi-restricted and restricted areas of the operating room.

(3) All personnel entering the restricted areas of the operating room should wear masks.

2. Sterile Operation Behavior in the Operating Room

Purpose
Ensure the sterilization area of the operating room, and every person in the operating room has the responsibility to execute sterile operations. Sterile operations should be strictly implemented before, during, and after surgery to reduce the opportunity for contamination.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->

2. Sterile Operation Behavior in the Operating Room
(1) Surgeons should wear hats, masks, clothes, pants, shoes, protective eyewear, and other isolation measures.

(2) Use surgical drapes to create a sterile area.

(3) All items in the sterile area must be kept sterile.

(4) The opening, distribution, and transfer of items sent to the sterile area should maintain their sterility and integrity.

(5) The sterile area must be continuously monitored and maintained.

(6) All personnel in or around the sterile area should pay attention to maintaining the integrity of the sterile area.

3. Surgical Hand Hygiene

Purpose

(1) Remove transient bacteria and some resident bacteria from the skin of the hands and arms.
(2) Prevent patients from being infected during surgery.

Methods: Two methods, hand scrubbing and no hand scrubbing.

3. Surgical Hand Hygiene
**Hand scrubbing precautions:**
(1) Scrubbing order: first fingers, then palms, first palmar surface, then dorsal side, and pay attention to the scrubbing of fingertips, interdigital webbing, nail margin, and nail groove.
(2) Rinse method: first hands, then forearms, then upper arms. Keep the fingertips at the highest position, and the elbow at the lowest position to avoid water flowing back towards the hands.
(3) Standardized actions and appropriate force during scrubbing.
(4) Hand scrub brushes should be sterilized.
(5) Control the water flow during handwashing to prevent splashing onto the surgical gown. If wet, change it promptly.

WHO's "Five Moments for Hand Hygiene":
• Before touching the patient
• Before aseptic procedures
• After contact with body fluids
• After contact with the patient
• After contact with the patient's environment

4. Donning sterile surgical gown
**Purpose:**
(1) Prevent the infection of the patient by microorganisms carried by the surgical staff's body and clothing.
(2) Establish an aseptic barrier.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->

4. Donning sterile surgical gown
**Operation process:**
(1) Take the folded sterile surgical gown, choose a spacious area, lift the collar with one hand, and shake it open. Pay attention not to let the surgical gown touch other items.
(2) Hold the two corners of the collar with both hands, with the sleeves facing forward, unfold the surgical gown, and make the inside face towards oneself.
(3) Lightly throw the surgical gown upwards, insert both hands into the sleeves as the hands follow, extend the arms forward, do not lift them over the shoulders, and do not spread them to the sides to avoid contamination.
(4) Tie the belt: loosen the belt, hand one end to another person (wearing sterile gloves or holding forceps), rotate in place, and tie the two ends in front of the waist.

<!-- Slide number: 30 -->

6. Skin antisepsis of surgical field
**Principles and methods of skin antisepsis:**
(1) When skin antisepsis is performed, it should be wiped from the central part of the surgical area to the periphery.
(2) If it is an infected wound or surgery at the anus, etc., it should be wiped from the periphery of the surgical area to the infected wound or perineum and anus.
(3) The gauze soaked with the drug liquid that has already contacted the contaminated area should not be wiped back to the clean area.
(4) The range of skin antisepsis of the surgical area should include a 15cm area around the surgical incision.

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->

Change surgical gown for consecutive surgeries
After the surgery is completed, if another surgery is to be performed, it is necessary to change the surgical gown and gloves. First, the circulating nurse unfastens the belt and the collar tie, then another person helps or the person themselves removes the surgical gown, and finally, the gloves are removed.

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->

4. Donning sterile surgical gown
**Precautions:**
(1) Don the sterile surgical gown within the intended sterile area to avoid contamination.
(2) The surgical gown should be of the appropriate size and length, requiring it to be free from contamination, dampness, and damage.
(3) When picking up the surgical gown, only touch the inside of the gown.
(4) After donning the surgical gown and gloves, place the hands in front of the chest, do not place the hands under the armpits or lift them over the shoulders, do not hang them below the waist, do not leave the operating room, and do not touch non-sterile items.
(5) If the surgical gown is contaminated with blood and body fluids, it should be changed promptly.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->

5. Donning sterile gloves
**Closed method:**
(1) Use the right hand to take the left glove through the sleeve, with the finger tip facing the arm, the thumb facing each other, place it on the left sleeve, and insert the thumbs of both hands through the reversed part of the glove and turn it over the sleeve.
(2) Do the same to wear the right glove.

**Open method (omitted)**

7. Surgical drapes
**Purpose:**
Establish an aseptic area, effectively prevent the invasion of microorganisms into the surgical wound, fully expose the surgical field, including possible extended incisions and additional incisions, respect the patient's privacy, and avoid unnecessary exposure.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->

7. Surgical drapes
**Principles and methods of surgical drapes:**
(1) When disinfecting and draping, consider the possible extended incisions and additional incisions during surgery, so leave some room.
(2) Those who have not donned a sterile surgical gown should start from the opposite side, and those who have donned a sterile surgical gown should start from the near side.
(3) When draping, the operator should wrap their already disinfected hands in the inner angle of the surgical drape to prevent contamination. The drape should be expanded from the incision to the periphery.
(4) The surgical incision edge, instrument table, and instrument tray all need to be draped with four or more layers.
(5) The sterile drape at the edge of the operating bed should hang down at least 30~40cm.
(6) If the disinfectant drape is damaged, stained, or damp, it should not be used.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->

8. Aseptic techniques in surgery
**Behavioral norms:**
(1) Clarify the concept of asepsis and establish an aseptic zone. The areas above the shoulders, below the waist, the back, and below the operating table plane are considered to be contaminated.
(2) All items within the aseptic zone must be sterile. If the sterile package is damaged, damp, or suspected of contamination, it is considered to be contaminated.
(3) The incision edge should be covered with a large gauze pad or surgical drape, and fixed with a hemostat and suture, only exposing the surgical incision. Preoperative application of sterile plastic film to the surgical area can achieve the same purpose.

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->

8. Aseptic techniques in surgery
**Behavioral norms:**
(4) Instruments should be passed from the chest of the surgical staff, avoiding the passing of items or instruments behind.
(5) If contaminated instruments from cutting the gastrointestinal tract are used, they should be placed in a bowl to distinguish them from other sterile items.
(6) If the gloves are pierced or contaminated during the operation, they should be changed promptly. When the surgical staff change positions on the same side, they should face each other.
(7) Close the doors and windows during the operation, try to minimize the number of times they are opened and closed. Limit the entry of non-surgical staff into the operating room, reduce personnel movement, and keep visitors at least 30cm away from the surgical staff.

<!-- Slide number: 38 -->

8. Aseptic techniques in surgery

(8) Cover the wet sterile drape promptly

(9) Disinfect and protect the skin and hollow organs during the operation

(10) Contact with a contaminated area on the forearm or elbow requires the replacement of the sterile surgical gown or the addition of sterile sleeves.
