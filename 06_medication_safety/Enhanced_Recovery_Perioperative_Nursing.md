# Definition of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS)
The definition of Enhanced Recovery has evolved from FTS to the currently widely adopted ERAS

The concept of ERAS was first reported and implemented by Danish surgeons Kehlet et al. in 1997. The idea of accelerated recovery surgery (fast track surgery, FTS), based on the principle of reducing perioperative stress responses, emerged and gradually became the key to perioperative management.

Accelerated recovery surgery has had several names, and the name commonly used at home and abroad is ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery). ERAS involves a series of evidence-based perioperative management measures to reduce the physiological and psychological trauma stress of surgical patients, achieving rapid recovery for surgical patients.

A multi-disciplinary collaborative approach, based on evidence-based principles, to promote rapid postoperative recovery through a multi-modal care

Dr. Kehlet H of Denmark

# Main Objectives and Significance of Enhanced Recovery Surgery
Multiple factors lead to body stress responses
ERAS is mainly to control the pathophysiological responses during the perioperative period, the purpose is to promote patient recovery, not just for early discharge

An important pathophysiological basis for the occurrence of postoperative complications is the body's stress response caused by surgical trauma, intraoperative hypothermia, inappropriate fluid therapy, postoperative pain, and the patient's long-term inactivity.

Malignant tumors, complex surgery, chemotherapy, and prolonged bed rest are risk factors for venous thromboembolism. In patients with risk factors who do not receive prophylactic antithrombotic therapy, the incidence of postoperative deep vein thrombosis can reach 30%, and the incidence of fatal pulmonary embolism is nearly 1%. It is recommended that patients with moderate to high risk (Caprini score ≥3) begin prophylactic antithrombotic therapy 2-12 hours before surgery and continue the medication until discharge or 14 days postoperatively. In addition to medication, mechanical measures such as intermittent pneumatic compression pumps or elastic stockings should be combined in high-risk patients with venous thromboembolism if necessary.

# Selection of surgical methods

In 1987, Frenchman Philippe Mouret completed the first laparoscopic cholecystectomy, marking a milestone in the development of modern minimally invasive medicine.
In just over 20 years, the development of minimally invasive disciplines has been rapidly evolving, with the development of technologies such as laparoscopy and the da Vinci robot, making surgical incisions smaller and smaller.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
# Small incisions do not equal small surgery
Laparoscopic surgery is a typical representative of minimally invasive surgery.

Small incisions are not limited to performing small surgeries such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), liver resection and pancreaticoduodenectomy can also be performed minimally invasively.

Small incisions do not necessarily mean small surgery; it is necessary to complete the same surgical procedures through small incisions as traditional approaches, with similar or even better outcomes.

# Intraoperative warming

Monitor the patient's body temperature intraoperatively and maintain the core body temperature >36 ℃ using measures such as pre-warming, increasing the room temperature of the operating room, using liquid warming devices, warming blankets, and warm air blowers.

# Fluid therapy
The purpose of fluid therapy is to improve tissue perfusion by optimizing circulatory volume, ensuring that the patient's blood volume and cardiovascular function are matched, and avoiding hypovolemia and fluid overload.
For small to medium-sized surgeries, follow the "standard protocol" (physiological needs + preoperative fluid loss + fluid redistribution + fluid loss after anesthesia) to supplement balanced crystalloids, with a baseline of 1-2 ml•kg－1•h－1, and provide 1-2 L of supplemental doses as needed; intraoperative blood loss can be replaced with 1:1 crystalloids, colloids, and/or blood products; monitor respiratory rate, heart rate, and oxygen saturation.
Complex surgeries require a precise fluid resuscitation plan, using the "goal-directed fluid therapy" strategy,完善监测, avoiding extravascular volume overload and tissue edema.
Vasopressor drugs may be used to treat hypotension caused by vascular obstruction.
Current evidence indicates that balanced salt crystalloids are the first choice for intraoperative fluid resuscitation.
    Goal-directed fluid therapy: After establishing hemodynamic monitoring (stroke volume, cardiac output, systolic pressure variability, pulse pressure variability, and stroke volume variability), administer a fluid challenge based on 1-2 ml•kg－1•h－1 balanced salt crystalloids as a baseline. (For example, when stroke volume decreases, administer 200-250 ml of colloids or balanced salt crystalloids; if stroke volume increases by 10%-15% or more, continue to administer 200 ml of fluid; if stroke volume increases by less than 10%, discontinue the fluid challenge and continue with baseline fluid administration.)

# Drainage Tube Management
Selective application of various catheters, aiming to minimize use or remove them as soon as possible.
Nasogastric Tube: Not recommended for routine use after surgery; selective use is only indicated in cases of delayed gastric emptying.
Catheter: Avoid use or remove as soon as possible; in the absence of special circumstances, the catheter can be removed 1-2 days after surgery.
In traditional practice, it was routine to leave a drainage tube in place after surgery to prevent complications such as fluid accumulation, hemorrhage, anastomotic fistula, and infection. Recent meta-analysis results show that the placement of drainage tubes around the anastomosis has no significant impact on postoperative complications and outcomes for patients. The placement of drainage tubes may affect early ambulation after surgery, increase postoperative complications, and prolong hospital stay. Therefore, routine placement of drainage tubes is not recommended. In cases where there is infection at the surgical site, poor blood supply, excessive tension at the anastomosis, or other factors that may lead to poor healing, it is recommended to leave a drainage tube in place. Pancreatic surgery requires routine placement of an abdominal drainage tube.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
# Promoting Intestinal Peristalsis Recovery
Postoperative ileus can delay the time for early oral intake in patients, and is one of the main factors determining the length of hospital stay after surgery, especially for abdominal surgery patients. Measures to prevent postoperative ileus include: multimodal analgesia, reducing the dosage of opioid drugs, controlling fluid intake, performing minimally invasive surgery, using selective peripheral opioid receptor antagonists, not leaving a nasogastric tube in place, chewing gum, early feeding, and ambulation.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
# Postoperative Nutritional Support
Resume oral intake as soon as possible
Patients undergoing rectal or pelvic surgery can start eating 4 hours after surgery
Patients undergoing colectomy or gastric resection can start eating and drinking water 1 day after surgery
For pancreatic surgery, oral intake can be gradually resumed 3-4 days after surgery, depending on the patient's tolerance.

    Over the past 20 years, the popularization of minimally invasive concepts, the widespread application of laparoscopic technology, and the establishment of evidence-based medicine have all provided the possibility and feasibility for the clinical application of ERAS. The implementation of the ERAS concept is a systematic project involving all aspects of diagnostic and therapeutic activities. It advocates for the establishment of a standardized management team involving surgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, physiotherapists, and even psychological experts. Clear and standardized goals should be set. Both evidence-based medicine and the objective reality of hospitals and patients should be respected. It should be emphasized that in clinical practice, one should not generalize and should not mechanistically and dogmatically simplify the understanding of the ERAS concept and various optimization measures. The practice of ERAS still needs to adhere to the principle of individualization to maximize the benefits to patients.
