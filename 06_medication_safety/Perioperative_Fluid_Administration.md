# Movement of Body Fluids Between Intravascular and Extravascular Spaces

The movement of body fluids between intravascular and extravascular spaces is the result of the interaction between hydrostatic pressure and colloid osmotic pressure.

Crystal Osmotic Pressure (278.39 mOsm/kg)
Colloid Osmotic Pressure (1.61 mOsm/kg, 25 mmHg)

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# Starling's Law
Q = KA [( pc-pi ) + δ(JⅠI - JⅠc]

Q = Fluid filtration coefficient
K = Capillary membrane area
pc = Capillary hydrostatic pressure
pi = Interstitial hydrostatic pressure
δ = Albumin reflection coefficient
JⅠI = Interstitial colloid oncotic pressure
JⅠc = Capillary colloid oncotic pressure

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# Formula for Calculating Plasma Volume Expansion

PVE = Infusion Volume × (PV / VD)

PVE represents the plasma expansion volume
PV represents the plasma volume
VD represents the fluid distribution volume

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# Example of Static Fluid Resuscitation Calculation

For a 70kg patient requiring the expansion of 500ml of plasma, how much 5% GS, 0.9% NS, or balanced fluid is needed?

5% GS: VD is the total body fluid volume
VD = 60% × 70 = 42L, therefore, the infusion volume = 500 × 42 / 3.5 = 6000

0.9% NS or balanced fluid: VD is the extracellular fluid
VD = 20% × 70 = 14L, therefore, the infusion volume = 500 × 14 / 3.5 = 2000

# Dynamic Analysis of Fluid Dynamics
Through the analysis of material balance laws, changes in volume are determined
Determine the Hb and red blood cell volume before and after infusion to analyze fluid expansion, transfer, and distribution, etc.
Determine the initial blood volume (BV0), and blood volume at subsequent time points
     BVn = BV0 × B - Hb0 / B - Hbn
Extravascular net transfer volume (EVV) = PV0 + IV - (PVn + UV)
      IV is the infusion volume, UV is urine volume
Volume expansion efficacy (VEE) = (BVn - BV0) / IV
Index of vascular dilation or constriction = IV - (BVn - BV0) - UV

# Fluid Dynamics Model
Established by Lars Stahle in 1997, a one- and two-compartment model similar to pharmacokinetics
Drobin established a three-compartment fluid model in 2002, providing a method for analyzing the dynamics of hypertonic and hypotonic fluids
It is not completely consistent with the traditional pharmacokinetic model. As the drug enters the body fluid with the solvent, there is a certain binding site, so the distribution and target volume are fixed

# Two-Compartment Fluid Dynamics Model

v1: The volume of fluid entering the central fluid compartment
v2: The volume of the peripheral compartment's expandable fluid compartment
V1 and V2 are the target volumes of the central and peripheral compartment fluid compartments
Ki: The fluid input rate
Kb: The basic elimination rate of the fluid in the central compartment
Kr: The coefficient when deviating from V
Kt: The coefficient for the difference between the two-compartment fluid compartment and the target volume

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
# Three-Compartment Fluid Dynamics Model
The one- and two-compartment models are only suitable for isotonic or nearly isotonic fluids
For hypertonic or hypotonic fluids, there will be transfer between intracellular and extracellular fluids, which is consistent with the three-compartment model

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
# Influence of Anesthesia on Fluid Dynamics
Connolly et al. showed that:
Under conscious conditions, the input of isotonic fluid is basically a one-compartment model, with limited and transient expansion effect, and is quickly eliminated through urine
Under general anesthesia, the input of isotonic fluid is basically a two-compartment model, with an expansion effect similar to that under conscious conditions, but the fluid is mainly quickly transferred and distributed to the peripheral compartment, with reduced urine output leading to limited fluid elimination

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
# Influence of Anesthesia on Fluid Dynamics
Robert et al. showed that:
The influence of spinal anesthesia on fluid dynamics is similar to that of general anesthesia
During subarachnoid block, infusion of Ringer's solution and dextran is basically a two-compartment model
The kt of Ringer's solution is significantly higher than that of dextran, indicating that Ringer's solution is more transferred and distributed to the peripheral compartment

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# Influence of Anesthesia on Fluid Dynamics
The target volume before anesthesia induction is significantly larger than that after induction, and Kr decreases after induction, indicating increased fluid retention in the central compartment and reduced transfer and distribution to the peripheral compartment, which helps maintain stable circulatory function

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Influence of Blood Loss on Fluid Dynamics
Blood loss reduces the effective blood volume below the target volume, increasing the retention of isotonic fluid in the vascular space

Drobind infused equal amounts of Ringer's solution in three groups of patients with normal blood volume, blood loss of 450ml, and 900ml, respectively. The clearance rate was reduced and the peak effect was high in patients with more bleeding, and the retention time of the fluid in the blood was prolonged

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
# Influence of Surgery and Trauma on Fluid Dynamics
Surgery and trauma affect fluid dynamics through stress and endocrine pathways
Changing the one-compartment model to the two-compartment model
Reduced fluid elimination, easy transfer and distribution to the peripheral compartment

# Changes in Perioperative Fluid Administration
In the 1950s, Moore advocated strict restriction of fluid administration.
In the 1960s, Shires increased intraoperative fluid administration.
In the 1970s, the contradiction between crystalloid and colloid fluids.
In the 1990s, blood conservation and blood dilution.
In the new century, fluid metabolism dynamics guiding fluid administration.

# Compensatory Expansion
Anaesthesia → Vasodilation, myocardial depression → Increase in vascular volume → Relative hypovolemia
Supplement with 5~7ml/kg balanced fluid at the same time as pre-anesthesia or induction.

Anaesthesia effect termination → Vascular volume recovery, patients with heart and kidney insufficiency should be aware of the risk of excessive blood volume.

<!-- Slide number: 41 -->
# Physiological Needs
4-2-1 rule:
The first 10kg requires 4ml/kg
The second 10kg requires 2ml/kg
The remaining kg of body weight requires 1ml/kg
Physiological needs are supplemented on a timed basis.

Hourly requirement

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
# Physiological Needs
Example: For a 70kg patient, the hourly physiological need is
4×10+2×10+1×50=110ml/h

Additionally, the daily energy requirement is 110kcal×24h=2640kcal/d
    Na+1.5mmol/kg, K+1~1.5mmol/L

<!-- Slide number: 43 -->
# Accumulated Loss
Accumulated loss =
   Physiological needs × fasting time + preoperative additional loss and third space loss
Assess the accumulated loss based on clinical symptoms, signs, and the impact on circulatory function
Accumulated loss is mainly corrected before surgery
For elective surgery with no additional loss, supplement during anesthesia

<!-- Slide number: 44 -->
# Continued Loss
Intraoperative blood loss: supplement in a timely manner to maintain normal blood volume and ECF. The ratio of blood loss to crystalloid is 1:3~4, and to colloid is 1:1. Blood transfusion should be administered if indicated
Pleural and peritoneal fluid electrolytes are similar to ECF, and protein is 30~100% of plasma. Supplement with crystalloid and colloid in a 2:1 ratio
Intraoperative urine is supplemented with crystalloid
Field and respiratory evaporation losses are supplemented with pure water using crystalloid
COP <15~17mmHg requires colloid supplementation

<!-- Slide number: 45 -->
# Indications for Blood Transfusion
US NIH recommends Hb<60g/L
Hebert recommends blood transfusion indications for ICU patients
Low-risk patients should maintain Hb 70-90g/L
High-risk patients should maintain Hb 100-120g/L

<!-- Slide number: 46 -->
# Indications for Blood Transfusion
Ministry of Health Blood Transfusion Guidelines (2000)
Hb>100g/L    No need to consider blood transfusion
Hb <70g/L    Consider transfusing concentrated red blood cells
Hb 70-100g/L  Based on the patient's compensatory capacity, general condition, and other organic lesions
Acute blood loss greater than 30% of blood volume can be transfused with whole blood

<!-- Slide number: 47 -->
# Third Space Loss
Primarily due to tissue edema or transcellular fluid shift, third space loss cannot participate in maintaining blood volume
Third space loss is related to the surgical site and method
For minor surgery: 2~3ml/kg/h
For moderate surgery: 4~6ml/kg/h
For surgery with larger exposed wound surfaces: 7~10ml/kg/h

Third space loss

# Example
A 70kg male patient undergoing a subtotal gastrectomy, preoperative Hb 13g/L, fasting for 10 hours, bleeding approximately 150ml in the first and second hours, and 50ml in the third hour, with a surgery duration of 4 hours. Please formulate an intraoperative fluid administration plan

<!-- Slide number: 50 -->
#
No additional loss before surgery
Hourly requirement: 4-2-1 rule 4×10+2×10+1×50=110ml/h
Fasting loss: 110×10=1100ml

Half of the above fluid volume is infused in the first hour of surgery, and the remaining volume is infused in the subsequent 2~3 hours

#
Compensatory Fluid Administration: Generally 5~7ml/kg, calculated as 7ml/kg in this case = 7×70=490ml
The first hour of fluid administration, starting 15~20 minutes before anesthesia induction, includes CVE 490ml, cumulative deficit 1100÷2=550ml, and physiological needs 110ml, totaling 1150ml
The remaining cumulative deficit of 550ml is administered over the second and third hours
110ml of physiological needs are supplemented per hour

#
5. Intraoperative Blood Loss: Blood loss of 150, 150, and 50ml in the first, second, and third hours, respectively, with balanced fluids supplemented in a ratio of 3~4:1 and colloidal fluids in a 1:1 ratio
6. Third Space: Partial gastrectomy is a moderate surgery, requiring 4~6ml/kg/h, with 6ml calculated as 6×70=420ml per hour. The third space loss decreases at the 4th hour when the abdomen is closed, and is estimated at 200ml

#
Total fluid requirements for this patient
Compensatory expansion = 490ml
Physiological needs = 4×110=440ml
Cumulative deficit = 110×10=1100ml
Continued deficit = 350×4=1400 ml or colloidal 500ml
Third space deficit = 420×3+200=1460ml
Compensatory expansion for the disappearance of anesthetic effect needs to be eliminated, and the third space fluid will gradually enter the bloodstream postoperatively

#
# Intraoperative Fluid Administration: Two Steps
First Step: Expansion
Firstly, supplement the preoperative cumulative deficit and CVE caused by anesthesia

Second Step: Maintenance
Continue deficit + physiological needs + third space loss

#
# What to Administer Intraoperatively?
Indications for blood transfusion are uncontested
Special fluids for special purposes: such as 5% NaHCO3-, hypertonic saline, etc.
The key controversy is whether to administer crystalloids or colloids
The ratio of crystalloids to colloids is 2:1, 1:1, or other, which should be determined based on specific circumstances

#
# Factors Affecting Fluid Selection
Comparison of Crystalloids and Colloids
|  | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crystalloids | Supplements interstitial fluid | Poor expansion effect, short duration |
|  | Increases urine output | Short duration of expansion maintenance |
|  | Low cost | Tissue and cell edema |
| Colloids | Good expansion effect | Affects coagulation function, allergic reactions |
|  | Long-term expansion maintenance | Reduces glomerular filtration | Not easy to cause tissue edema | High cost |

#
# Factors Affecting Fluid Selection
Crystalloids can achieve the same expansion effect as colloids with sufficient volume
The volume of crystalloids required for volume replacement is three times that of colloids
Most surgical patients have a deficit of extracellular fluid exceeding that of intravascular fluid deficit
Colloids can quickly correct severe hypovolemia
Rapid administration of a large volume of crystalloids (>4~5L) often causes significant tissue edema

#
# Indicators of Appropriate Fluid Administration
Hemodynamic stability: HR, BP, CVP
Oxygen supply and demand balance: DO2=Q×CaO2×10
                         DO2≥600ml/min/m2
Good visceral perfusion: urine output >50ml/h
                             pHi>7.32
Other: respiratory system, clinical symptoms, signs, etc.
