# Mechanism of Shivering
1. The exact mechanism of shivering is not yet clear, and it may involve the coordinated regulation of the nervous, endocrine, and motor systems in the process of shivering occurrence and development.
2. Shivering, through the rapid rhythmic contraction of skeletal muscles, produces heat, which is the body's compensatory response to hypothermia.
3. Redistribution of body heat is an important factor causing shivering.

# Influence on the Thermoregulatory Set Point
In 1963, Feldberg and Myers first discovered that the balance of norepinephrine and 5-HT in the preoptic area of the hypothalamus determines the level of the thermoregulatory set point. Studies have shown that injecting adrenaline and other amine neurotransmitters into the brain ventricles of cats can produce corresponding thermoregulatory effects. For example, injecting 5-HT can cause shivering, vasoconstriction, and simultaneously increase the body's deep temperature; conversely, injecting norepinephrine and epinephrine can lower body temperature and counteract the thermoregulatory effect of 5-HT; injecting dopamine at a dose similar to that of norepinephrine into the hypothalamus of awake monkeys can produce a similar effect to norepinephrine, but with a relatively lighter degree of cooling.

# Hazards of Shivering
1) The body's metabolic rate significantly increases, oxygen consumption increases, and the production of carbon dioxide and lactic acid increases. The body compensates by strengthening respiration and cardiac work, thereby increasing the workload on the heart and lungs, which can lead to cardiovascular failure in critically ill patients. Wound rupture.
2) Dynamic electrocardiogram evidence shows that the proportion of myocardial ischemia in patients with a body temperature below 35°C is significantly higher than that in patients with normal body temperature.
3) Under hypothermia, shivering can damage platelet function, inhibit the coagulation cascade reaction, and significantly increase blood loss, resulting in increased blood transfusion.

# Causes of Shivering After Lumbar Puncture
The incidence of shivering after lumbar puncture is as high as 60%. It is even higher in cesarean sections. The possible mechanisms are: 1. The body's response to cold is weakened, 2. Due to the blockage of motor nerves, muscle movement and tension are reduced, which reduces heat production, 3. The peripheral cold sensation signal is blocked after the blockage, so the central nervous system does not instruct an increase in peripheral temperature, etc., leading to reduced heat production and further decrease in central core temperature, causing shivering.

# Causes of Shivering Due to General Anesthesia
Patients who recover from general anesthesia with hypothermia often experience shivering. The mechanism may be that anesthetics suppress the thermoregulatory system, lowering the threshold for shivering. During the disappearance of the anesthetic effect, the threshold for shivering returns to normal, creating a difference between the normal thermoregulatory thresholds, thus causing shivering. However, a phenomenon worth further study is that in severe hypothermic states, shivering often does not occur, while in patients with body temperatures close to normal, shivering often occurs.

# Other Inducing Factors
Research has shown that 50% of patients have a deep body temperature of 38.4°C, so it is believed that the stimulation of surgery can lead to an upward shift in the thermoregulatory set point, causing shivering and an increase in body temperature. In addition, infection, atelectasis (hypoxic shivering), heat-producing substances released from the wound, surface disinfection, exposure of the surgical field, heat loss exceeding metabolic heat production, and the administration of large amounts of hypothermic fluids during surgery, low room temperature, and patient-related factors can all lead to the occurrence of shivering.

# Prevention and Treatment

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
# Preventive Measures
1. Preoperative Psychological Nursing - On the day before surgery, visit the ward to understand the patient's condition and psychological state, and provide necessary explanations, including the reliability and methods of epidural anesthesia.
2. Adjust the temperature and humidity of the operating room to reduce heat loss by the body - During the autumn, winter, and early spring seasons when the temperature is low, maintaining body temperature before and during surgery is very important. Therefore, before the patient enters the operating room, the room temperature should be raised to about 25°C in advance, and the patient should be covered with a blanket before anesthesia, and the doctor should be reminded to minimize the time for skin disinfection. It should be noted that if conditions permit, the humidity in the room should be controlled between 40% and 60% to reduce the patient's evaporative heat loss during surgery. Additionally, it should not be determined based on self-perceived comfort, as the patient has little coverage during surgery and the body cavity is exposed, resulting in greater heat loss than in ordinary situations.
3. Administration of Preheated Fluids - The fluids administered during surgery and the fluids used for cavity irrigation should be preheated to avoid unnecessary heat exchange and subsequent heat loss. Our practice is to heat the fluids in a water bath at 40°C in advance and then use them for the patient. The blood banked during surgery can be preheated by personnel on the floor.
4. Preoperative and Intraoperative Medication - Preoperative sedative-hypnotic drugs should be administered according to the specific condition of the patient. During surgery, cooperate with the anesthesiologist to administer combined medication for hibernation and analgesia, such as pethidine and diazepam.
5. Promptly Handle the Pyrogenic Reactions Induced by Blood Transfusion or Intravenous Infusion - Such reactions are accompanied by symptoms such as shivering and rash, so careful observation and differentiation are required, and timely antiallergic treatment should be administered.
6. Oxygen Administration - If the patient's body temperature is lowered to 34-30°C due to low room temperature or other reasons, the body maintains body temperature by increasing heat production and reducing heat loss, thus causing shivering. At this time, blood glucose levels rise, and heart rate and rhythm also change accordingly, oxygen utilization decreases, and if the oxygen concentration inhaled is insufficient, it is easy to develop hypoxemia. To prevent this reaction, continuous oxygen administration via a face mask should be maintained during surgery.

# Commonly Used Surgical Treatment Drugs for Shivering (1)
Research has shown that monoamine substances, anticholinergic agents, cations, endogenous peptide substances, and NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptor antagonists, as well as stimulants, can all act on the thermoregulatory system and participate in thermoregulation. These substances are now all used in the clinical treatment of shivering and have achieved varying degrees of efficacy.

# Experimental Data
1. Nishimura et al. indicate that post-anesthesia chills are related to peripheral body surface temperature reduction and the incidence of chills after anesthesia in young patients is higher than that in elderly patients.
2. Crossley observed 2595 patients in the observation room and found that the incidence of chills in male patients was significantly higher than that in female patients; young and middle-aged patients were higher than children and elderly patients. The occurrence of chills is not related to patient height and weight.
3. Goold reported that the incidence of chills was higher in patients who received preoperative analgesics than in those who did not.
4. Sessler et al. conducted skin heating or non-heating measures on patients with epidural anesthesia, and the incidence of chills in the non-heating group was higher, with a significant decrease in tympanic temperature.
5. Sharkey observed 30 patients with chills using [radiation heat therapy], 22 cases of chills disappeared, and 4 cases of chills were reduced.
6. Joris et al. selected 40 patients with chills, and there was no difference in the control effect of the saline group or the clonidine 37.5μg group. However, the clonidine 75μg group had a 100% disappearance rate of chills within 4 minutes after administration; the clonidine 150μg group had all chills disappear within 2 minutes after administration. This indicates that the use of clonidine in the treatment of chills is effective and correlates with the dose.
