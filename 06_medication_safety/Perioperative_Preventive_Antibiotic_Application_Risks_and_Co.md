# Principles for Preventive Antibiotic Use
1. The timing of administration is crucial, and it should be started 30 minutes before incision (at the time of anesthesia induction) to ensure that the drug has reached an effective concentration (>MIC 90) in the serum and tissues before bacterial contamination occurs.
2. It should be administered intravenously and completed within 30 minutes, and should not be slowly infused into a large bottle of liquid, otherwise the effective concentration cannot be achieved.
3. The effective concentration of antibiotics in the serum and tissues must be able to cover the entire surgical process. The serum half-life of commonly used cephalosporins is 1-2 hours, therefore, if the surgery is extended to more than 3 hours or the blood loss exceeds 1500ml, an additional dose should be administered.
4. Generally, short-term use should be adopted, and no further use is necessary after elective surgery is completed. If the patient has obvious high-risk factors for infection, or applies artificial implants, or there is preoperative bacterial contamination (such as open trauma), one or more doses can be administered up to 24 hours.
5. If bacterial infection is found during surgery, the drug should be continued after surgery until the infection is eliminated.

# Selection of Preventive Antibiotics

① Choose relatively broad-spectrum, effective (bactericidal), covering most pathogenic bacteria, safe, and inexpensive drugs
② Cephalosporins are recommended as the first choice
③ For cardiovascular, head and neck, thoracic and abdominal wall, and limb surgery—first-generation cephalosporins are recommended
④ For gastrointestinal, respiratory, and reproductive surgery—most use second-generation cephalosporins, and a few use third-generation cephalosporins
⑤ Aminoglycosides have ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity, and attention should be paid to selection
⑥ Quinolone drugs are generally not used (can be used for urinary system surgery)

# Perioperative Antibiotic Allergic Reactions
Incidence: 1:10,000-1:20,000, with an increased incidence of allergic reactions in patients under anesthesia.
A study found that among approximately 70,000 anesthetic patients over 2 years, by detecting histamine and plasminogen levels in the blood of patients who experienced an allergic reaction during anesthesia and performing skin tests 4-6 weeks later, the incidence of allergic reactions during anesthesia reached 1:4,667-1:3,080.
The conclusion suggests that the probability of IgE-mediated allergic reactions in patients during anesthesia increases by 50%.
Severe allergic reactions account for 3% of anesthetic-related deaths.
Drugs that can cause allergic reactions during anesthesia include muscle relaxants, latex, and antibiotics.
Penicillin and cephalosporins account for more than 70% of severe allergic reactions caused by perioperative antibiotics.

www.themegallery.com,

# Pathogenesis of Perioperative Allergic Reactions
Antigens mediated by IgE act on the body's mast cells and basophils.

Cell degranulation changes, rapidly releasing large amounts of histamine, leading to vasodilation of systemic blood vessels, increased vascular permeability, hypotension, angioedema, bronchospasm, itching, and increased mucus secretion.

# Clinical Manifestations
Onset rapid, symptoms appear within seconds to minutes after contact with the allergen.
The earlier the allergic symptoms appear, the more severe the condition.
Respiratory obstruction symptoms:
    Due to laryngeal edema, bronchospasm, and pulmonary edema.
    Clinical manifestations: chest tightness, shortness of breath, asthma, dyspnea.
Circulatory failure symptoms:
    Due to peripheral vascular dilation leading to insufficient effective circulating blood volume.
    Manifested as: pale complexion, cold sweat, cyanosis, weak pulse, and decreased blood pressure.
Central nervous system symptoms: due to hypoxia of the brain tissue.
    Manifested as: dizziness, blurred vision, numbness of the face and limbs, restlessness, loss of consciousness, convulsions, or incontinence of urine and feces.
Other allergic reactions:
    Urticaria, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and fever, etc.

# Principles of Management for Perioperative Allergic Reactions
Immediately stop the suspected antigen, reduce anesthesia; call for help, stop surgical procedures
Lay the patient flat, assess breathing, blood pressure, heart rate, and consciousness,
Immediately inject epinephrine,
Volumize, fluid options include 500ml of low molecular weight dextran, Ringer's solution, etc., elevate the lower limbs;
Ensure airway patency, if there is respiratory distress due to angioedema, intubate immediately, use 100% pure oxygen ventilation; tracheal intubation requires a catheter diameter more than twice smaller than the normal catheter to avoid causing damage to the narrow airway
In the event of cardiac arrest, perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation: hypotension (systolic blood pressure measured by iBP in the range of 30-40mmHg), unclear arterial pressure waveform, unclear SpO2 waveform, and faint pulse in the great arteries indicate ineffective heartbeat and immediate CPR
According to medical advice, intravenously inject dexamethasone 5-10mg or sodium succinate hydrocortisone 200-400mg added to 500ml of 5% to 10% glucose solution for intravenous infusion
Antihistamines:
  H1 receptor antagonist: diphenhydramine 20-40mg (or promethazine 50mg or chlorpheniramine 10mg) intramuscular injection
  H2 receptor antagonist: ranitidine, famotidine
  Effective for shock that cannot be controlled by epinephrine, corticosteroids, fluid resuscitation, and H1 blockers. Cimetidine prolongs the metabolism of beta-blockers, which may prolong the allergic state in patients using beta-blockers, and non-cimetidine H2 antihistamines should be used to treat hypersensitivity
9. Relief of significant bronchospasm: aminophylline 0.25-0.5g diluted in 5%-10% glucose injection for intravenous infusion.
10. Correct acidosis, vasoactive drugs, and symptomatic treatment with dopamine 10-20µg/kg-1.min-1

# Assessment
Rapid clinical assessment:
First, determine if the patient has or is suspected of having an allergen.
Second, determine if the patient has acute respiratory obstruction symptoms (such as asphyxia, asthma, tracheal spasm, wheezing).
Third, determine if the patient has hypotension and symptoms of hypovolemia (such as dizziness, chest tightness, palpitations, sweating, pale complexion, cyanosis of the lips).
Fourth, determine if the patient has skin/mucous membrane symptoms (wheals, generalized itching).
Fifth, also determine if the patient has altered consciousness, mild allergic shock patients may have confusion.
Severe cases may present with coma or gastrointestinal symptoms (such as spastic abdominal pain, vomiting).

# Neuro muscular blockade of antibiotics
1. Certain antibiotics have muscle relaxing effects
The most concerning side effect of antibiotics for anesthesiologists is their effect on the neuromuscular system
Common antibiotics that cause muscle relaxation include:
    Aminoglycosides
      Clindamycin and Lincomycin
      Tetracyclines
      Polymyxins

www.themegallery.com,

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
#
2. Causes
The mechanism of muscle relaxation caused by antibiotics is not only their own neuromuscular relaxation effect, but also the enhancement effect on certain anesthetics such as depolarizing muscle relaxants, inhaled anesthetics
The main mechanism of muscle relaxation caused by antibiotics is related to their action on the neuromuscular junction, however, the mechanisms of different types of antibiotics are different, and even the specific mechanisms of the same type of antibiotics are quite different.

#
Neuromuscular blockade can cause myocardial depression, hypotension, limb paralysis, and respiratory failure, which is related to dose and route of administration, common after large-dose intraperitoneal or intrapleural administration, occasionally after intramuscular or intravenous injection. The incidence rate is in the order of Tobramycin > Gentamicin > Amikacin, Kanamycin > Streptomycin > Neomycin > Netilmicin. The cause may be the chelation of the drug with calcium ions, or the competition with calcium ions, inhibiting the release of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular endplate and reducing the sensitivity of the postsynaptic membrane to acetylcholine, causing blockage at the neuromuscular junction. It is prone to occur in patients with renal insufficiency, low blood calcium, concurrent use of muscle relaxants, and general anesthesia, especially in patients with myasthenia gravis. Glucose-galactose and neostigmine can reverse this blockage. It is not recommended to administer aminoglycoside antibiotics intravenously to avoid sudden increase in blood drug concentration, leading to respiratory arrest and death.
