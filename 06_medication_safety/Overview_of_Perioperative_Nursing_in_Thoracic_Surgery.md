# Overview of Perioperative Nursing in Thoracic Surgery
Department of Thoracic Surgery, Jin Zhengnian

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->
#
Diseases in thoracic surgery include lung and pleural diseases, esophageal and cardia diseases, mediastinal diseases, and various chest injuries, among which esophageal and cardia malignant tumors, lung cancer, and bullae are common. Most surgical patients require surgical treatment to achieve cure, and good perioperative nursing is the focus of surgical nursing work.

#
Before surgery, it is necessary to assess and correct physiological and psychological problems that may increase the risk of surgery; provide patients and their families with information about the purpose and precautions of the surgery; demonstrate postoperative exercises beneficial to patients; and develop a plan for adjusting lifestyle changes.

After surgery, it is necessary to maintain the physiological function of each system; alleviate pain and discomfort; prevent postoperative complications; provide health guidance; and develop and implement a discharge plan. This ensures patients smoothly transition through the surgical period.

# I. Preoperative Nursing
1. Psychological Nursing
Surgical patients not only have to bear the pain caused by the disease but also the pressure brought by "surgery."
They often feel anxious, tense, fearful, and insecure about issues such as anesthesia, surgery, pain, body damage, disease prognosis, and the economic, family, and employment responsibilities brought by the disease. They may even become negative and pessimistic. Due to a lack of medical knowledge, negative thoughts often dominate.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# 1 Psychological Nursing
Extensive research both domestically and internationally has shown that surgical patients experience varying degrees of psychological stress responses during the perioperative period, which manifest differently before, during, and after surgery, and most are extremely severe.
From a physiological and psychological perspective, the larger the surgery, the greater the functional damage, and the stronger the level of anxiety.
Thoracic surgery is one of the most significant surgeries, so patients in this category commonly experience strong psychological reactions during the perioperative period, with excessive anxiety and fear, even leading to dysregulation of the nervous system, changes in personality and behavior, such as tension, insomnia, irritability, and restlessness.

# 1 Psychological Nursing
Therefore, nurses should have a high sense of responsibility and a good service attitude, be patient in explaining the situation, help patients prepare psychologically, overcome negative psychological reactions, and correctly face the surgery they are about to undergo.
Research has shown that psychological preparation for patients based on their psychological characteristics before surgery can effectively reduce their stress responses. Patients who have undergone psychological preparation experience a reduction in the intensity of anxiety, fear, depression, and pain, a decrease in postoperative complications, and an improvement in recovery.

# 2. Smoking Cessation
   Hazards of Smoking
1  Cigarettes are one of the causes of cancer. For those who smoke more than 40 cigarettes a day, the incidence of squamous cell lung cancer and small cell lung cancer is 4-10 times higher than that of non-smokers.
2  Smoking stimulates the gradual squamous metaplasia of the ciliated columnar epithelium, making respiratory secretions difficult to expel, and reducing the resistance of the respiratory tract.
3  Smoking can also increase postoperative respiratory tract infections, and the incidence of postoperative complications in heavy smokers is up to 2-3 times higher than that of non-smokers, which can be severe enough to cause respiratory failure.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# 2. Smoking Cessation
Smoking Cessation
It is reported that stopping smoking for 48 hours before surgery can reduce CO-HB content and improve oxygen supply, and stopping smoking for more than 2 weeks can improve the ability to clear secretions.
Therefore, explain to patients with a smoking history the hazards of smoking to health and surgery, advise and supervise them to strictly quit smoking upon admission, and ensure they stop smoking at least 1-2 weeks before surgery.

# 4. Improving Overall Nutrition
Some thoracic surgery patients may have difficulty eating, excessive nutritional consumption, or gastrointestinal dysfunction, preventing them from obtaining the necessary nutrients, electrolytes, and fluids through the gastrointestinal tract. Some patients have anemia and hypoproteinemia.
These patients have poor tolerance to surgery, are prone to postoperative complications, and can affect the recovery of the body. Correction is necessary.
Before surgery, guide patients to eat high-calorie, high-protein, high-vitamin, and easily digestible diets; for those with poor appetite and difficulty eating, provide intravenous nutritional supplementation, inputting whole blood or plasma, albumin, fat emulsion, etc., and correct water and electrolyte imbalances.

# 5. Controlling Infection
Increased respiratory tract secretions can affect pulmonary ventilation and exchange function, and severe cases can cause respiratory insufficiency, tissue hypoxia, and damage to the heart and other organs.

Patients should pay attention to keeping warm before surgery and avoid catching a cold. If patients have respiratory tract infections or other complications before surgery, they should be actively treated, and antibiotics should be used according to the drug sensitivity test and in a standardized manner. Oral expectorants should be taken. For those with a lot of sputum, nebulization inhalation should be given 2-4 times a day to dilute sputum or reduce respiratory tract secretions and promote expectoration.

# 6. Attention to Oral Hygiene
      Research data suggest that the direct cause of postoperative pneumonia after thoracotomy is the increased colonization of respiratory tract bacteria, most of which come from the resident bacteria in the oropharynx. Therefore, it is necessary to maintain oral cleanliness before surgery and actively treat chronic oral lesions.
      Starting 3 days before surgery, brush your teeth first and then rinse with 100 ml of 0.9% saline and 80,000 U of gentamicin. You can also choose an oral cleaning solution based on the oral pH value.
      Good oral care before surgery can effectively reduce the incidence of postoperative pneumonia, esophageal anastomotic fistula, and other complications.

# II. Postoperative Nursing

          Thoracic surgery has a large trauma, affecting respiratory and circulatory function. Postoperative close observation of the condition is necessary, timely handling of discovered problems, otherwise serious complications can occur, affecting the surgical outcome, and in severe cases, it can threaten life.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
1. Position
After surgery, the patient should be sent back to the ward only when the condition is stable. The patient should be moved gently, especially for patients who have undergone total lung resection, to avoid affecting mediastinal displacement. In the unconscious state, a supine position should be maintained, and then changed to a semi-recumbent position after becoming conscious and the vital signs are stable, which is conducive to respiratory movement and chest drainage.

<!-- Slide number: 30 -->
# 2. Oxygen Inhalation
After thoracic surgery, there is varying degrees of hypoxia. Oxygen should be inhaled through nasal cannula or face mask upon returning to the ward, and the oxygen flow rate should be adjusted according to the blood oxygen saturation, generally 4-6 liters/minute. When the respiration is smooth at 20 times/minute, the oxygen flow rate can be gradually reduced to 2-4 liters/minute.
A high oxygen flow rate can make the respiratory tract dry, hinder the clearance of sputum, and reduce the function of pulmonary macrophages, thereby increasing the risk of infectious pulmonary complications.

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
# 3. Cardiac Monitoring
Strengthen postoperative monitoring for 2-4 days. The purpose of monitoring includes understanding the changes in the patient's cardiovascular and pulmonary function and correctly evaluating the effects of various treatments.
Upon returning to the ward, blood pressure, pulse, respiration, and oxygen saturation should be closely observed, and monitoring records should be made. It is also necessary to monitor the heart rhythm, as arrhythmias often occur after thoracotomy, and early detection, diagnosis, and treatment should be achieved.

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
# 4. Maintaining Airway Patency
Respiratory care is a key aspect of postoperative nursing.
          After thoracic surgery, due to incision pain, the influence of anaesthetic drugs, the stimulation of tracheal intubation, and the patient's reluctance to cough, sputum can block the respiratory tract, affecting ventilation and gas exchange, causing chest tightness and dyspnea.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
# 4. Maintaining Airway Patency
4.1 Before becoming conscious, the patient should be in a supine position with the head turned to one side. After the condition is stable, change to a semi-recumbent position to facilitate breathing and expectoration.
4.2 Encourage the patient to actively cough and expectorate sputum and perform deep breathing as soon as they become conscious. Nebulization inhalation, 2-4 times/day, can dilute sputum and make it easier to cough out.
          Guaifenesin, a mucus-thinning agent recently launched on the market, has been shown to stimulate the secretion of pulmonary surfactant substances, repair damaged airway mucosa, reduce mucosal oedema, and reduce the stickiness of sputum, increase the amount of expectoration, and alleviate the patient's subjective feeling of difficulty in expectoration.

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
# 4. Maintaining Airway Patency
4.3 After the vital signs are stable, assist the patient in intermittently sitting up and perform physical therapy by patting the back to vibrate sputum, making it easier to cough out. It is also possible to stimulate coughing and expectoration by pressing on the upper part of the sternum.
4.4 If necessary, perform nasal catheter tracheal intubation, fiberoptic bronchoscope aspiration, or tracheotomy aspiration.
4.5. Closely observe the position of the trachea. If tracheal displacement or difficulty breathing is found in the patient, report to the doctor immediately.

# 5. Drainage Tube Care
5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
Following thoracotomy surgery, the original negative pressure in the pleural cavity is lost, and there is usually a certain degree of pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and hemorrhage, as well as possible alveolar air leakage. Therefore, a chest drainage tube connected to a water-seal bottle, i.e., closed chest drainage, is routinely placed postoperatively.
Closed chest drainage can exclude air and fluid in the pleural cavity, restore and maintain negative pressure in the pleural cavity, maintain the normal position of the mediastinum, promote lung expansion, eliminate dead space, and prevent infection.
Therefore, strengthening the observation and care of closed chest drainage postoperatively plays a very important role in maintaining normal respiratory and circulatory function, preventing postoperative complications, and promoting the patient's early recovery.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
1) Properly secure the drainage device and maintain the patency of the drainage tube
Correctly connect the drainage device, ensuring it is securely fastened and not allowing any air leakage.
Maintain the patency of the drainage tube by squeezing it every 30 minutes to prevent the orifice from being blocked by blood clots. If the patient develops active internal hemorrhage, the drainage tube should be continuously squeezed.
Squeezing can cause pain, so instruct the patient to take deep breaths during squeezing to alleviate pain.

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
2) Enhance drainage
After recovery from general anesthesia, once blood pressure and pulse are stable, assume a semi-recumbent position to allow pleural effusion to flow downward, which is beneficial for drainage.
During the postoperative period of bed rest, bed activities should be performed and positions changed frequently. Early postoperative activity not only prevents postoperative complications and promotes recovery but also facilitates drainage and early tube removal, reducing pain.
The incision pain after chest surgery is generally severe, and effective analgesia should be provided postoperatively, and patients should be encouraged to cough and expectorate sputum and perform deep breathing exercises to re-expand the lungs. Lung re-expansion is beneficial for the excretion of air and fluid in the pleural cavity.

<!-- Slide number: 38 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
3) Accurately observe and record the amount and color changes of pleural drainage
Under normal circumstances, the drainage volume should be less than 80ml/h, starting as bloody and gradually turning to light red, not easily clotting. Approximately 500ml of pleural fluid leakage on the first postoperative day is within the normal range.
If the drainage volume is high, with blood-stained effusion greater than 100ml/h, and there is no decrease in the trend after continuous observation for 5 hours, or although a large amount of blood transfusion has been given, the signs of shock are not significantly improved, or it is estimated that there is a large amount of blood in the pleural cavity, it should be considered as active bleeding in the pleural cavity and immediate thoracotomy for hemostasis is required.
At this time, the nurse should provide bedside care for the patient, frequently squeezing the drainage tube to prevent blockage.

<!-- Slide number: 39 -->
# 3) Accurately observe and record the amount and color changes of pleural drainage

         If the thoracic duct or its branches are damaged during thoracotomy surgery, and chyle leaks into the pleural cavity, chylothorax occurs, with an incubation period of several days to several weeks. If the patient's pleural drainage volume is abnormally increased, it is milky chyle without odor, not easily clotting, and accompanied by symptoms such as varying degrees of dyspnea, palpitations, chest tightness, chest pain, tachycardia, and low blood pressure, chylothorax should be considered.

<!-- Slide number: 40 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage

4) Observe the intrapleural negative pressure and water column fluctuation
The normal intrapleural pressure is negative, which not only maintains lung expansion and ventilation function but also promotes venous centripetal return and maintains the normal position of the mediastinum.

The water-seal bottle should be at least 60cm above the bed, and the water-seal bottle should not be too small, and the glass tube should not be submerged too shallowly in water to prevent the liquid in the water-seal bottle from being inhaled into the pleural cavity.

The water column fluctuation in the glass tube of the water-seal bottle should be observed frequently. If the water column fluctuation disappears, the breath sound on the affected side is weakened, or subcutaneous emphysema appears, check whether the drainage tube is patent and handle it promptly; if the water column fluctuation is too great, it indicates the presence of residual cavity or atelectasis, and respiratory tract management should be strengthened.

<!-- Slide number: 41 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
5) Observe for air leakage
After thoracotomy surgery, there may be residual gas in the pleural cavity, and due to the leakage of the lung segment surface caused by the resection of lung segments or lobes, gas may溢出 from the drainage tube, especially after coughing and deep breathing. Generally, it can heal spontaneously.
If the continuous discharge of bubbles from the drainage tube is not stopped, it may be due to the surgery itself or a leak in the closed chest drainage device, and immediate inspection and handling should be performed. For patients with severe air leakage, do not encourage active coughing and expectoration of sputum, just allow the sputum to be coughed out, to avoid prolonging the healing time of the lung segment surface and not being beneficial for early tube removal.

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
6) Care for Drainage Tubes After Pneumonectomy
After pneumonectomy, one drainage tube is placed to understand the position of the mediastinum and judge the intrathoracic pressure. The opening and closing of the drainage tube can be adjusted according to the situation, and the water column fluctuation pressure in the glass tube of the water-seal bottle should be adjusted to 5cm up and down.
The focus of care should be to observe the patient's respiratory status, whether there is subcutaneous emphysema, and whether the trachea is centrally located. The amount and time of opening should be determined based on the position of the trachea and the specific condition of the patient.

<!-- Slide number: 43 -->
# 6) Care for Drainage Tubes After Pneumonectomy

If a large amount of air and fluid is found in the pleural cavity, and the trachea shifts to the healthy side, report to the doctor and assist in opening the drainage tube. The drainage speed should be slow, and the amount of fluid drained at one time should not exceed 1000ml. Closely observe the patient's respiratory status to prevent sudden movement of the mediastinum due to large-volume fluid drainage, leading to cardiac arrest. At the same time, instruct the patient not to cough at this time to avoid causing mediastinal flutter. If the trachea shifts to the affected side, the clamping time should be appropriately extended, using the amount of pleural effusion to promote tracheal复位, avoiding over-expansion of the healthy lung, leading to pneumothorax and bullae, and avoiding sudden changes in the patient's position, causing mediastinal flutter, leading to arrhythmia and other complications.

<!-- Slide number: 44 -->
# 5.1 Observation and Care of Closed Chest Drainage
7) Care for Tube Removal

After surgery for 48-72 hours, if the pleural drainage volume is less than 100ml/day, the color is light serum-like, there is no gas exhalation from the drainage tube, the water column fluctuation in the water-seal bottle is small or stationary, the breath sound is clear on auscultation, and the chest X-ray shows good lung re-expansion, the drainage tube can be removed.
Before removal, instruct the patient to take a deep breath and hold it to prevent the tip of the tube from damaging the lung or causing pain. Immediately cover the wound with sterile vaseline gauze and multiple layers of gauze, secure and bandage properly to prevent air from entering the pleural cavity. Within 24 hours after removal, observe the patient's respiratory status, local渗液, bleeding, air leakage, and subcutaneous emphysema.

# 5.2 Care for Gastrointestinal Decompression
1) Maintain effective gastrointestinal decompression: Securely fasten to prevent tube dislodgment. Maintain negative pressure of 8-10 kPa to reduce the tension at the anastomosis, ensure good blood circulation at the anastomosis, and promote healing of the anastomosis.
2) Observe and record the amount and color of the drainage fluid daily. If the drainage volume is greater than 500 ml/day, additional acid secretion inhibiting drugs should be used; if the drainage volume significantly decreases, consider gastric tube obstruction, and use normal saline for low-pressure irrigation; if the color is coffee-colored or dark red on the third postoperative day, consider gastrointestinal bleeding.
3) Maintain oral hygiene: Perform oral care BID, instruct the patient not to swallow saliva or saliva.
4) When oral medication is necessary, it should be crushed and mixed with water for injection, and the tube should be clamped for 30 minutes after administration to continue decompression.

# 5.3 Care of Duodenal Nutritional Tube
Esophageal cancer patients, due to swallowing difficulties and the consumption of the tumor itself, show varying degrees of malnutrition. Additionally, the large surgical trauma and prolonged fasting period after surgery exacerbate postoperative malnutrition. Malnutrition increases the risk of serious complications such as anastomotic fistula, incision dehiscence, and low immune function.

Domestic and international scholars, as well as clinical observations, report that enteral nutrition has a protective effect on the gastrointestinal mucosa, promotes gastrointestinal motility and the recovery of gastrointestinal digestion and absorption, and enhances the body's immune function.

<!-- Slide number: 47 -->
# 5.3 Care of Duodenal Nutritional Tube
During esophageal surgery, the nasogastric tube is inserted below the duodenum, and enteral nutrition is administered through the nasogastric tube from 1 to 7 days postoperatively.

Follow medical instructions to dilute nutritional solutions such as Resol and Enerjule with glucose saline before adding vitamins and potassium chloride, and prepare the infusion solution. Use an infusion warmer to bring the temperature of the nutritional solution to 38-40°C, starting at a rate of 20-30ml/min, and gradually increase according to the patient's sensation and tolerance. Before and after each infusion, flush the tube with 20ml warm normal saline. Flush the tube every 4 hours during the infusion process.
During the infusion process, closely monitor the patient's condition for signs of bloating, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and the presence of anal defecation and flatus. Pay attention to the color and quantity of gastric juice, and if enteral nutrition is drained from the gastrointestinal decompression, notify the doctor immediately and handle it properly.

# 6. Effective Postoperative Analgesia
Patients who undergo thoracotomy have severe pain due to large incisions, the cutting of many muscle fibers, and the stimulation of intercostal nerves by the chest drainage tube postoperatively. This often leads to muscle tension, restricted breathing, shallow breathing, and an increased risk of complications such as atelectasis, pneumonia, hypoxemia, and hypercapnia.

Timely and effective postoperative analgesia to relieve restrictive breathing and coughing caused by pain is crucial for preventing postoperative complications.

<!-- Slide number: 50 -->
# 6. Effective Postoperative Analgesia
Currently, a patient-controlled analgesia pump is commonly used for continuous epidural analgesia after thoracotomy, with tramadol as the drug used. It acts on the central pain perception nervous system and partially on the morphine receptors in the brain or spinal cord. Clinical doses do not cause respiratory depression, do not reduce lung capacity and respiratory rate, and can improve tidal volume in patients with respiratory diseases.

Clinical observations show that the analgesia pump is effective for postoperative analgesia and has a long duration of action. For individuals with poor response, pethidine can be administered intramuscularly, but it has a lower analgesic intensity and a shorter duration of action.

# 7. Rational Diet
Provide specific dietary guidance to increase nutritional intake. For early stages after non-esophageal surgery, it is advisable to consume light, easily digestible fluids and semi-liquid foods, gradually increasing high-protein, high-calorie, and high-vitamin diets. At the same time, pay attention to consuming more roughage to maintain bowel regularity.

After esophageal surgery, general gastrointestinal decompression is for 4-6 days, and after the gastric tube is removed, experimental drinking is given, transitioning to fluid and semi-liquid diets, with small and frequent meals.

# 8. Early Activity and Functional Exercise
Early postoperative activity is beneficial for the recovery of the entire body's function, such as preventing pulmonary complications, promoting blood circulation, facilitating wound healing, preventing deep vein thrombosis of the lower limbs, and promoting the recovery of urinary function, etc.
Encourage patients to move on the bed and turn over regularly within 1-3 days postoperatively to prevent pressure sores. Early ambulation should be encouraged after the chest tube is removed to increase lung capacity and reduce pulmonary complications, but avoid overexertion. Patients with cardiovascular diseases should exercise caution to prevent accidents.
Encourage patients to perform lifting exercises for the shoulder joint and arm on the surgical side.

# 9. Observation and Care of Common Postoperative Complications
Complications after thoracotomy are numerous and severe, affecting the quality of life of patients and even posing a threat to life. In the nursing process, early detection and prevention can reduce the occurrence of complications.

Therefore, nurses are required not only to have specialized theoretical knowledge and skills but also to have a people-oriented nursing concept, be responsible and conscientious, closely observe changes in the patient's condition, provide sufficient diagnostic evidence for the doctor, and adopt appropriate nursing measures to promote the patient's smooth recovery.

<!-- Slide number: 54 -->
# 9. Observation and Care of Common Postoperative Complications
9.1 Postoperative Hemorrhage
Postoperative hemorrhage is often due to incomplete hemostasis during surgery, the detachment of vascular ligatures, large transfusion of blood products, and coagulation dysfunction. It generally occurs within 12 hours, with the patient complaining of thirst, palpitations, dyspnea, decreased blood pressure, increased heart rate, and pale complexion and lips.
Nurses should closely monitor the patient's vital signs and the nature, color, and volume of the drainage fluid to detect early and handle it in a timely manner.

<!-- Slide number: 55 -->
# 9. Observation and Care of Common Postoperative Complications
9.2 Empyema
Empyema is often caused by surgical contamination during surgery, and bronchopleural fistula and esophageal anastomotic fistula can also cause empyema. Patients may have high fever, chest pain, dyspnea, drowsiness, anorexia, and the pleural cavity can drain cloudy pleural effusion.
If found, perform closed chest drainage surgery promptly, use effective antibiotics, transfuse blood in small and frequent doses, and provide a high-protein, high-calorie diet.

<!-- Slide number: 56 -->
# 9. Observation and Care of Common Postoperative Complications
9.3 Atelectasis and Pulmonary Infection
Atelectasis or pulmonary infection may occur due to postoperative incision pain, fear of coughing, and the inability to effectively expel sputum from the bronchi, leading to bronchial obstruction and the inability to exhale air from the alveoli.
For such patients, it is important to guide them to perform deep breathing exercises and effective coughing to expel sputum early to prevent hypoxemia and ineffective coughing due to weakened thoracic breathing.
Maintain the correct position, reduce local incision tension, alleviate discomfort, and reduce the occurrence of atelectasis. Assist with turning and back percussion to expel sputum, causing cough reflexes in the terminal bronchi due to vibration, and coughing out sputum. Choose effective antibiotics and perform nebulization inhalation 2-4 times a day to dilute sputum.

<!-- Slide number: 57 -->
# 9. Observation and Care of Common Postoperative Complications
9.4 Arrhythmia
The incidence rate is 3.2%-20.8%, with atrial arrhythmia being the most common, occurring most frequently in the first week postoperatively.
Patients with preoperative cardiovascular and pulmonary dysfunction are more prone to arrhythmia.
Arrhythmia generally occurs within 48 hours postoperatively, so it is very important to strengthen monitoring within the first 48 hours postoperatively.
Provide psychological care to eliminate tension and anxiety that can lead to arrhythmia; strengthen nutrition before surgery to improve cardiac and pulmonary compensation capacity; provide patient-controlled analgesia pumps for pain relief after surgery; maintain continuous cardiac monitoring for 3-5 days, closely observe Sa02, pulse, blood pressure, and changes in the condition; provide sufficient oxygen and correct hypoxemia; keep the chest drainage tube unobstructed; and promptly resolve adverse triggers such as electrolyte and acid-base disturbances, hypermetabolism, and drainage tube stimulation when arrhythmia occurs.

# 9. Observation and Nursing of Common Postoperative Complications
9.5 Pulmonary Function Insufficiency
Respiratory insufficiency can occur in any patient after chest surgery, but it is more common in patients with poor pulmonary reserve after pneumonectomy or thoracoplasty, secondary to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or in patients with atelectasis and pneumonia.
Patients may experience severe dyspnea, cyanosis, restlessness, and even coma or convulsions; oxygen saturation below 90%, and arterial blood oxygen pressure decrease as shown by blood gas analysis. Artificial ventilation assistance is necessary.

# 9. Observation and Nursing of Common Postoperative Complications
9.6 Subcutaneous Emphysema
After pulmonary, tracheobronchial, and esophageal surgery, gas may escape into the pleural cavity and be drained out through the pleural drain.
If the drain is not patent or if a large amount of gas leaks out, the pleural gas may escape into the soft tissue of the incision, mostly accumulating subcutaneously. If there is too much subcutaneous gas, it can spread to the head and neck, abdominal wall, and scrotum.
Small amounts of subcutaneous emphysema can be absorbed. When subcutaneous emphysema occurs, it is necessary to maintain the patency of the drain, adjust the position of the drain, replace the drain, or place another drain at a higher intercostal space. Chest closed drainage connected to negative pressure aspiration helps to expel the gas.

Maintain a cheerful spirit and stable emotions.
Gradually increase activity levels to prevent respiratory tract infections. Maintain smooth defecation.
Strengthen nutrition, eat small and frequent meals. Consume more high-protein, high-calorie, high-vitamin, and easily digestible foods. For patients after esophageal surgery, they should remain semi-recumbent for 30 minutes after meals to prevent food reflux.
If coughing, expectoration, chest tightness, fever, difficulty in eating, wound infection, and other conditions occur, seek medical attention immediately.
Regular follow-up.
