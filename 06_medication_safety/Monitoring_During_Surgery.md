# Electrocardiogram
Rhythm monitoring
The relationship between P waves and QRS waves can be used for the diagnosis of arrhythmias; P waves are most clearly seen in lead II.

Monitoring of myocardial ischemia
When myocardial ischemia occurs, the ST segment and T wave change first. Monitoring V5 lead alone can detect 75% of myocardial ischemia, while monitoring II lead, V4, and V5 can detect 96% of myocardial ischemia. Lead II can observe anterior wall myocardial ischemia, while V5 can observe left wall myocardial ischemia.

### Notes:
Before introducing the arterial puncture needle, let's first look at the anatomical and physiological differences between arteries and veins: first, let's look at the most superficial blood vessel in the human body -- the vein, which has a large lumen, thin walls, poor elasticity, no spontaneous pulsation, slow blood flow, low pressure, close to the surface, easy to fix. Its course is to gradually converge from capillary veins into small veins and then form superior and inferior vena cava and finally enter the right atrium.

# Anatomical and Physiological Characteristics of Arteries
Small lumen, thick walls
High flow rate, high pressure
Deep location
Gradually branch into a network
Finally form capillaries connected to veins

### Notes:
After introducing veins, let's look at the characteristics of arterial blood vessels: arteries are important channels for supplying nutrients and oxygen to the distal organs of the human body. They have a small lumen, thick walls, high elasticity, spontaneous pulsation, fast blood flow, high pressure, deep location, difficult to fix. Its course is from the left ventricle of the heart, pumped out through the aorta, gradually branching into small arteries, and finally forming capillary arteries connected to veins.

# Arterial Structure
Vascular adventitia

Smooth muscle

### Notes:
The structure of arteries is clearly different from that of veins in that it has a layer of smooth muscle between the vascular intima and adventitia, which is innervated by autonomic nerves and has good contraction ability, which can push blood towards the tissue terminal. Therefore, the catheter in the artery will be compressed during arterial contraction. If the catheter is not hard enough or cannot recover in time, it will significantly affect the accuracy of pressure measurement and cause occlusion.

# Arterial Puncture Indications
Arterial Pressure Monitoring         Shock, trauma, surgery, respiratory system disorder
Blood Sample Collection             Respiratory metabolic disorder
Diagnostic Procedures               Injection of contrast medium for coronary angioplasty

### Notes:
Arterial puncture is primarily used for arterial pressure monitoring, blood sample collection, and diagnostic injections. Arterial pressure monitoring is commonly used during surgery and in patients with shock, trauma, and respiratory metabolic disorders; in cases of respiratory metabolic disorder, repeated arterial blood sampling is required; in addition, arterial diagnostic injections are mainly used to inject contrast medium into the blood for coronary ventricular angiography during cardiac interventional examinations and treatments.

# Selection of Puncture Sites

Dorsal Pedal Artery  Chosen only when other arteries cannot be inserted
Arterial Puncture
- 90% Radial Artery

### Notes:
The selection of puncture sites for arterial puncture mainly includes the following: Radial artery - It is superficial and easily palpable, and the ulnar artery provides good collateral circulation; Brachial artery - It is the optimal choice because it is thicker and deeper than the radial artery, easier to puncture, but difficult to secure; Femoral artery - Easy to secure but prone to hematoma formation; Dorsal Pedal artery - Chosen only when other arteries cannot be inserted.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
# Precautions
Collateral Blood Circulation   Ensure that the collateral blood supply to the tissue is not affected in case of arterial spasm or thrombosis
     Attention! When performing radial artery puncture, the Allen's test must be done to determine if the ulnar artery is patent and if the collateral circulation is effective. If it cannot compensate, the puncture should not be performed
Superficial                 Easily palpable, not easily movable, easy to puncture
Not Adjacent to Veins        Reduce the risk of accidental venous puncture

### Notes:
The precautions to be taken during arterial puncture include the following: 1. Choose a vessel with collateral circulation to ensure that collateral blood supply to the tissue is not affected in case of arterial spasm or thrombosis, 2. Choose a superficial vessel, the purpose of which is to be easily palpable, not easily movable, and easy to puncture, 3. Try to choose a location not adjacent to veins to reduce the risk of accidental venous puncture.

# Procedure Steps 1

Carefully select the puncture area and vessel, disinfect the puncture site. Remove the needle protective cap, visually inspect the arterial catheter sheath. Position the needle bevel and the red thumb switch (FloSwitchTM) upwards. Puncture the skin with the needle. The needle angle should be 30-45 degrees to the skin. Always hold the needle hub (not the catheter sheath) during the puncture process. After piercing the skin, insert the catheter into the artery, which can be done using the described direct puncture method or the arterial puncture method.

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# Procedure Steps 2

When the puncture needle reaches the posterior wall of the artery, hold the needle hub and partially withdraw the needle core while holding the side wings of the catheter sheath to maintain its fixed position.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Procedure Steps 3

Gently withdraw the catheter sheath until blood returns, indicating that the tip of the catheter sheath is in the arterial lumen.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
# Procedure Steps 4

Insert the catheter sheath into the artery while withdrawing the needle core. Once the needle core is completely withdrawn, push the red thumb switch forward (note: only when the needle core is completely removed can the thumb switch be pushed forward.), close the catheter sheath, and discard the needle in an appropriate container.
Note: Do not reinsert the needle core into the catheter sheath, as this may cut the catheter sheath.

<!-- Slide number: 30 -->
# Procedure Steps 5

Connect the extension tube or monitoring line. Open the thumb switch to perform the relevant operation. Withdraw blood to check for successful catheter placement and flush the line with normal saline.

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
# Procedure Steps 6 & 7

Secure the arterial catheter with sterile dressings. The arterial device is now ready for use.

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
# Procedure Steps 8

Operate the FloSwitchTM as shown. If the switch is pushed forward, a small ball inside compresses the catheter, placing it in the closed state. A "click" can be felt. If the switch is moved backward, the liquid passage is open.

# Cardiac Output
Thermistor Dilution Method
    This method requires the use of a pulmonary artery catheter. The classic method involves injecting 10 mL of cold saline (room temperature or below) or 5% glucose water into the central venous end of the pulmonary artery catheter over a period of more than 4 minutes. The thermistor placed at the tip of the pulmonary artery catheter's main channel will detect changes in temperature. The area under the temperature-time curve is inversely proportional to blood flow and directly proportional to cardiac output.
Dye Dilution Method
    This method requires a central venous catheter and an arterial channel. A fixed volume and concentration of non-toxic dye is injected into the central circulation, and then blood samples are taken from the arterial circulation to measure dye concentration. The area under the concentration-time curve is inversely proportional to cardiac output. The commonly used tracer is indocyanine green.

# End-tidal Carbon Dioxide Waveform

Due to the ingestion of gas, esophageal intubation may also show waveforms similar to tracheal intubation.

During malignant hyperthermia, the end-tidal carbon dioxide level rises rapidly, and the rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide is insensitive to overventilation.

Hypovolemia, low perfusion, embolism, airway obstruction, and system leakage will all lead to a decrease in end-tidal carbon dioxide.

Increased end-tidal carbon dioxide is common in: malignant hyperthermia; absorption of carbon dioxide during laparoscopic surgery; reperfusion of carbon dioxide after the artery clamped is released; when it is time to replace the carbon dioxide absorbent.

# Bispectral Index

Mechanism

   The patient's electroencephalogram (EEG) signal is transmitted to the converter, where the EEG signal is converted into a digital signal and analyzed.

   The BIS value is taken from the EEG monitoring of the first 15 to 30 seconds, ranging from 0 to 100.

# Bispectral Index
Indications

Used to assess the depth of general anesthesia and the level of sedation, which can reduce the occurrence of intraoperative awareness.
Can avoid the use of excessive anesthetic agents in patients with unstable hemodynamics.
In closed-loop anesthesia, dual spectrum analysis can directly guide the application of anesthetic agents.

<!-- Slide number: 71 -->
# Bispectral Index
Result Analysis

The BIS value during general anesthesia is 40 to 60.
The response of BIS to anesthetic agents increases with age.
Opioids do not affect the BIS value.
Patients with BIS values over 70 may awaken at any time.

<!-- Slide number: 72 -->
# Bispectral Index
Interfering Factors

Excessive muscle activity may cause the BIS value to increase.

Exogenous currents or mechanical interference may make the BIS value unreliable.

Different anesthetic agents have different effects on BIS values.
The response of BIS to nitrous oxide is not linear and may be unreliable.
Ketamine has little effect on BIS, and under effective therapeutic doses, BIS may not show a significant response.
During etomidate induction, increased muscle activity may result in an erroneously high BIS value.

# Volume Monitoring

Influence of spontaneous breathing on arterial blood pressure

During inspiration:
Venous return increases, right ventricular preload increases, afterload decreases, and right ventricular stroke volume increases
Intrathoracic pressure decreases, pulmonary vascular bed dilates
Left ventricular preload decreases, stroke volume decreases
Blood pressure decreases during inspiration
During expiration, the opposite occurs: blood pressure increases
This phenomenon is known as "paradoxical pulse"
