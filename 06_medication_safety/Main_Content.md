# What Infections Are Prevented by the Use of Antimicrobial Drugs in the Perioperative Period?
Preventive medication purpose: to prevent surgical site infection (SSI)

SSI: Infection occurring in the superficial or deep incision, organ, or cavity during the perioperative period (in some cases, after the perioperative period)
Guidelines for the Preventive Application of Antimicrobial Drugs in the Perioperative Period

Company Logo

### Notes:
Distinguish
        The concept of SSI is broader than "wound infection" because it includes infections in organs and cavities that were involved in the surgery, such as incision infection, brain abscess, peritonitis, etc. SSI accounts for about 15% of all hospital infections and 35% to 40% of hospital infections in surgical patients.
        It is narrower and more specific than the concept of "postoperative infection" because it does not include infections that occur at different times after surgery but are not directly related to the surgical procedure, such as pneumonia and urinary tract infection.

# SSI Diagnostic Criteria
Guidelines for the Preventive Application of Antimicrobial Drugs in the Perioperative Period

Superficial Incision Infection

Deep Incision Infection

Infection in Organs/Cavities
Infection occurring within 30 days after surgery (within 1 year after the placement of an artificial implant), involving only the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the incision, and meeting at least one of the following conditions:
1. Presence of purulent secretion in the superficial layer of the incision
2. Culturing bacteria from the superficial layer of the incision
3. Presence of one or more of the following symptoms and signs: pain or tenderness, swelling, redness, or the incision is opened by the physician due to these symptoms (if the culture is negative, it is not considered an infection)
   Suture abscess and puncture hole infections around the incision are not classified as surgical site infections
Infection occurring within 30 days after surgery (within 1 year after the placement of an artificial implant), involving the deep fascia and muscle layer of the incision:
1. Purulent discharge from the deep incision
2. The incision spontaneously opens or is actively opened by the physician: ① Body temperature > 38℃; ② Local pain or tenderness
3. Clinical or diagnostic findings by surgery, pathological histology, or imaging showing abscess in the deep incision
    If the infection involves both the superficial and deep incision, it should be classified as deep infection

Infection occurring within 30 days after surgery (within 1 year after the placement of an artificial implant), involving organs or cavities in the areas where surgery was performed, through surgical opening or other surgical procedures:
1. Presence of purulent drainage from the drainage tube placed in the organ/cavity
2. Culturing pathogenic bacteria from the fluid or tissue of the organ/cavity
3. Diagnosis of abscess in the organ/cavity by surgery, pathological histology, or imaging

Preoperative Preparation
Minimize the preoperative hospital stay to reduce the opportunity for hospital-acquired pathogens to colonize the patient;
Ensure thorough preoperative preparation to place the patient in the best possible condition,
No need to shave in areas with sparse hair, and in areas with dense hair, shave immediately in the operating room before the start of surgery.

### Notes:
Such as controlling diabetes, improving malnutrition, actively treating existing infections, etc.;

# Guidelines for the Preventive Use of Antimicrobial Agents in the Perioperative Period - Chinese Medical Association, Division of Surgery

1. For Class I (clean) incision surgery, strict aseptic techniques and meticulous surgical operations should be observed. Generally, preventive medication is not required, but it should be considered in the following situations:
   (a) Large surgical procedures with extended duration and increased risk of contamination;
   (b) Procedures involving vital organs, where infection would result in serious consequences, such as craniotomy, cardiac and great vessel surgery, splenectomy, etc.;
   (c) Surgery involving the use of artificial materials or devices, such as abdominal hernia repair with artificial materials, artificial valves, etc.;
   (d) Patients with high-risk factors for infection, such as elderly individuals, diabetes, malignant tumors, immunodeficiency or impairment, malnutrition, etc.

# Guidelines for the Preventive Use of Antimicrobial Agents in the Perioperative Period

### Notes:
Drug selection: Generally, first and second-generation cephalosporins are preferred, and it is advisable to avoid the use of broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents, aminoglycosides (aminoglycoside antibiotics have ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity, and are not ideal preventive agents), and quinolone drugs.
Principally, it should be selected based on efficacy, (bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic), safety, and relatively low cost. Cephalosporins are the most suitable for these conditions.

# Guidelines for the Prophylactic Application of Antimicrobial Drugs in the Perioperative Period

### Notes:
For cardiovascular, head and neck, chest and abdominal wall, extremity soft tissue, and orthopedic surgeries, the main infectious pathogens are Staphylococcus, and first-generation cephalosporins such as cefazolin and cefradine are generally preferred. For surgeries involving intraperitoneal and pelvic hollow organs, the main infectious pathogens are Gram-negative bacilli, and second-generation cephalosporins such as cefuroxime are often used. For complex surgeries that are prone to infection, third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone and cefotaxime can be used. For lower gastrointestinal surgeries, gynecological surgeries involving the vagina, and head and neck surgeries involving the oral pharyngeal mucosa, there is often contamination with anaerobic bacteria, and it is necessary to cover anaerobic bacteria. Generally, metronidazole, which targets anaerobic bacteria, is added on the basis of second and third-generation cephalosporins. For liver and biliary system surgeries, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone, or cefoperazone/sulbactam, or piperacillin can be selected, which can form high concentrations in liver, biliary tissue, and bile.

# Timing of Prophylactic Medication
1. Early administration is a shot in the dark; it should be started 30 min to 2h before the start of surgery to ensure that effective blood drug concentrations (> MIC 90) are reached in the serum and tissues before contamination occurs.
2. It should be administered intravenously and infused within 30 min.
3. The half-life of commonly used beta-lactam antimicrobial drugs is 1-2h; if the surgery exceeds 3h, the second dose should be administered, and if necessary, the third dose; no additional administration is required for antimicrobial drugs with a longer half-life (such as ceftriaxone).
4. Intramuscular and oral administration have individual differences in absorption and cannot guarantee blood and tissue drug concentrations, and should not be used.
5. Ensure sufficient antimicrobial drug concentrations throughout the entire surgical period.

# Guidelines for the Preventive Use of Antimicrobial Agents in the Perioperative Period
1. Generally, long-term use of antimicrobial agents is not required after elective surgery, and if used, should not exceed 24 hours.
2. A single dose before elective surgery is sufficient; continuous use for several doses or several days after surgery does not further improve the preventive effect.
3. If the patient has obvious high-risk factors for infection or is using an artificial implant, an additional dose or several doses up to 24 hours, or up to 48 hours in special cases, may be administered.
4. For patients with organ transplantation, medication should be continued for several days (3-5 days) after surgery.
5. For severe contamination, existing infection, or organ perforation (Class IV incision), antimicrobial agents should be continued for treatment purposes after surgery and not used for prevention.

### Notes:
Generally, short-term use should be employed, and antimicrobial agents should not be used after the elective surgery is completed. If the patient has obvious high-risk factors for infection, or is using an artificial implant, or if bacterial contamination has occurred before surgery (such as open trauma), an additional dose or several doses up to 24 hours, or up to 48 hours in special cases, may be administered. Continuous use for several days or even until the suture is removed is not necessary and cannot further reduce the incidence of surgical site infections. If bacterial infection is found during surgery, medication should be continued until the infection is resolved.

# Advantages of Short-Term Preventive Antimicrobial Use
1. Reduces toxic and side effects
2. Less likely to induce the development of drug-resistant strains
3. Less likely to cause dysbiosis
4. Reduces the patient's economic burden
5. Reduces workload
