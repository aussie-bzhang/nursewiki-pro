# Orthopaedic Surgery Perioperative Management

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->
Perioperative Period
     Refers to the period from the time surgery is determined to the time when the treatment related to this surgery is basically completed. The duration is approximately 5-7 days before surgery to 7-12 days after surgery.
Perioperative management refers to various measures taken with surgery as the center.
Great importance should be attached to perioperative management, which is of great significance for ensuring patient safety and improving treatment outcomes.

Preoperative Preparation
Preoperative preparation refers to taking appropriate measures based on the comprehensive preoperative examination results of the patient and the expected surgical method, in order to make the patient as mentally and physically prepared as possible, so as to tolerate the surgery more safely.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Types of Surgery
Emergency Operation
Refers to urgent conditions that require rapid preparation and implementation of surgery in the shortest possible time.
Constrained Operation
Due to the nature of the illness, the surgery time cannot be delayed for too long, but it can be chosen within a limited time. For example, open reduction and internal fixation of closed fractures, radical resection of malignant tumors, etc. Preoperative preparation and nursing work should be completed more comprehensively in a shorter period of time.
Selective Operation
The delay in surgery time does not affect the surgical outcome. For example, various deformity correction surgeries, benign tumor resection, etc. These patients have a relatively generous period of preoperative preparation time, and comprehensive preoperative preparation and nursing can ensure the patient's good physical condition and strong tolerance to surgery, thus ensuring the safety of the surgery.

# Psychological Preparation
Explain to the patient, starting from care and encouragement, the condition, the necessity of surgery, the possible effects after surgery, the risks of surgery, and possible complications with appropriate language and comforting tone.

Gain the patient's trust and consent, and complete the written informed consent procedure.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
Physiological Preparation
Adaptive Exercise: Proper methods for bedridden urination and defecation, correct coughing and expectoration techniques.
Blood Transfusion and Fluid Resuscitation: Preoperative blood matching, correction of electrolyte and acid-base balance disorders, and correction of anemia (generally should reach hemoglobin 100g/L).
Prevention of Infection: Promptly handle existing infection foci; patients should not come into contact with infected individuals before surgery; strictly follow aseptic techniques.

The following situations require prophylactic use of antibiotics

(1) Incisions close to the infected area;
(2) Long operation time, severe trauma;
(3) Cancer surgery;
(4) Surgery involving large blood vessels;
(5) Surgery requiring the implantation of artificial products.

Administration: Give 0.5-2 hours before surgery or at the start of anesthesia. If the operation time exceeds 3 hours or the blood loss is greater than 1500ml, a second dose can be given intraoperatively.

Antibiotics should be used rationally based on the patient's preoperative liver and kidney function
(1) Drugs mainly cleared by the liver with no significant toxic reactions, such as macrolides (except for erythromycin estolate), clindamycin, and lincomycin, should be used with caution and the dose should be reduced if necessary.

(2) Drugs mainly cleared by the liver or a significant amount by the liver, whose clearance or metabolism is reduced in cases of liver dysfunction, leading to significant toxic reactions, such as chloramphenicol, rifampin, erythromycin estolate, ampicillin estolate, isoniazid, amphotericin B, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, ketoconazole, and miconazole, etc. These drugs should be avoided as much as possible in cases of liver disease.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
Antibiotic use in liver dysfunction
(3) Drugs cleared through both renal and hepatic pathways, whose blood concentration increases in severe liver dysfunction, and these patients often have functional renal insufficiency, which can further increase the blood concentration, so dosage reduction is required. These drugs include mezlocillin, azlocillin, and piperacillin in the ureidopenicillin group, cefoperazone, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, cefalothin, and aztreonam, antiviral drugs didanosine, zidovudine, and amantadine, fluoroquinolones such as pefloxacin, fleroxacin, norfloxacin, and moxifloxacin, etc.

(4) Drugs mainly excreted by the kidneys, which generally do not require dose adjustment in cases of liver dysfunction. These drugs include penicillin, cefazolin, ceftriaxone, aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, etc.), vancomycin, polymyxin, and ofloxacin. When used in patients with severe liver dysfunction, they still need to be used with caution or in reduced doses to prevent the occurrence of hepatorenal syndrome.

Gastrointestinal Preparation
Adults should be fasting for 12 hours and drinking restricted for 4 hours before surgery to prevent aspiration or inhalation due to vomiting during surgery;

For general surgery, a 0.1%-0.2% soap water enema should be performed the day before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
Other:
Skin preparation (cleaning, shaving) should be done the afternoon or evening before surgery;
On the night before surgery, carefully check the preoperative preparation, and apply sedatives if necessary to ensure the patient's sleep;
If the patient has an elevated body temperature or menstrual periods in women unrelated to the disease - delay surgery;
If the surgery is expected to be long (over 3h) or rectal and pelvic surgery - a catheter should be placed;
If the patient has active dentures or jewelry, they should be removed.

Management of Underlying Diseases

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Malnutrition
Preoperative weight loss of more than 20% due to illness not only increases the mortality rate but also increases the infection rate by three times.

Serum albumin below 30g/L and serum transferrin below 1.5mg/L indicate malnutrition. Nutritional support with enteral or parenteral nutrition before surgery is required.

# Extracorporeal Nutrition (PN)
Perioperative period for major surgery and trauma: Nutritional support has no significant effect on patients with good nutritional status, and may even increase the risk of infection complications. However, for patients with severe malnutrition, it can reduce postoperative complications. Severe malnourished patients require nutritional support for 7-10 days before surgery; for patients expected to have gastrointestinal function not recoverable within 5-7 days after major surgery, parenteral nutrition support should be initiated within 48 hours after surgery until the patient has sufficient enteral nutrition or intake.

# Hypertension
Classification Criteria for Hypertension
Hypertension is classified into three grades based on the level of systolic and diastolic blood pressure:
Grade 1 Hypertension (Mild): Systolic pressure 140-159 mmHg, Diastolic pressure 90-99 mmHg
Grade 2 Hypertension (Moderate): Systolic pressure 160-179 mmHg, Diastolic pressure 100-109 mmHg
Grade 3 Hypertension (Severe): Systolic pressure ≥180 mmHg, Diastolic pressure ≥110 mmHg
Based on the degree of target organ damage, hypertension is further divided into three stages after being diagnosed:
1. Stage 1 Hypertension: Hypertension with no organic changes in target organs
2. Stage 2 Hypertension: Hypertension with organic changes in target organs
3. Stage 3 Hypertension: Hypertension with organic changes and functional compensation in target organs

# Hypertension
Patients with mild to moderate hypertension generally do not need to delay or cancel surgery, but antihypertensive medications should be continued to control blood pressure at an ideal level before surgery.
Severe hypertension (DBP > 110 mmHg) should be delayed, and blood pressure should be controlled.
It has been reported that for patients with difficult-to-control hypertension before surgery, approximately 5.1% experience myocardial infarction, 5.7% experience cardiac arrest, and about 12%-17% experience myocardial ischemia after surgery.

# Cardiovascular Disease
The incidence and mortality rate of cardiovascular complications are significantly increased in patients with cardiovascular disease or high-risk factors for cardiovascular disease undergoing non-cardiac surgery.
Cardiovascular complications during the perioperative period for non-cardiac surgery not only affect early postoperative recovery but also affect the prognosis for 1-2 years or even longer after surgery.

# Preoperative Preparation for Cardiac Surgery
1. Patients with long-term low-sodium, low-fat diet and diuretics, and electrolyte balance, need to be corrected before surgery.
2. Patients with arrhythmia should be treated differently according to different conditions:
Occasional ventricular premature complexes generally do not require special treatment
If there is atrial fibrillation with a heart rate of over 100 beats/min, intravenous injection of 0.4 mg of digoxin in 20 ml of 25% glucose solution or oral administration of 10 mg of propranolol three times a day can be used to control the heart rate as close to normal as possible.
Bradycardia: If the ventricular rate is below 50 beats/min, atropine 0.5-1 mg can be used before surgery; if necessary, a temporary pacemaker should be placed.
3. Patients with acute myocardial infarction should not undergo elective surgery within 6 months. If there is no angina for more than 6 months, surgery can be performed under monitoring. Patients with heart failure should wait 3-4 weeks after heart failure is controlled before surgery.

## Respiratory Dysfunction
Smokers should cease smoking for 2 weeks, practice deep breathing and coughing exercises, and use a spirometer to enhance inspiratory function.
Bronchodilators such as ephedrine and aminophylline, and inhalation of isoprenaline, can increase lung volume and have a good effect on obstructive pulmonary dysfunction. When sputum is thick, steam inhalation or oral medication can be used to thin the sputum for easier expectoration. Patients who frequently cough up sputum containing pus should receive antibiotics 3-5 days before surgery and perform postural drainage.
Patients with frequent asthma attacks can take dexamethasone to reduce bronchial mucosal edema.
The dose of pre-anesthetic medication should be minimized to avoid respiratory depression and difficulty in expectoration. Pethidine is preferable to morphine due to its bronchodilatory effect. Atropine should also be administered in appropriate amounts to avoid increasing sputum viscosity.

## Liver Function Evaluation
Mild liver function impairment does not affect the tolerance to surgery.

Severe liver function impairment or approaching decompensation significantly weakens the tolerance to surgery, and it is necessary to undergo a longer and stricter preparation period before elective surgery can be performed.

Severe liver function impairment, with obvious malnutrition, ascites, jaundice, or patients with acute hepatitis, are generally not suitable for surgery, except for emergency rescue.

## # Renal Disease
Routine laboratory tests are used to understand the patient's preoperative renal function status.
Mild to moderate impairment can generally tolerate surgery well after appropriate medical treatment;
Severe impairment can be quite safely tolerated with effective dialysis therapy, but renal function should be maximally improved before surgery.

## # Renal Disease
Acute renal failure associated with surgery is almost always of pre-renal origin, such as hypovolemia, hypotension, sepsis, or other causes of reduced circulatory blood volume.
Prompt correction of pre-renal causes and appropriate sodium and water supplementation can prevent or reduce the severity of acute tubular necrosis.
33

## Diabetes Mellitus
Control blood glucose, correct water and electrolyte and acid-base imbalances, and improve nutritional status.
For patients undergoing major surgery, blood glucose should be controlled at a mild elevation (5.6-11.2mmol/L), with urine sugar + to ++.
Surgery should be performed as early as possible on the day of surgery to shorten the preoperative fasting period and avoid ketosis acidosis.
Patients taking oral hypoglycemic agents can continue to take them until the evening before surgery, and patients taking long-acting oral hypoglycemic agents such as chlorpropamide should discontinue them 2-3 days before surgery.
34

Preoperative fasting blood glucose measurement, followed by the initiation of intravenous infusion of 5% glucose, and 1/3 to 2/3 of the usual morning insulin dose is administered subcutaneously.

Intraoperatively, insulin is added to the glucose solution at a 5:1 ratio (5g of glucose plus 1U of insulin).

Postoperatively, the insulin dose is adjusted according to urine sugar levels (every 4-6 hours). If urine sugar is++++, use 12U; +++, 8U;++, 4U; +, no insulin is required. If urine ketones are positive, the insulin dose should be increased by 4U.

# Coagulation Dysfunction
Discontinue aspirin 7 days before surgery.

Discontinue non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs 2-3 days before surgery.

Discontinue antiplatelet drugs such as clopidogrel 10 days before surgery.

Platelet count <50*10^9/L, platelets should be transfused.

For patients requiring anticoagulation therapy, a balance should be struck between intraoperative bleeding and postoperative thrombosis.

Postoperative Analgesia

Pain can lead to a series of physiological, pathological, and psychological changes in patients, such as anxiety, irritability, insomnia, increased blood pressure, and decreased immune function, which may even affect the expected outcome of surgery and postoperative recovery, evolving into chronic pain. With the continuous progress of society, people's health awareness is gradually increasing, and higher requirements have been put forward for the quality of diagnosis and treatment.
Medical staff should formulate individualized analgesic plans for patients according to standardized pain management processes and a comprehensive pain assessment system, trying to keep pain at a minimal level, even painless, so that patients can comfortably go through the perioperative period and functional recovery period.

Deep Venous Thrombosis - Pulmonary Embolism
A concept that requires an overall understanding.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE)
Deep venous thrombosis (DVT)
Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE)
 VTE = DVT + PTE
 DVT and PTE are the same disease, with different manifestations at different stages and different locations.

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->

Epidemiological surveys have confirmed that orthopedic patients undergoing major surgery in Asia have a high incidence of DVT.

Patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery (including hip and knee replacement, hip fracture surgery) are all at a very high risk for VTE.

Orthopedic patients undergoing major surgery must be routinely prevented from DVT.

# VTE Risk Factors
The primary risk factors for VTE include: antithrombin deficiency, factor V Leiden mutation (activated protein C resistance), congenital fibrinolysis abnormality, prothrombin gene G20210A mutation, protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, plasminogen deficiency, factor XII deficiency, hyperhomocysteinemia, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, increased plasminogen activator inhibitor, abnormal thrombomodulin, abnormal plasminogenemia.

<!-- Slide number: 44 -->
# VTE Risk Factors
The secondary risk factors for VTE include: trauma or fracture, surgical procedures and tourniquet application, stroke, paralysis, previous VTE history, severe infection, immobilization, malignant tumors, intravenous chemotherapy of tumors, advanced age, central venous catheterization, chronic venous insufficiency, smoking, pregnancy or puerperium, Crohn's disease, nephrotic syndrome, hypercoagulability, platelet abnormalities, obesity, heart failure, long-distance air travel or car travel, oral contraceptives, lupus anticoagulant, artificial joint implantation, myocardial infarction, chronic respiratory disease.

# DVT Diagnosis
About 50% to 80% of DVT cases may have no clinical manifestations, but due to the great harm that can be caused by complications such as fatal PTE and long-term lower limb deep venous dysfunction, early detection and treatment are crucial, which depend on early discovery and correct diagnosis of the disease condition.
(One) DVT diagnosis
1. Clinical characteristics of DVT with symptoms and signs
(1) Common in patients after surgery, trauma, advanced tumor, coma, or long-term bed rest.
(2) Onset is acute, with swelling, hardness, and pain in the affected limb, which worsens with activity, and occasionally fever and tachycardia.

# DVT Diagnosis
3) Thrombus site tenderness, palpable cord-like structures along the vessel, swelling of the distal limb or the entire limb, skin discoloration to purple, decreased skin temperature, decreased or absent dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial artery pulsations, or the occurrence of venous gangrene. When the thrombus extends to the inferior vena cava, there is significant edema in both lower limbs, buttocks, lower abdomen, and external genitalia. When the thrombus occurs in the calf muscle venous plexus, Homans sign and Neuhofs sign are positive.

This causes passive stretching of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles, stimulating the diseased veins in the lower leg muscles, causing deep pain in the lower leg muscles, which is positive.
Neuhofs sign, i.e., the compression gastrocnemius test.

# Basic Preventive Measures
- Gentle surgical manipulation to avoid venous intimal injury
- Standardized application of lower limb tourniquets
- Elevating the affected limb postoperatively
- Encouraging patient-initiated movement and ambulation as soon as possible
- Intraoperative and postoperative fluid resuscitation, increased fluid intake, and avoiding dehydration
- Improving lifestyle, quitting smoking and alcohol, and controlling blood glucose and lipids

# Drug Preventive Methods
- Unfractionated heparin
  - Requires monitoring of APTT and adjustment of dosage
  - Requires monitoring of platelet count
  - Long-term use of heparin may lead to osteoporosis

- Low-molecular-weight heparin
  - Dose adjusted according to body weight, convenient for subcutaneous injection
  - Fewer severe bleeding complications, relatively safe
  - Generally does not require routine monitoring

- Vitamin K antagonists
  - Warfarin is inexpensive and used for long-term prevention of DVT
  - Requires routine monitoring of INR values
  - Easily affected by many medications and vitamin K-rich foods

- Xa factor inhibitors

  - Indirect inhibitors - Fondaparinux
    - Subcutaneous injection
    - More effective than enoxaparin, similar in safety

  - Direct inhibitors - Rivaroxaban
    - Convenient for oral administration
    - More effective than LMWH, does not increase bleeding risk

- Other novel anticoagulant drugs

- Severe head trauma or acute spinal cord injury
- Platelet count below 20×109/L
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)

# Timing of VTE Prevention
The perioperative period of orthopedic major surgery is a high-risk period for VTE, with the peak occurring 12-24 hours postoperatively. Prevention should be initiated as early as possible.

The closer the drug prevention is to the time of surgery, the higher the risk of bleeding complications. Therefore, the timing of drug prevention for VTE should weigh the risks and benefits.
