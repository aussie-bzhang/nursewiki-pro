# Purpose of Preoperative Fasting and Fluid Restriction (NPO)
Preoperative fasting and fluid restriction (non-peros, NPO) is aimed at ensuring the stomach is adequately empty to prevent aspiration of gastric contents during anesthesia, which could lead to aspiration pneumonia. Therefore, the traditional 12-hour fasting and 4-hour fluid restriction before surgery has become a standard practice and is still listed in textbooks to this day.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
# Significance of NPO
1. Origin of NPO
   (1) In the late 19th century, Lister suggested that a 2-hour fluid restriction before anesthesia could reduce the risk of aspiration during surgery.
   (2) Mendelson syndrome refers to the reflux of small amounts of acidic substances into the lungs, causing severe bronchospasm. Subsequently, due to damage to the alveolar epithelial cells, the permeability of the capillary walls increases, leading to the leakage of a large amount of plasma, forming varying degrees of acute lung injury.
   (3) Preoperative fasting can reduce the volume of gastric contents, reduce the risk during anesthesia, and decrease the incidence of Mendelson syndrome. Strict preoperative fasting and fluid restriction has become a standard practice.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# Impact of NPO on Patients
1. Prolonged preoperative fasting and fluid restriction can increase the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting and may prolong the time of postoperative ileus.
2. Prolonged fasting and fluid restriction can lead to a decrease in body water and salt, causing blood to become more concentrated, resulting in low or insufficient blood volume.
3. Prolonged fasting and fluid restriction can cause most patients to experience significant thirst and hunger, leading to changes in the metabolic background of the body, a decrease in insulin sensitivity, and the development of insulin resistance.
4. Hunger can also cause sympathetic nervous system activation, leading to irritability and anxiety.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# Impact of NPO on Patients (continued)
5. Invasive surgery under prolonged fasting and fluid restriction can lead to hemodynamic instability, shock, and even collapse.
6. Prolonged fasting can also lead to a reduction in the absorption of glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids, so prolonged fasting may cause surgical patients to lose the optimal gastric environment, leading to dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, malnutrition, nausea, hypothermia, fatigue, and other adverse reactions, affecting vital signs stability.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
# Current Status of NPO
1. International research status
   In the past 10 years, countries such as Norway, Sweden, the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom have revised the preoperative fasting regulations, shortening the preoperative fasting and fluid restriction time.
    In 1990, the Canadian anesthesia guidelines had already deleted the section on preoperative diet, suggesting that each anesthesiology department should have its own regulations. Some countries in Europe and the United States have also changed the traditional preoperative fasting, allowing patients to consume carbohydrate beverages before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
        In 1999, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) revised the preoperative fasting guidelines, stipulating that any age patient can consume non-alcoholic clear liquids containing a small amount of sugar (such as water, tea, coffee, juice) 2 hours before elective surgery; adults and children can consume easily digestible foods (such as bread, milk) 6 hours before surgery; solid foods can be consumed 8 hours before surgery; in terms of dairy products, breastfeeding mothers should fast for 4 hours, and non-human milk and formula milk should be fasted for 6 hours.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
　 Current Status of NPO
2. Domestic research trends   In China, clinical nurses have also tried to shorten the NPO time limit and provide carbohydrate intake 2 hours before surgery.
   (1) Patients (excluding intestinal surgery) should fast from solid foods at least 6 hours before surgery and consume 500 ml of sugar water (containing 50 g of sucrose) under the guidance of a professional nurse 2 hours before surgery; for patients undergoing intestinal surgery, bowel preparation should be done according to the requirements of the surgery. Solid foods should be fasted for 3 days before surgery, and enteral nutrition preparations should be taken orally. 500 ml of sugar water (containing 50 g of sucrose) should also be consumed 2 hours before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->

(3) Consuming 800 ml of isotonic carbohydrate (about 100 g of sugar) the night before surgery and drinking a carbohydrate-rich beverage (containing 50 g of sugar) 2-3 hours before anesthesia can increase blood glucose and insulin concentrations, causing the body to transition from a state of hunger to an energy storage state, which is very beneficial for helping patients tolerate surgery.
(4) The optimal fasting time is: solid foods and dairy products 4-6 hours before surgery, milk 4 hours, and full liquid foods 2 hours.

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
# Outlook of NPO
       The NPO time limit in Chinese textbooks is still the traditional method. Although new fasting schemes suitable for Chinese people have been developed in line with international trends, further basic research and clinical practice on NPO are helpful for further adopting rigorous scientific research designs to explore
       Clinical medicine, anesthesia, nursing, and other fields should closely cooperate, based on evidence-based medicine to solve the problem of preoperative fasting and fluid restriction. Under the current legal nursing form, how to transform, supplement, improve, and promote the research achievements of NPO is our direction of effort.

# Respiratory Management
1. Proper assessment of respiratory function, timely treatment of respiratory system diseases
   Preoperative pulmonary function testing is necessary to properly assess respiratory function. For patients with abnormal pulmonary function, a detailed pulmonary function exercise plan should be formulated to ensure adequate preoperative preparation and to treat existing respiratory system diseases.
2. Smoking cessation

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
# Respiratory Management
3. Ventilation function training
   (1) Pursed-lip breathing  Instruct patients to inhale through the nose and then exhale slowly through half-closed lips, counting while exhaling and making a "puff" sound after counting to seven.
   (2) Diaphragmatic breathing  Patients place their hands on their abdomen to help contract the abdominal muscles while inhaling, take a deep breath, and then relax the abdomen to exhale the air.
     These training methods should be performed 3-4 times a day, for 8-10 minutes each time, with patients in a semi-recumbent position.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# Respiratory Management
4. Effective Cough and Sputum Expectoration Training
     Inform patients of the importance of coughing and expectorating after surgery. Instruct patients to assume a sitting position, relax their limbs, press on the abdomen with their hands, take a deep breath, hold their breath, and then cough out sputum.

5. Preoperative Nebulization Inhalation
     Twice daily, for 15-20 minutes each session.

6. Enhanced Nutrition
     For patients with concurrent nutritional disorders, poor surgical tolerance, weakened respiratory muscle contraction, and postoperative difficulty in expectorating sputum, improving the patient's nutritional status helps to reduce the occurrence of postoperative respiratory complications.

# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
1. Introduction of the Concept of Bowel Composite Barrier
     The so-called bowel composite barrier is actually a three-dimensional, organic, and biological barrier. It can be roughly divided into three parts: the intestinal lumen microecological barrier, physical barrier, and intestinal associated lymphoid tissue.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
2. Abandonment of Enema Viewpoint
   ① Long-term and repeated enemas can lead to electrolyte and water imbalance and malnutrition, reducing the patient's tolerance for surgery;
   ② Damage to the bowel composite barrier, further aggravating the body's adverse reactions;
   ③ After retrograde lavage, the detached tumor cells are flushed towards the proximal end, and with the downward movement of intestinal contents, tumor cells can implant in the existing or anastomotic wound surface, increasing the possibility of local recurrence.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
3. Enteral Nutrition Formula Assists in Preoperative Bowel Preparation
     Using the method of oral enteral nutrition formula for preoperative bowel preparation can meet the patient's nutritional needs to a certain extent and can stimulate the gastrointestinal tract, activate the intestinal neuroendocrine system, promote the synthesis and release of intestinal hormones, regulate the secretion of the stomach, bile, and pancreas, promote gastrointestinal motility and mucosal growth, and help maintain the mechanical, biological, and immune barrier functions of the bowel, prevent bacterial translocation, and reduce the incidence of infection.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
4. Improved Oral Laxative
     The mechanism of polyethylene glycol electrolyte bowel cleansing is through the gravitational action of the solution itself after administration, stimulating enhanced peristalsis of the small intestine, and can soften stool and promote defecation. PDG is neither absorbed nor metabolized and has good gastrointestinal tolerance, and its safety has been confirmed by a large number of domestic and foreign literature reports. Phosphate salts and PDG are effective, safe, rapid, and easily accepted bowel preparation drugs.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
5. New Concept of Bowel Preparation Time
     Traditional bowel preparation methods include: 4-5 days of semi-liquid diet before surgery, 2-3 days of liquid diet, oral antibiotics for 3-5 days, starting to take mild laxatives 3 days before surgery, such as 30 mL of 50% magnesium sulfate or 30 mL of castor oil, etc., lavage once a night before surgery, and until the stool is clear water-like.
     The 1-day bowel preparation scheme represented by sodium phosphate salts shows no significant differences in anastomotic healing, infectious complications, serological indicators, and intestinal mucosal structure. The incidence of diarrhea and postoperative mortality is lower in the 1-day scheme, and patients are more easily tolerated.

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
6. Is Long-term Administration of Antibiotics Necessary for Bowel Preparation
    It is currently believed that continuous preoperative oral administration of antibiotics can easily cause excessive proliferation of intestinal fungi and increase complications. Antibiotics inhibit the intestinal flora in the intestine, promote the implantation of cancer cells at the anastomosis, increase local recurrence, and can also cause dysbacteriosis, producing related complications.
    Clinical studies have found that there is no significant difference between the results obtained from a single dose of oral antibiotics and traditional antibiotic use. It is advocated that the use of antibiotics for bowel preparation in clinical practice should follow the following principles: single drug, short-term, broad-spectrum, high-efficiency, and low-toxicity, not absorbed by the intestine.

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
# New Perspectives on Bowel Preparation
7. Non-conventional Bowel Preparation Viewpoint
     Studies have shown that patients who do not undergo bowel preparation have more feces in the intestine during surgery than the traditional group, but since the surgery is performed in a bowel functionally quiet state, it has less interference with the human internal environment, early postoperative排气, lower rates of infection and anastomotic leakage, and rarely develop pseudomembranous colitis. Muller-Stich et al. analyzed that there is no evidence to prove that bowel preparation can reduce the incidence of postoperative complications such as anastomotic dehiscence and infection.

# Four. Skin Preparation
1. Common skin preparation methods at home and abroad include shaving hair skin preparation, depilatory skin preparation, push hair skin preparation, and non-shaving skin preparation.
2. There is no need to shave hair when the hair on the surgical site does not exceed 1 cm and does not involve the patient's hair, armpit hair, and pubic hair.
3. Studies have shown that chemical depilatories have antibacterial effects on pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, but a skin allergy test must be performed before use.
4. The time for skin preparation should be as close as possible to the time of surgery. Skin preparation in the operating room can reduce the infection rate of surgical incisions.

# One. Intraoperative Warming
       Perioperative warming - Continuous intraoperative hypothermia can suppress platelet function, damage the coagulation mechanism, and even trigger a triad of hypothermia, coagulation disorders, and metabolic acidosis that can lead to death; warming during and in the early postoperative period can reduce intraoperative bleeding, postoperative infection, cardiovascular complications, and reduce catabolism. Therefore, before the patient enters the operating room, we maintain the room temperature at 29°C or above. If necessary, the surgical bed mattress should be heated to keep warm, and a heating device should be used during infusion to maintain the normal body temperature of the head and upper limbs; the use of warm, sterile distilled water for lavage of the thoracic and abdominal cavities during surgery can greatly reduce the risk of surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
# The Harm of Hypothermia to Surgical Patients
1. Increase the wound infection rate
2. Affect coagulation function
3. Affect body metabolism
4. Increase cardiovascular complications
5. Delay postoperative recovery

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# Factors Affecting Hypothermia
1. Cold environment
2. Inadequate heat production
3. Anesthesia and drug effects
4. Large-volume blood transfusion and fluid infusion
5. Open body cavity during surgery

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Nursing Measures to Prevent Hypothermia
1. Adjust the temperature of the operating room
2. Heat the blood and fluid infusion
3. Use warming equipment
4. Install a gas heater in the anesthesia machine breathing circuit
5. Heat the lavage fluid

# Postoperative Pain Treatment Methods
1. Pharmacological treatment mainly refers to oral administration, intramuscular injection, intravenous injection, and epidural analgesia, etc.
2. Physical therapy involves the use of various physical energies on the body, where the first to receive stimulation are the receptors with the lowest excitability threshold. It then acts on certain pain-causing factors, through neural reflex actions and the regulatory effects of the humoral system, resulting in the rapid excretion of pain-causing chemical mediators, thereby reducing or eliminating pain. It is specifically divided into electrotherapy, thermal therapy, ultrasonic therapy, physical therapy, massage therapy, etc.
3. Aromatherapy treatment is designed to prevent damaged tissues from releasing prostaglandins, pre-emptively eliminating pain; and to increase endorphins within the body, blocking the transmission of pain signals in nerve cells.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
# Postoperative Pain Treatment Methods
4. Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA), which has the advantage of maximizing individual patient needs, recording and preserving usage, and a high safety factor; single-use pumps, which have the advantages of being portable, lightweight, and easy to operate.
    Intravenous PCA (PCIA)
    Epidural PCA (PCEA)
    Subcutaneous PCA (PCSA)
    Peripheral Nerve Root and Plexus PCA (PCNA)

# PCA Nursing
1. Assess the patient's basic condition;
2. Conduct handover with care;
3. Master the use, parameter settings, and drug characteristics of PCA;
4. Instruct patients on the correct use of PCA; ensure the normal operation of PCA;
5. Use a separate intravenous channel for PCIA;
6. Monitor and record vital signs, analgesic regimens, and analgesic effects at regular intervals;
7. Prevent and treat infections;
8. Prevent and treat complications;
9. Precautions for extubation in PCEA.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
# Principles to Follow in Postoperative Analgesia
1. Proactive prevention of medication, scheduled administration is preferable to on-demand administration;
2. Pay attention to the local condition of the surgery and clearly identify the cause of pain;
3. Select analgesic drugs with low toxicity, minimal impact on physiological indicators, and proven efficacy, and pay attention to the observation of vital signs during medication;
4. Initially use non-narcotic analgesics and sedatives in combination, and decide whether to use narcotic analgesics based on the analgesic effect;
5. The assessment and management of pain require the participation of patients.

         The patient's self-report is the gold standard for assessing pain.

# Implementation of Pain Care
1. Pay attention to listening to the patient's self-report and accurately assess the degree and nature of pain;
2. Preemptive analgesia to avoid the adverse effects of pain on the body;

# Three, Postoperative Activity
Does it really need to be supine for 6 hours after general anesthesia?
    Low bed head and prolonged supine position are risk factors for aspiration pneumonia
    Continuing to maintain a supine position postoperatively will not allow respiratory function to recover quickly, as the position can reduce tidal volume by 9.2%.

<!-- Slide number: 40 -->
# Early postoperative semi-recumbent position
       Once the anesthesia has worn off and vital signs are normal, the bed head can be raised to 20° within 1 to 2 hours postoperatively, to 30° within 2 to 4 hours, to 45° after 4 hours, and to 60° after 12 hours. At the same time, the bed base support can be raised by 10° to 20° to allow the patient to assume a head-high position, which can reduce chest and diaphragm compliance by 7%, and increase vital capacity by 10% to 15%.

<!-- Slide number: 41 -->
# Benefits of Early Postoperative Semi-recumbent Position
1. Increased mobility of the lower chest and diaphragm, with the diaphragm descending, allowing better expansion of the lower lung fields, thereby increasing the gas exchange area, which is beneficial for ventilation and improves breathing
2. It can also increase the return of blood volume and cardiac output, promote systemic circulation, improve blood oxygen content, and improve systemic hypoxia
3. Semi-recumbent position can reduce the tension of the abdominal incision, alleviate pain, and increase patient comfort

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
# Benefits of Early Postoperative Semi-recumbent Position
4. Within 2 to 8 hours after extubation, the incidence of gastroesophageal reflux and aspiration is very high. If a semi-recumbent position is maintained, and the gastrointestinal decompression drainage is kept unobstructed, it can prevent the reflux of gastric contents into the pharynx through the lower esophageal sphincter, causing aspiration.
5. Beneficial for the drainage of various drainage tubes, reducing the body's inflammatory response

<!-- Slide number: 43 -->
# Early Postoperative Activity Content for Abdominal Surgery
1. Upper limb exercises, including fist clenching, elbow flexion, arm lifting, and shoulder rotation;
2. Chest exercises, including deep breathing and chest expansion;
3. Lower limb exercises, including knee flexion and extension, leg lifting, and hip abduction;
4. Bedside whole-body exercises, including sitting up on the bed with hands supporting, and autonomous side-lying;

<!-- Slide number: 44 -->
# Early Postoperative Activity Content for Abdominal Surgery
5. Bedside sitting, that is, sitting on the bed edge with both legs on the ground and hands supporting;
6. Assisted ambulation, including assisted sitting on a chair, bed walking, and indoor walking with support;
7. Self-ambulation, that is, walking indoors and outdoors independently, changing from passive activity to active behavior, completing daily activities such as eating, dressing, washing, and using the toilet.

<!-- Slide number: 45 -->
# Benefits of Early Postoperative Activity
1. Emphasize early ambulation, as it can increase intestinal motility and lung capacity, enhance disease resistance, and accelerate physical recovery.
2. Accelerate blood circulation in the incision area, promote incision healing and lower limb venous return, prevent the formation of postoperative deep vein thrombosis, and reduce the occurrence of postoperative complications.
3. Shorten the patient's bed rest time, improve their psychological satisfaction, and enable them to reach treatment goals, restore functional activities, and speed up discharge.

<!-- Slide number: 46 -->
# Principles of Early Activity
       Engage in activities as described above, under the guidance and supervision of nursing staff to ensure patients complete their daily activities. Perform activities four times a day, at morning, afternoon, evening, and before bedtime, each for 15 to 20 minutes. Try to choose times when the patient is in good spirits and when activities do not interfere with treatment or examinations. The intensity of the activities should be tailored to individual differences.

<!-- Slide number: 47 -->
# Principles of Early Activity
           Perform the first three activities within 12 hours postoperatively, the fourth activity within 12 to 24 hours postoperatively, continue with activities 1 to 3 but with increased intensity, perform the fifth and sixth activities within 24 to 48 hours postoperatively, and the seventh activity within 48 to 72 hours postoperatively, gradually extending the walking time. For the elderly, frail, or those with special conditions, the activities may be delayed by one day.

# Four: Selection of Postoperative Nutritional Methods
    Application of Glutamine
        Glutamine is a special neutral amino acid essential for maintaining the structure and function of the gastrointestinal tract. It is the most abundant amino acid in the human whole blood amino acid profile. Patients with various severe diseases, dietary restrictions, or those receiving long-term total parenteral nutrition (TPN) support (TPN does not contain glutamine) are prone to severe glutamine deficiency.

<!-- Slide number: 49 -->
      Patients with financial difficulties can be given appropriate amounts of normal saline, glucose, rice porridge, vegetable soup, and milk, etc., as homemade nutritional fluids to alleviate their financial burden and reduce the amount of intravenous fluid administration.
