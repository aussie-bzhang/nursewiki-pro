#
Knowledge of Surgical Incisions

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
1. Surgical Site Infections (SSIs)
SSIs refer to infections occurring in the incision or deep organs or cavities during the perioperative period, such as incisional infection, brain abscess, peritonitis.

SSI accounts for approximately 15% of all hospital infections and 35% to 40% of hospital infections in surgical patients. The concept of SSI is broader than that of "wound infection" because it includes infections in organs and cavities that were involved in surgery; it is narrower and more specific than the concept of "postoperative infection" because it does not include infections that occur at different times after surgery but are not directly related to surgical procedures, such as pneumonia and urinary tract infections.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
2. Classification of Surgical Incisions
| Classification | Criteria |
| --- | --- |
| I Class (Clean) Incision | Surgery does not involve inflammatory areas, does not involve respiratory, digestive, urinary, and reproductive organs that are in direct communication with the external environment of the human body |
| II Class (Clean-Polluted) Incision | Operations involving the upper and lower respiratory tract, upper and lower digestive tract, urinary and reproductive tract, or surgery through these organs, such as oral pharyngeal surgery, biliary tract surgery, total hysterectomy, rectal prostate surgery, as well as open fracture or trauma surgery |
| III Class (Polluted) Incision | Surgery that causes severe contamination of the surgical site, including: surgery involving acute inflammation but not suppurated areas; obvious overflow of gastrointestinal contents causing contamination; fresh open wounds that have not been timely debrided; significant defects in aseptic technique such as thoracotomy, cardiac compression |
| IV Class (Contaminated-Infection) Incision | Surgery on old trauma with necrotic tissue; surgery with existing clinical infection or organ perforation |

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
Classification by the above methods shows significant differences in the infection rates of different incisions. The infection rate of clean incisions is 1%, clean-polluted incisions is 7%, polluted incisions is 20%, and contaminated-infection incisions is 40%. Accurate classification is generally made after surgery, but surgeons should predict it before surgery as an important basis for deciding whether prophylactic antibiotics are needed.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
3. Bacteriology of Surgical Site Infections
Common pathogenic bacteria: Staphylococcus (Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococcus), Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia coli, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, etc.).
The pathogenic bacteria of SSI mainly come from the bacteria in the skin, mucosa, and hollow organs of the patient.
Most of the pathogenic bacteria carried by the skin are Gram-positive cocci, but in the perineum and inguinal area, the skin is often contaminated with feces and carries Gram-negative bacilli and anaerobic bacteria. The typical pathogenic bacteria of SSI when the gastrointestinal tract, biliary tract, urinary tract, and female reproductive tract are opened are Gram-negative enteric bacilli, and in the rectum and vagina, there are anaerobic bacteria (mainly Bacteroides fragilis), which are the main pathogenic bacteria of organ/cavity infections in these areas. In any location, most surgical incision infections are caused by staphylococcus.

#
Indications for Prophylactic Drug Use in the Perioperative Period

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
1. Indications for Drug Use in Class I Clean Incisions
Most clean incisions do not require prophylactic use of antibiotics (relying on aseptic technique and surgical procedures).

Conditions requiring prophylactic use of antibiotics
      (1) Large surgical scope, excessive bleeding, long duration, increased risk of contamination
      (2) Surgery involving important organs
      (3) Implantation surgery
      (4) Other high-risk factors for infection: elderly
                             Poorly controlled diabetes for many years
                             Immunosuppressed individuals
                             Malnourished individuals

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
★
The "Specialized Rectification Campaign on the Clinical Application of Antibiotics in 2012" clearly stipulates that the following seven types of surgical patients should not be given prophylactic antibiotics in principle:
Inguinal hernia repair (including mesh repair)
Thyroid disease surgery
Breast disease surgery
Arthroscopic examination surgery
Carotid endarterectomy
Craniotomy for brain tumor
Diagnostic interventional surgery via vascular access

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
2. Indications for Drug Use in Other Types of Incisions
II Class Clean-Polluted Incisions: The surgical site exists a large number of human resident flora, and the surgical procedure may contaminate the surgical site, causing infection, so this type of surgery usually requires prophylactic use of antibiotics.
III Class Polluted Incisions: Surgery that has already caused severe contamination of the surgical site requires prophylactic use of antibiotics.
IV Class Contaminated-Infection Incisions: Treatment with antibiotics has already begun before surgery, and it continues during and after surgery, which does not belong to the category of prophylactic application.

#
Methods for Prophylactic Drug Use in the Perioperative Period

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
Drug Selection
The selection of antimicrobial agents during the perioperative period should be determined based on the purpose of prophylactic use.
To prevent postoperative incisional infection - mainly select drugs against Staphylococcus aureus.
To prevent surgical site infection or systemic infection - select drugs based on the surgical field contamination or possible contamination bacteria.
Choose relatively broad-spectrum, effective (bactericidal), able to cover most SSI pathogenic bacteria, safe, and inexpensive drugs.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
Cephalosporins are listed as the first choice for common surgical prophylactic antibiotics (Document No. 38)
Second-generation cephalosporins have strong bactericidal activity against Gram-positive cocci and Gram-negative bacilli and are suitable for the prevention of clean-polluted surgeries.
Aminoglycosides have ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity, and it is not recommended to use them for prophylaxis.
Generally, quinolone drugs are not used (only for urinary system surgery).
Macrolides are bacteriostatic agents and are generally not used as surgical prophylactic drugs.
Polypeptides are generally not used as surgical prophylactic drugs. In hospitals with a high incidence of MRSA, vancomycin or teicoplanin can be selected for surgery involving the implantation of artificial materials.
Carbapenems are not suitable for surgical prophylaxis.

2. Method of Use

Antimicrobial agents should be administered 0.5 to 1 hour before the start of surgery to ensure that effective concentrations of the drug are reached in the serum and tissues before contamination occurs.

Administration in the operating room rather than on call in the ward should be preferred.

If the surgery duration exceeds 3 hours or more than twice the half-life of the drug used, or if blood loss exceeds 1500ml, an additional dose should be administered intraoperatively.

The prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents for clean surgeries should not exceed 24 hours, and for cardiac surgeries, it may be extended to 48 hours as per the situation.

The prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents for clean-polluted surgeries and polluted surgeries is also 24 hours, and may be extended to 48 hours if necessary for polluted surgeries.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
# 3. Other Measures for Preventing SSI

Attempt to shorten the preoperative hospital stay to reduce the opportunity for inherent pathogenic bacteria to colonize the patient.

Ensure thorough preoperative preparation to place the patient in the best possible condition, such as controlling diabetes, improving malnutrition, actively treating existing infections, etc.

The traditional practice of shaving the day before surgery has been proven to be a misconception in the field of surgery. Bacteria will colonize on the epidermal wound after shaving, which can multiply the risk of SSI. Shaving is not necessary in areas with sparse hair. Hair can be trimmed in areas with dense hair.

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
Strictly adhere to the aseptic principles during surgery, perform detailed operations, care for tissues, and achieve thorough hemostasis. The infection of incisions is closely related to the presence of a large amount of necrotic tissue, retained foreign bodies, blood clots, and dead spaces.

Drainage items that can be left in or not should be left in as little as possible. Closed drainage should be used instead of open drainage where possible, and non-functional drainage items should be removed as soon as possible. Long-term placement of drainage items is not an indication for continuous use of prophylactic antimicrobial agents.

Locally using normal saline to flush the wound cavity or wound can help to clear blood clots, foreign debris, and residual bacteria, but there is no definite preventive effect of flushing the wound cavity or wound with antibiotic solutions, and it is not recommended.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->

Strive to control the intensity of antimicrobial use to below 40DDD.
The rate of antimicrobial use in inpatients should not exceed 60%.
The proportion of prophylactic antimicrobial use in patients undergoing class I incision surgery should not exceed 30%.
The duration of prophylactic antimicrobial use in inpatients undergoing surgical procedures should be controlled between 30 minutes and 1 hour before surgery.
The duration of prophylactic antimicrobial use in patients undergoing class I incision surgery should not exceed 24 hours.
The rate of prophylactic antimicrobial use in interventional diagnostic and therapeutic procedures should be zero.
★
The rate and intensity of antimicrobial use should be controlled within a reasonable range.
