Diabetes Diagnostic Criteria
Diabetes: Fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour blood glucose after OGTT ≥11.1 mmol/L, or random blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L with obvious diabetes symptoms;
Impaired fasting glucose (IFG): Fasting blood glucose 6.1-7.0 mmol/L, and 2-hour blood glucose after OGTT <7.8 mmol/L;
Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT): Fasting blood glucose <7.0 mmol/L, and 2-hour blood glucose after OGTT 7.8-11.1 mmol/L;
Gestational diabetes: Fasting blood glucose ≥5.1 mmol/L, or 1-hour blood glucose after OGTT ≥10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour blood glucose after OGTT ≥8.5 mmol/L (diagnosis can be confirmed if blood glucose is higher than the standard at one or more time points).

## Diabetes and the Close Relationship with Surgery

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
 Diabetes - A Troubling Problem for Surgery
1. The number of diabetes patients is increasing, reaching 11.6%;
2. The prevalence of gestational diabetes in different regions has reached 8-20%;
3. 40%-50% of diabetes patients will experience various surgeries in their lifetime, such as peripheral vascular disease, diabetic retinopathy, gallbladder disease, gastrointestinal disease, tumors, etc.;
4. 50% of diabetic surgical patients are over 50 years old;
5. 30-50% of surgical patients have abnormal glucose metabolism, and in some cardiovascular surgery departments, it can reach up to 70%;
6. After surgery, it is more likely to be accompanied by urinary tract infection, pulmonary infection, infection in other parts of the body, and electrolyte imbalance, causing difficult wound healing and prolonged course of disease;
7. The length of hospital stay is 30%-50% longer than that of non-diabetic patients;
8. The mortality rate is 2-3 times higher than that of non-diabetic patients, even higher.

### Notes:
Vomiting and inadequate fluid intake due to fasting before surgery can lead to insufficient fluid intake; the diuretic effect of hyperglycemia further causes fluid loss, leading to increased blood concentration and higher blood glucose levels, converting latent metabolic abnormalities into clinical diabetes, or causing acute exacerbation leading to hyperglycemic hyperosmolar dehydration or diabetic ketoacidosis; reduced blood volume leads to decreased cardiac output and renal blood flow, insufficient tissue perfusion, and cell dehydration. The irritation of surgery, postoperative discomfort, and fasting place patients in a highly stressed state; the massive secretion of stress hormones, including ACTH, cortisol, adrenaline, growth hormone, and glucagon, which are against insulin action, accelerates glycogenolysis and raises blood glucose levels; organic acids produced by fat and protein breakdown can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis. Potassium, sodium, and other cations are excreted with acidic substances, causing electrolyte disorders. In high-thoracic epidural anesthesia, beta cells of the pancreas are insensitive to hyperglycemia, causing insulin secretion deficiency in non-insulin-dependent diabetic patients. Hypothermic anesthesia during cardiopulmonary bypass surgery inhibits glucose utilization in the blood. The overall result is increased blood glucose levels.

Adverse effects of diabetes on surgical procedures (1)
(i) Diabetes can increase misdiagnosis:
Diabetic ketoacidosis can present with abdominal pain, elevated total white blood cell count, and neutrophils, which can be confused with acute abdomen;
Older diabetic patients with acute abdomen may have不明显 abdominal signs, thus delaying treatment;
(ii) Increased risk of hypoglycemia:
Type 1 diabetes: absolute insulin deficiency, large fluctuations in blood glucose levels, alternating highs and lows;
Type 2 diabetes: older age, weakness, long duration of disease, decreased perception of hypoglycemia;
(iii) More stringent surgical conditions:
High blood glucose levels are prone to induce diabetic ketoacidosis;
Low blood glucose levels may lead to anesthetic accidents or hypoglycemia induced by anesthetics during surgery;
(iv) Diabetes increases surgical mortality:
The mortality rate of diabetic patients undergoing surgery is 2-3 times, even 5-6 times higher than that of non-diabetic patients. This is particularly prominent in the elderly, those with long-standing disease, and poor blood glucose control;

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Adverse effects of diabetes on surgical procedures (2)
(v) Diabetes can significantly increase surgical complications:
1. Increased anesthetic accidents;
2. Delayed healing or non-healing of incisions: The presence of vascular lesions leads to poor local blood circulation; abnormal glucose metabolism leads to increased protein breakdown; reduced collagen synthesis leads to weakened tissue repair capacity;
3. High rates of infection (systemic and local): Diabetic patients have reduced cellular and humoral immunity, weakened antibacterial ability; high blood glucose levels themselves are a breeding ground for bacteria; high blood glucose inhibits the phagocytic ability and chemotaxis of white blood cells and phagocytes;
4. High risk of self-complications:
1) Renal lesions → renal insufficiency;
2) Neurological lesions → cardiac autonomic neuropathy, orthostatic hypotension, etc.;
3) Cardiovascular lesions → hypertension, myocardial ischemia, congestive heart failure, arrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, etc.;
4) Cerebrovascular lesions → transient ischemic attack, cerebral infarction, cerebral hemorrhage, etc.;
5) Peripheral vascular lesions → arterial embolism, deep vein thrombosis, etc.;

III. Preoperative Preparation

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
General Principles
Emphasize preoperative assessment to reduce surgical risk

1. Comprehensive evaluation of glucose metabolism level: The mortality rate of undiagnosed diabetes patients is 18 times higher than that of non-diabetic patients, and 3 times higher than that of diagnosed diabetes patients. Complete preoperative assessment of all surgical patients should include the detection of glucose metabolism levels.
2. Full assessment of surgical risks in diabetic patients: Surgical treatment for diabetic patients should be based on the principle of safety and simplicity. A full assessment of the patient's tolerance to surgery and anesthesia is an important aspect of ensuring surgical safety.
3. Collaboration between endocrinologists, surgeons, and anesthesiologists is required:
     Understand the patient's: age, health status, condition (complication status and heart and kidney function), treatment, and laboratory test results, as well as the type of surgery and anesthesia method;
     Develop a reasonable surgical treatment plan (preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative), perform perioperative risk assessment, inform, and prepare corresponding preventive measures;
Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (2013 Edition)
Nutr Hosp. 2013 Mar;28 Suppl 2:47-52.

Understanding Surgical Categories and Risk Factors for Diabetic Patients

Surgical Categories:
Small surgeries (such as biopsy, superficial surgery, angiography, or interventional procedures) completed in 0.5 to 1 hour: local anesthesia, no fasting required;
Medium to large surgeries (such as thoracotomy, laparotomy, craniotomy, fracture internal fixation, amputation, etc.) taking more than 1 hour, spinal or general anesthesia, fasting required.

Risk Factors:
Patient age > 65 years;
Diabetes duration over 5 years;
Comorbid cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases or diabetic nephropathy;
Surgical duration > 90 min;
General anesthesia, etc.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
Preoperative Blood Glucose Requirements for Diabetic Patients

Preoperative blood glucose concentration emphasizes individualization;
Elective surgery generally preferred with fasting blood glucose < 7.8 mmol/L and postprandial blood glucose < 10 mmol/L;
If fasting blood glucose > 10 mmol/L, or random blood glucose > 13.9 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level > 8.5%, it is recommended to postpone non-emergency surgery;
For ocular surgery, it is preferred to be 5.8-6.7 mmol/L;
For gestational diabetes, it is preferred to be below 6.7 mmol/L;
Emergency surgery should be controlled below 13.9 mmol/L;
Ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar coma patients are contraindicated for surgery.

China Type 2 Diabetes Prevention and Treatment Guidelines (2013 Edition)

Preoperative Preparation for Elective Surgery

1. Selection of timing for elective surgery:
1) Patients with diabetic hyperglycemic crisis (diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar syndrome) should postpone elective surgery;
2) Patients with long-term good blood glucose control and stress-induced hyperglycemia can undergo elective surgery. For patients with poor long-term blood glucose control, comprehensive assessment should be made based on the potential risks of poor wound healing and wound infection, and the presence of cardiovascular diseases and other diabetic complications;
3) Selecting the best timing for surgery: patients with glycated hemoglobin levels > 8.5% are recommended to consider postponing elective surgery. Preoperative fasting blood glucose should be ≤180 mg/dl (10 mmol/L), and random or 2-hour postprandial blood glucose ≤216 mg/dl (12 mmol/L) is preferred.

3. Preoperative management principles:
1) Avoiding the occurrence of hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia during and after surgery;

5. Selection of anesthetic agents:
1) The metabolism of local anesthetics, general anesthetics, and skeletal muscle relaxants is slowed down, and tolerance decreases, making it more likely to occur local anesthetic toxicity and delayed emergence from general anesthesia. Preoperative Preparation for Emergency Surgery
Comprehensive examination of blood glucose, ketone bodies, electrolytes, blood gas, liver and kidney function, electrocardiogram, and chest X-ray;
Insulin replacement therapy: Insulin is the only drug for controlling hyperglycemia during the perioperative period;
For random blood glucose ≥ 14 mmol/L, GIK mixture (glucose-insulin-potassium) or normal saline + a small dose of insulin (0.1~0.15 U/kg/h) can be administered continuously intravenously, with close monitoring of blood glucose levels (1 time/h), maintaining blood glucose to decrease steadily at a rate of 4~6 mmol/L per hour to < 11.1 mmol/L, and surgery can be performed when the condition is stable;
For patients with acute complications of diabetes such as diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar coma, metabolic disorders should be corrected first, and surgery can only be performed when blood glucose < 14 mmol/L, ketone bodies disappear, osmotic pressure and pH value return to normal; If delaying surgery poses a risk to life, diabetic ketoacidosis can be treated during surgery, and strict monitoring should be conducted.

Intraoperative Monitoring
1. Intraoperative blood glucose control targets:
      1) It is not recommended to control blood glucose too strictly; maintaining blood glucose between 5 mmol/L and 11.0 mmol/L is more appropriate during surgery;
      2) For patients with long-term elevated blood glucose, it is not advisable to decrease blood glucose too rapidly during the perioperative period: compared to hyperglycemia, the risk of perioperative mortality is higher when blood glucose fluctuates, and individualized targets should be established around the preoperative baseline level.
      3) For plastic surgery, which requires high wound healing, it is recommended to lower the blood glucose target to 6.0 mmol/L to 8.0 mmol/L to reduce postoperative wound infection;
      4) For patients with cerebrovascular diseases, who have poor tolerance to hypoglycemia, the target value can be relaxed to ≤12.0 mmol/L; the maximum should not exceed 13.9 mmol/L.

Intraoperative Monitoring
2. Intraoperative blood glucose monitoring:
     1) Monitoring methods: bedside rapid blood glucose meter measurement of fingerstick (capillary) blood glucose is used for patients with stable hemodynamics and metabolism; arterial or venous blood gas analysis is the gold standard for perioperative blood glucose monitoring; in cases of hypotension, low tissue perfusion, anemia, as well as hyperlipidemia, hyperbilirubinemia, hyperuricemia, and other metabolic abnormalities, the accuracy of fingerstick blood glucose decreases, and arterial blood gas monitoring should be used.

     2) Monitoring frequency: monitor blood glucose every 1~2 hours during surgery; for critically ill patients, major surgeries, or patients receiving intravenous insulin, monitor blood glucose every 30~60 minutes; during extracorporeal circulation surgery, blood glucose fluctuates greatly during cardiac arrest, cooling, and warming, and should be monitored every 15 minutes. Monitor blood glucose every 5~15 minutes until hypoglycemia is corrected when blood glucose ≤ 3.9 mmol/L.

Postoperative Nursing - Blood Glucose Control
1) Blood glucose control targets: For critically ill patients or those on mechanical ventilation, blood glucose control at 7.8mmol/L to 10.0mmol/L is appropriate; for patients undergoing minor to moderate surgery, fasting blood glucose should be less than 7.8mmol/L, and postprandial blood glucose should be less than 10mmol/L;
2) For patients unable to eat, continue to use G-I-K fluid therapy, with a sugar-to-insulin ratio of 3-5g:1U, and blood glucose levels should be measured regularly;
3) For patients who can eat, discontinue G-I-K fluid therapy and switch to subcutaneous insulin injection, once before each meal, with the dose based on the total daily dose of intravenous infusion;
4) For patients who were taking oral hypoglycemic agents before surgery, insulin should be gradually reduced and not discontinued immediately; when the total dose of insulin is less than 20U and blood glucose levels are good, oral hypoglycemic agents can be resumed.

Postoperative Nursing - Wound Healing Promotion
Measures to promote wound healing:
1) Basic nursing: Pay attention to the cleanliness and dryness of bed sheets and pillowcases, turn the patient regularly to prevent pressure sores, closely observe the wound condition, ensure the cleanliness of dressings; check the wound condition, observe the color, amount, and nature of the drainage fluid, and ensure the patency of the drainage tube.
2) Blood glucose monitoring: Pay attention to the fluctuation of the patient's blood glucose, strengthen postoperative blood glucose monitoring measures, and adjust insulin and hypoglycemic agents in a timely manner based on the patient's own blood glucose condition.
3) Infection prevention: Use antibiotics rationally; and change dressings in a timely manner, paying attention to aseptic operation during dressing changes.
4) Enhanced nutrition: Postoperative patients may experience complications due to nutritional deficiency and poor blood glucose control. Postoperative patients can be given a reasonable diet, pay attention to blood glucose control, and provide appropriate parenteral or enteral nutritional support to meet the needs of the patient's body.

Postoperative Nursing - Enhanced Nutritional Support
1. Importance of perioperative nutritional support: Now, the purpose of nutritional support is no longer simply to maintain the nitrogen balance of surgical patients and keep the patient's lean body mass, but to maintain organ, tissue, and immune function, promote organ and tissue repair, and accelerate patient recovery;
2. American ADA guidelines: The minimum daily calorie requirement for hospitalized diabetic patients is 25-35kcal/kg;
3. For patients who can eat: They should have a balanced diet while ensuring sufficient daily calories, and to reduce fluctuations in blood glucose, the proportion of carbohydrates can be appropriately reduced, and the proportion of fats rich in unsaturated fatty acids can be increased;
4. For patients who cannot eat: Glucose is still the most commonly used carbohydrate in clinical practice, but the application of fructose has gradually increased in recent years (because its metabolism does not depend on insulin, it has less blood glucose fluctuation and less urine sugar); at the same time, provide fat, with energy accounting for 30-50% of the total energy.

Postoperative Nursing - Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes Complications (1)
Prevention of cardiovascular complications:
1. More than one-third of diabetic patients have cardiovascular diseases, which are often characterized by the coexistence of coronary atherosclerosis and cardiac autonomic neuropathy, with poor tolerance to surgery;
2. Preoperative assessment: Whether there is coronary heart disease and its type and severity; history of myocardial infarction or asymptomatic myocardial infarction; cardiac function classification and the presence of heart failure;
3. Preoperative administration of coronary dilator drugs;
4. Postoperative admission to the ICU for electrocardiogram and myocardial enzyme spectrum monitoring.

Postoperative Nursing - Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes Complications (2)
Ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar coma:
    Causes: 1. Poor preoperative blood glucose control; 2. Unrecognized diabetes before surgery and undergoing surgery; 3. Administration of glucose, especially high sugar, without insulin; 4. Excessive glucose supplementation during the perioperative period; 5. Failure to increase insulin dosage in a timely manner due to surgical stress.

Method: 
- Preoperative blood glucose should be controlled to <13.9mmol/L
- For complex surgeries with long duration, the GIK protocol should be chosen
- Monitor ketones and osmolality promptly

Postoperative Nursing - Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes Complications (3)
Hypoglycemia:
    Causes: 1. Preoperative and postoperative fasting; 
            2. Insufficient glucose supplementation; 
            3. Excessive insulin use;

    Methods: 
        - Under preoperative fasting conditions, sufficient glucose should be supplemented;
        - Strengthen nutritional support postoperatively;
        - Monitor blood glucose closely and adjust insulin dosage accordingly.

Postoperative Nursing - Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes Complications (4)
Electrolyte Imbalance - Hyponatremia:
    Causes: 1. Sodium salts and accumulated ketones are excreted together;
            2. Hyperglycemia leads to increased osmolality, causing sodium to move from extracellular to intracellular spaces;

    Methods: 
        - Preoperative and postoperative blood biochemistry and ketone tests should be performed to comprehensively assess the patient's condition;
        - Correct ketones and hyperglycemia promptly;
        - Treat symptomatically and supplement sodium promptly.
