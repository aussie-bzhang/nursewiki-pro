# Perioperative Cardiac Risk and Venous Thrombosis Prevention

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->
**Part 1: Perioperative Cardiac Risk**

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->
## Perioperative Cardiac Risk Factors
① Type of surgery (thoracic and abdominal surgeries have higher risk than limb surgeries)
② Presence and severity of coronary artery disease, especially unstable disease (heart failure or unstable angina)
③ Left ventricular function status (left ventricular ejection fraction – EF)
④ Age
⑤ Severe valvular heart disease (especially aortic stenosis)
⑥ Severe arrhythmias
⑦ Comorbid conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypoxemia, diabetes, and renal insufficiency
⑧ Overall functional status

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
## Predictors of Cardiac Risk
High-risk surgery
History of ischemic heart disease
History of congestive heart failure
History of cerebrovascular disease
Preoperative insulin use
Preoperative creatinine level greater than 2.0 mg/dl

## Cardiac Risk Classification for Non-cardiac Surgery
High risk (*Cardiac risk often > 5%)
  Emergency major surgery, especially in the elderly
  Aortic or other major vessel
  Peripheral vascular
  Expected long-duration surgery with significant fluid or blood loss

Moderate risk (Cardiac risk generally < 5%)
  Carotid endarterectomy
  Head and neck
  Intra-abdominal and thoracic
  Orthopedic
  Prostate

Low risk (△Cardiac risk generally < 1%)
  Endoscopic
  Superficial surgery
  Cataract
  Breast

*Indicates cardiac death and non-fatal myocardial infarction
△Generally does not require further preoperative cardiac examination

## Preoperative Cardiovascular Function Assessment
The patient's exercise capacity is an important indicator of their ability to tolerate surgery well.
If a patient can engage in moderate exercise (4-5 metabolic equivalents [METs]) without symptoms, the relative risk of surgery is lower.
Performing an exercise stress test before non-cardiac surgery is of significant objective importance in assessing preoperative exercise function, especially for patients with unclear functional status.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
## Assessment of Cardiac Risk Through Exercise Testing
| Patients with suspected or confirmed coronary artery disease |
| --- |
| High risk: Low-level exercise* (<4 METs or heart rate <100 bpm or <70% age-predicted value) induces ischemia, with one or more of the following: Horizontal or downsloping ST segment depression >0.1 mV No infarction-related ST segment elevation >0.1 mV Five or more leads with abnormalities Post-exercise ischemic response duration >3 min Typical angina |
*When assessing risk based on workload and heart rate, the patient's age should be considered. For those not taking cardiac active drugs, the maximum target heart rate at the 40-year and 80-year age groups is 180 bpm and 140 bpm, respectively.

If a patient can perform moderate exercise (4-5 METs) without symptoms, the perioperative risk is relatively low.
Generally, postoperative cardiac events often occur in patients with abnormal exercise stress test results and those unable to tolerate moderate workloads (such as 4-5 METs).

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
## Impact of Coronary Artery Disease
For patients without cardiac symptoms, the risk of perioperative myocardial infarction (MI) is low (about 0.15%).
The mortality rate of perioperative MI is significantly higher than that of MI unrelated to surgery.
The risk of perioperative re-infarction within 6 months after MI is highest.
For patients with complete revascularization of myocardial vessels after MI, elective surgery can be performed 4-6 weeks after MI.

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
## Timing of Surgery After Coronary Artery Stent Placement
The use of antiplatelet drug clopidogrel (Plavix) after stent placement delays the time for non-cardiac surgery.
The perioperative risk is highest when surgery is performed immediately after interventional treatment.
Patients who have received bare-metal stent placement should delay non-cardiac surgery to at least 6 weeks after surgery.
Patients who have received drug-eluting stent placement should delay non-cardiac surgery to 6 months after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
## Perioperative Points for Patients with Chronic Heart Failure
For patients with clear-cut CHF, preoperative medication treatment should be as comprehensive as possible, but diuretics and vasodilators should be avoided to prevent dehydration and hypotension due to excessive use.
For patients with clear-cut CHF, the use of Swan-Ganz catheter monitoring before or during surgery is useful, especially in high-risk surgeries, as this method can well monitor intravascular volume and guide medication treatment.
Randomized trials have not shown that invasive hemodynamic monitoring can reduce the incidence of perioperative cardiac events.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# Perioperative Medication for Patients with Cardiovascular Disease
Beta-blockers can improve perioperative conditions and should be continued long-term as much as possible, particularly for patients with coronary artery disease. It is important to prevent sinus tachycardia in such patients after surgery.
Although calcium channel blockers and anesthetics both have vasodilatory and negative inotropic effects, anesthesia is generally safe for most patients on medication.
Although surgery can usually be scheduled without delay for patients with mild or moderate hypertension, those with severe hypertension (i.e., systolic blood pressure > 180 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure > 110 mmHg) should have their blood pressure controlled before surgery.
Appropriate analgesia is particularly important for the elderly.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# Perioperative Management of Arrhythmias
Patients with symptomatic arrhythmias or significant hemodynamic changes should receive treatment before non-cardiac surgery.
For patients on digoxin, it is important to correct even mild hypokalemia before surgery.
Asymptomatic conduction system diseases, such as bundle branch block, bifascicular block, or even trifascicular block, do not necessarily predict the occurrence of high or complete heart block during non-cardiac surgery and do not require prophylactic temporary pacing.

# Hazards of Venous Thromboembolism
Venous thromboembolic disease (VTE) includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the lower limb and pulmonary embolism (PE).
According to American data, approximately 600,000 cases of VTE occur each year, and 26% of patients with missed diagnoses and untreated VTE will experience fatal thrombotic events, while another 26% will have non-fatal thrombotic events that ultimately result in death.
DVT is closely related to PE, with nearly 50% of patients with proximal DVT having asymptomatic PE, and 80% of patients with PE being found to have DVT (including asymptomatic DVT).
Pulmonary embolism caused by lower limb DVT is a silent killer, with a very high mortality rate among patients with PE. 43% of patients die within 2 hours of onset, 10% within 1 hour, 36% within 2 to 24 hours, and only 21% within 1 month after onset.

# Perioperative and DVT
Before surgery: Fasting, bowel cleansing, etc., insufficient hydration often leads to patients being in a "dehydrated" state, causing blood to become concentrated and leading to a hypercoagulable state.
During surgery: Bed rest, immobilization, anesthesia, venous injury, hypotension, etc.
After surgery: Fasting, bed rest, hemostatic drugs, discontinuation of anticoagulants

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
# Diagnosis of DVT During the Perioperative Period
80% of patients have no clinical manifestations.
Main symptoms: Lower limb pain, swelling, and limited activity.
Common signs: Lower limb edema; when edema is severe, the skin color may become cyanotic, and the condition is related to the location (affected vein) of the thrombus, the speed and extent of formation. There may be deep pressure pain in the gastrocnemius muscle or Homan's sign (induced by plantar flexion of the affected limb, causing pain in the gastrocnemius muscle).
Ultrasound, MRI, etc.

# One of the main obstacles to thrombosis prevention strategies is concern about bleeding complications. However, a large number of meta-analyses and placebo-controlled, double-blind, randomized clinical studies have confirmed that prophylactic doses of low-dose unfractionated heparin (LDUH), low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), or vitamin K antagonists (VKA) almost do not increase the risk of clinically significant bleeding complications.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
# General Principles of DVT Prevention
For patients at high risk of venous thromboembolism with a tendency to bleed, mechanical prophylaxis should be provided, such as wearing elastic stockings (pressure 15~30 mmHg).
Aspirin is not required for the prevention of venous thrombosis.
Anticoagulants such as low molecular weight heparin, pentasaccharide, and argatroban are all excreted by the kidneys, and the renal function status of the patient should be considered when they are used. If necessary, ordinary heparin should be substituted.
Preventive anticoagulation therapy should be used with caution during neuraxial anesthesia.

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# DVT Prevention in General Surgery
For low-risk general surgery patients undergoing minor surgery, under the age of 40, and without other risk factors, no special prophylaxis is required, except for early and persistent activity (1C level).
For patients at moderate risk of general surgery, i.e., aged 40~60, undergoing non-major surgery, or with other risk factors, as well as for patients under the age of 40 undergoing major surgery without other risk factors, LDUH 5000U, twice daily, or LMWH ≤3400U, once daily (1A level).
For patients undergoing non-major surgery and over the age of 60 or with other risk factors, as well as for patients undergoing major surgery and over the age of 40 or with other risk factors, LDUH 5000U, three times daily, or LMWH ≥3400U, once daily (1A level).

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
For high-risk general surgery patients with multiple risk factors, the combined use of pharmacological methods (such as LDUH 5000U, three times daily, or LMWH ≥3400U/day) and elastic stockings or intermittent pneumatic compression devices (1C level) is recommended.
For general surgery patients at high risk of bleeding, mechanical prophylaxis methods should be used from the beginning, at least until the risk of bleeding is reduced (1A level).
For certain high-risk general surgery patients, including those undergoing larger tumor surgery, it is recommended to continue LMWH prophylactic treatment after discharge (2A level).

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
# Preoperative Recommendations for Warfarin Users
Discontinue warfarin 4 days before surgery, adjust INR to normal levels, apply prophylactic LDUH 5000U or LMWH, and encourage the patient to perform gastrocnemius exercises.
For patients with moderate thrombotic risk, discontinue warfarin 4 days before surgery, lower INR, and switch to prophylactic heparin 2 days before surgery.
For patients at high risk of thrombosis, reduce warfarin 4 days before surgery, and may switch to LDUH or LMWH 2 days before surgery.
For patients with a high risk of bleeding during surgery and a low risk of thrombosis, INR should be reduced to 1.3~1.5, and medication should not be discontinued (reduction in 4~5 days before surgery).
