**Introduction**

Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained arrhythmia in clinical practice, with a prevalence of over 6% in people over 60 years old and over 30% in those over 80 years old.

The most serious complication of atrial fibrillation is thromboembolism, with ischemic stroke being the most common.

Studies have shown that the risk of stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation is 5 times higher than that in patients without atrial fibrillation, and over 90% of thrombi in non-valvular atrial fibrillation originate from the left atrial appendage.

**Introduction**

The left atrial appendage is an ear-shaped small pouch extending from the left atrium, a remnant of the left atrium during embryonic development, and is part of the left atrium. It is located at the third intercostal space.

During sinus rhythm, the left atrial appendage rarely forms thrombi due to its normal contractile ability. During atrial fibrillation, the orifice of the left atrial appendage becomes significantly wider, and it loses effective regular contraction, leading to stasis of blood in the left atrial appendage, which is prone to thrombus formation.

Left atrial appendage occlusion is performed by using a special occluder to occlude the left atrial appendage, preventing thrombus formation within the left atrial appendage, thereby avoiding the occurrence of ischemic stroke and systemic embolism.

**Introduction**

**Indications for Surgery:**

(1) Patients with non-valvular persistent atrial fibrillation;

(2) Age over 18 years;

(3) High risk of cerebral embolism, CHADS2.VAS score ≥2, HAS.BLED score ≥3;

(4) Able to take aspirin and clopidogrel long-term, with contraindications to warfarin or unwilling to take warfarin long-term.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->

**Surgical Process**

Under general anesthesia, the entire process requires esophageal echocardiography. After successful transseptal puncture via the femoral vein, left atrial appendage angiography is performed to measure the diameter and depth of the left atrial appendage and compare them with the results of esophageal echocardiography. A watchman occluder of appropriate size is selected for occlusion. At the same time, the patient is heparinized, and the occluder is precisely located and confirmed to have no residual leak by esophageal echocardiography and left atrial angiography before releasing the occluder to isolate the intracavity space between the left atrium and left atrial appendage. Routine follow-up chest X-ray and esophageal echocardiography are performed postoperatively to observe the position and morphological changes of the occluder.
