(1) The Purpose of Preoperative Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Purpose: To prevent surgical site infections (SSI), including superficial incision infections, deep incision infections, and infections involving organs/cavities involved in surgery, but not including other sites of infection that may occur postoperatively and are not directly related to surgery.
×Inappropriate prophylaxis: Pneumonia, urinary tract infections, catheter-related bloodstream infections, etc.

### Notes:
The old version included systemic infections that may occur postoperatively, while the new version does not include them. It can be said that systemic infections that may occur postoperatively are not preventable by prophylactic medication, which can be understood to some extent as protection for doctors.

### Notes:
According to the degree of microbial contamination at the surgical incision, surgical incisions are classified into clean incisions, clean-polluted incisions, polluted incisions, and contaminated-infection incisions. The probability of infection in class I incisions is the same with or without medication.

1. Indications for Prophylactic Antibiotic Use
Clean-polluted surgery (class II incisions): The surgical site contains a large number of human resident flora, which may contaminate during surgery.
The surgical site may cause infection, and prophylactic antimicrobial drugs are required.
This includes upper and lower respiratory tract, upper and lower gastrointestinal tract, urogenital tract surgery, or surgery through these organs, such as oropharyngeal surgery, biliary tract surgery, total hysterectomy, rectal prostate surgery, and open fracture or trauma surgery, etc.

Polluted surgery (class III incisions): The surgical site has been severely contaminated, and prophylactic antimicrobial drugs are required.
This includes surgery involving acute inflammation without suppuration; gastrointestinal contents with obvious overflow contamination; fresh open wounds that have not been debrided in a timely manner; and significant defects in aseptic technique such as thoracotomy, cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

Contaminated-infection surgery (class IV incisions): Treatment with antimicrobial drugs has started before surgery and continues intraoperatively and postoperatively, which does not belong to the category of prophylactic use.
This includes old trauma surgery with necrotic tissue; surgery with existing clinical infection or organ perforation.

### Notes:
Class II incisions: Upper and lower respiratory tract, upper and lower gastrointestinal tract, urogenital tract surgery, or surgery through these organs, such as oropharyngeal surgery, biliary tract surgery, total hysterectomy, rectal prostate surgery, and open fracture or trauma surgery, etc.
Class III incisions: Surgery causing severe contamination of the surgical site, including: surgery involving acute inflammation without suppuration; gastrointestinal contents with obvious overflow contamination; fresh open wounds that have not been debrided in a timely manner; and significant defects in aseptic technique such as thoracotomy, cardiopulmonary resuscitation.
Class IV incisions: Old trauma surgery with necrotic tissue; surgery with existing clinical infection or organ perforation.

2. Selection of Antibiotic Species
Consideration should be given to the type of surgical incision, possible types of contaminating bacteria, their sensitivity to antimicrobial drugs, whether the drug can reach an effective concentration at the surgical site, and other factors.
Select species that are strongly targeted against possible contaminating bacteria, have sufficient evidence of prophylactic efficacy from evidence-based medicine, are safe, easy to use, and reasonably priced.
It is recommended to use a single antimicrobial drug for prophylactic use to avoid unnecessary combined use.
Prophylactic use should target the possible contaminating bacteria in the surgical pathway.
For example, cardiovascular, head and neck, thoracic and abdominal wall, extremity soft tissue surgery, and orthopedic surgery, which are surgical procedures through the skin, usually select antimicrobial drugs targeted against Staphylococcus aureus.
For colonic, rectal, and pelvic surgery, antimicrobial drugs targeted against Gram-negative bacteria in the intestines and anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides fragilis should be selected.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
2. Selection of Antibiotic Species
First choice: First and second-generation cephalosporins
Evidence-based: Cefazolin, cefuroxime
For patients with cephalosporin allergy: G+ bacteria can use vancomycin, teicoplanin, or clindamycin; G- bacilli can use aztreonam, fosfomycin, or aminoglycosides
For the prevention of MRSA, vancomycin should be used with strict control of the duration of use
It should not be arbitrarily selected to use broad-spectrum antimicrobial drugs as prophylactic drugs during the perioperative period
Strict control of fluoroquinolones as prophylactic drugs during the perioperative period of surgery

### Notes:

For certain surgical site infections that can cause serious consequences, such as heart valve replacement surgery, artificial joint replacement surgery, etc., if there is a possibility of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) colonization before surgery or if the incidence of MRSA is high in the institution, vancomycin, teicoplanin can be used for prophylactic infection, but the duration of use should be strictly controlled.
Considering that the resistance rate of Escherichia coli to fluoroquinolones is high in China, strict control should be exercised in the use of fluoroquinolones as prophylactic drugs during the perioperative period of surgery.

3. Timing of Administration

Intravenous administration should be given 0.5 to 1 hour before the incision of the skin and mucosa or at the start of anesthesia, and the operation should begin after the completion of the infusion to ensure that the local tissue has reached a drug concentration sufficient to kill the bacteria contaminated during the operation. (Consistent with the 2014 IDSA/SHEA version of the "Guidelines for the Prevention of Surgical Site Infections")

Vancomycin or fluoroquinolones, which require a longer infusion time, should be started 1 to 2 hours before the operation.

### Notes:
The 2014 IDSA/SHEA version of the "Guidelines for the Prevention of Surgical Site Infections" recommends the use of antimicrobial prophylaxis based on evidence-based standards and guidelines. It is recommended to start medication within 1 hour before incision to maximize the drug concentration in the tissue site. The 2008 NICE version of the UK guidelines suggests that antimicrobial drugs should be administered intravenously at the start of anesthesia, and earlier administration may be considered for surgeries using tourniquets. The 2009 Hong Kong Department of Health guidelines also suggest that the first dose should be used within 30 minutes before skin incision (in line with the time of anesthesia induction). The 1999 CDC version of the guidelines does not explicitly state the timing of medication.

4. Route of Administration

Most of the administration routes are intravenous, with only a few being oral. Administration via oral or intramuscular injection is not recommended due to individual differences in drug absorption and cannot guarantee effective plasma concentration.

Oral administration
For colorectal surgery, on the basis of systemic prophylaxis, oral antimicrobial drugs should be administered for intestinal preparation (fever, ABX guidelines: oral neomycin + erythromycin or metronidazole the day before surgery)
Artificial abortion - dilatation and curettage, induction of labor    doxycycline
Topical administration
Ophthalmic surgery     topical application of tobramycin or levofloxacin

### Notes:
The IDSA and SHEA guidelines in the United States suggest that oral combined parenteral antibiotic administration can be used for colorectal surgery to reduce the risk of SSI. There is currently no research supporting the additional reduction in SSI risk from mechanical bowel preparation, but there is data supporting the effectiveness of oral antibiotics combined with bowel preparation, while simple mechanical bowel preparation does not reduce the risk of SSI.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
5. Duration of Treatment

Maintenance Time: Coverage time includes the entire operation process
For clean surgeries with a short operation time (<2 hours), a single dose of preoperative medication is given.
For operations lasting more than 3 hours or more than twice the half-life of the drug used, or for adults with blood loss exceeding 1500ml, an additional dose should be administered during the operation.
The prophylactic use of antimicrobials for clean surgeries should not exceed 24 hours, and for cardiac surgery, it may be extended to 48 hours depending on the situation.
The prophylactic use time for clean-polluted surgeries and polluted surgeries is also 24 hours, and may be extended to 48 hours if necessary for polluted surgeries.
Extending the duration of medication does not increase the preventive effect, and the risk of drug-resistant bacterial infection increases when the duration of prophylactic medication exceeds 48 hours.

### Notes:
The IDSA/SHEA version of the guidelines and the Hong Kong Department of Health guidelines both indicate that the duration of prophylactic medication is usually not more than 24 hours, while the CDC version of the United States and the NICE version of the UK guidelines do not explicitly state the duration of antimicrobial prophylactic use.
For operations lasting more than twice the half-life of the drug used (from the time of preoperative medication administration—based on the United States guidelines for the prevention of surgical site infections)

Note: For clean surgeries, high-risk factors are indications for prophylactic medication, but they do not determine the postoperative medication time. Prophylactic medication should not exceed 24 hours regardless of the number of high-risk factors.
