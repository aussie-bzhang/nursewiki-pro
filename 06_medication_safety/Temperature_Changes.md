Introduction - Pathophysiology of Urination

1
Bladder
The bladder expands with increasing urine volume without significant pressure increase. When the bladder is full, the outlet resistance increases accordingly. The detrusor muscle of the bladder remains in a static state when the bladder is full. When the bladder is filled to a certain capacity and threshold, the stretch receptors initiate the micturition reflex, causing the detrusor muscle to contract reflexively and the urethral sphincter and pelvic floor muscles to relax. During normal voiding, the pelvic floor and urethra automatically relax, and the detrusor muscle continues to contract until the bladder is completely emptied. The sympathetic nervous system primarily controls bladder storage, while the parasympathetic nervous system controls bladder voiding. The somatic nervous system controls the lower urinary tract through its innervation of the pelvic floor and external urethral sphincter.

2
Urethra
The maintenance of normal urethral closure is primarily due to two factors: 1) the structure of the urethra itself, including the urethral wall striated muscle, urethral wall smooth muscle, urethral sphincter, and submucosal venous plexus; 2) the supporting structures below the urethra, including the levator ani muscles, pelvic fascia, and pelvic wall urethral attachments. This structure forms a "hammock" below the urethra, which tightens in response to increased intra-abdominal pressure. This "hammock" exerts a compressive force against the urethra. When the supporting structures below the urethra are defective, it can manifest as increased mobility of the urethra and bladder neck, and the urethra cannot be completely closed when abdominal pressure is increased, leading to stress urinary incontinence.

3
Micturition
Micturition is initiated and controlled by the central nervous system. The higher central nervous system has complex inhibitory and facilitatory effects on micturition, with the center located in the gray matter of the pons in the brainstem, commonly referred to as the pontine micturition center. The cerebellum is the center for coordinating the relaxation and contraction frequency and intensity of the pelvic floor muscles and detrusor muscle, and it has multiple connections with the brainstem reflex center.

Uterine Prolapse and Urinary Incontinence
The pelvic floor tissue supports the female reproductive system, suspending the urethra. Once the pelvic floor muscle fascia tissue is relaxed, it can lead to pelvic organ prolapse, changes in the anatomical position of the urethra and bladder, and a decrease in urethral resistance, ultimately causing stress urinary incontinence.

### Notes:

Diaphragmatic or Abdominal Breathing

PHOTO
Patients can take an upright, supine, or semi-recumbent position, placing their hands on the anterior chest or upper abdomen. When inhaling slowly through the nose, the diaphragm descends to its maximum extent, the abdominal muscles relax, the abdomen protrudes, and the hands feel the abdomen rising. When exhaling, exhale through the mouth, the abdominal muscles contract, the diaphragm relaxes, and the diaphragm rises with the increase in intraperitoneal pressure, pushing the pulmonary gas out, and the hands feel the abdomen descending.

Pursed-lip Breathing

PHOTO
The technique of pursed-lip breathing is to extend the exhalation time by creating a slight resistance through the pursed lips, increasing airway pressure, and delaying airway collapse. Patients inhale through the nose and exhale through pursed lips (like whistling) slowly, while contracting the abdomen. The ratio of inhalation to exhalation is 1:2 or 1:3. The size of the pursed lips is proportional to the exhalation flow rate.

Huffing and Puffing Coughing Sputum Removal Method

PHOTO
After taking a deep breath and inhaling as much air as possible, close the glottis, and simultaneously contract the chest and abdominal muscles. This increases the intrathoracic pressure, causing the glottis to suddenly open, producing a high-speed explosive exhalation that expels sputum from the airways, maintaining airway patency.

TR is not a serious complication, but it may cause inconvenience to both doctors and patients, and theoretically, it may damage detrusor function. According to statistics, the incidence of TR is 4.9% to 7.1%.
