# Definition
The International Association for the Study of Pain defines pain as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with tissue damage or the potential for tissue damage. Acute pain is a behavioral and physiological change caused by tissue damage, with a duration generally not exceeding 7 days.

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->
# Pain Classification
1. Acute pain (usually less than 3 months) — pain caused by trauma or surgery;
2. Chronic pain — pain that recurs after an injury or lasts for more than 3 to 6 months.

# Overview
Pain has become increasingly recognized as the fifth vital sign, following temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure, in the field of international medicine. In 2004, the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) designated October 11 as "World Pain Relief Day" and proposed that "the relief of pain is a basic right of patients." The WHO also states that "relief of pain is a basic right of patients." Surveys show that the incidence of moderate to severe pain in postoperative patients is 70% to 80%, but a significant number of these patients do not receive adequate treatment. The reasons for this include insufficient understanding of pain, individual differences in pain treatment, and issues related to treatment costs. With the research on the mechanism of pain, various analgesic drugs and techniques with different mechanisms of action have been sequentially applied in clinical practice, but the situation of postoperative acute pain has not been significantly improved. On the contrary, there are increasing reports of side effects from various analgesic techniques and drugs.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
# Hazards of Pain
Pain, characterized as nociceptive pain, is also the most common and most urgent acute pain that needs to be treated in clinical practice. Postoperative pain may be the most severe pain experienced by patients in their lifetime and is the main source of suffering for patients in the perioperative period. Poor control of postoperative pain is one of the risk factors for the development of chronic pain.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# Hazards of Pain
Fear of pain can lead to joint stiffness and muscle atrophy in the limbs; pain can cause an increase in heart rate and blood pressure; it also leads to the inability or reluctance to cough forcefully, which is not conducive to the excretion of respiratory secretions and can lead to complications such as pulmonary infection; it can lead to a decrease in gastrointestinal motility and a delay in the recovery of gastrointestinal function; prolonged bed rest, reduced activity, and the use of high doses of central analgesic drugs may weaken the movement of the urinary bladder and urethra, causing urinary retention; it can cause increased muscle tension, muscle spasms, limit body movement, and promote the formation of deep vein thrombosis; it can lead to insomnia, anxiety, panic, helplessness, depression, dissatisfaction, over-sensitivity, frustration, and melancholy.

# Non-pharmacological Analgesic Treatment
Local cold therapy, such as ice packs, is one of the most commonly used physical treatment methods, which uses substances below body temperature to act on the local skin to constrict small blood vessels, reduce the basic metabolic rate, slow down local blood circulation, and reduce vascular permeability, thereby achieving the purpose of cooling, hemostasis, analgesia, and anti-inflammatory. Other methods such as traditional Chinese medicine's acupuncture, massage, cupping, qigong, as well as the sound, light, electricity, magnetism, and heat analgesic instruments developed in modern Western science can all alleviate pain to some extent.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
# 1. Opioid Analgesics
Opioid analgesics can act on multiple sites, including peripheral nerves, spinal dorsal horn sensory neurons, the pain center in the brain and brainstem, and exert an inhibitory effect on pain. Among the traditional opioid analgesics, codeine, dextropropoxyphene, and oxycodone belong to weak opioids, while morphine, fentanyl, methadone, and diamorphine belong to strong opioids and are currently the main drugs for postoperative analgesic treatment in China.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# I. Opioid Analgesics
Although opioid analgesics have strong analgesic effects, they also have significant adverse reactions, which may include confusion (hallucinations), nausea and vomiting, delayed gastric emptying, constipation, respiratory depression, skin itching, and urinary retention. After the analgesic effect diminishes, the adverse reactions may still persist, which is related to the action of opioids on peripheral opioid receptors. Moreover, these drugs often have addictive potential if used improperly.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# I. Opioid Analgesics
When used for the treatment of chronic pain, the preferred route of administration for opioid analgesics is non-invasive (oral, transdermal, etc.). When the analgesic effect of non-invasive administration is insufficient, or in cases of acute severe pain due to surgery or trauma, intramuscular, intravenous, epidural, and PCA administration are also commonly used methods in orthopedic clinical practice.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# II. Local Anesthetics
Local anesthetics are used in perioperative analgesia treatment, primarily by acting on the peripheral nerves or the surrounding nerve endings to temporarily, completely, and reversibly block the generation and conduction of peripheral nerve impulses caused by noxious stimuli to the central nervous system, thus temporarily eliminating local pain sensation and preventing the central nervous system from becoming sensitized and developing pathological reorganization. The main drugs include bupivacaine and ropivacaine.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# III. Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) primarily inhibit the activity of cyclooxygenase (COX), reducing the formation of prostaglandins, thromboxane A2 (TXA2), and prostacyclin from arachidonic acid, thereby affecting the corresponding target sites to achieve analgesic effects. COX has two isoenzymes, COX-1 and COX-2. The analgesic mechanism of NSAIDs is related to the inhibition of COX-2 activity, while their adverse reactions are often due to the blockade of COX-1 receptors.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# III. Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)
Traditional NSAIDs (such as diclofenac, meloxicam, ibuprofen, etc.) can simultaneously inhibit COX-1 and COX-2, have significant adverse reactions, and are not suitable for perioperative analgesics. Selective COX-2 inhibitors (such as celecoxib) have minimal or no inhibition of COX-1 and less impact on TXA2, with fewer adverse reactions and better analgesic effects, and have become a common choice for clinicians. Currently, COX-2 inhibitors in clinical practice mainly include oral Celebrex and intravenous Tanezumab.

### Notes:

# IV. Steroidal Drugs
Steroidal drugs are used in clinical practice to assist in analgesia primarily by utilizing their anti-inflammatory effects, improving capillary permeability, inhibiting inflammatory factors, and reducing their damage to the body, which can reduce the need for opioids and their adverse reactions, promoting postoperative recovery. At the same time, the use of small doses of topical corticosteroids does not affect wound healing or increase the risk of infection. Commonly used corticosteroids include methylprednisolone and dexamethasone.

# Specific Measures for Orthopedic Perioperative Analgesia
1. Adhere to the WHO three-step treatment guidelines for oral administration, on-time administration, stepwise administration, individualized administration, closely monitor the effects of administration, the occurrence of adverse reactions, and handle them in a timely manner. 2. Prophylactic analgesia, which involves administering analgesic treatment before the occurrence of noxious stimuli, significantly reduces peripheral and central sensitization, thereby alleviating postoperative pain and reducing the dosage of analgesic drugs. 3. Multimodal analgesia with combined drug use, utilizing the advantages of different types of drugs, reducing the required dosage of various analgesic drugs, and improving pain relief through synergistic or additive effects, reducing side effects and toxic effects. 4. Individualized analgesia, as there are individual differences in patients' responses to pain and analgesic drugs, the methods of analgesia should be tailored to the individual, and fixed drug regimens should not be applied. Pay attention to special populations such as children and the elderly, and the ultimate goal of individualized analgesia is to achieve the best analgesic effect with the minimum dose.

# Common Methods of Perioperative Analgesia
Postoperative pain in orthopedic surgery is often severe. In addition to general non-invasive administration (oral, transdermal, mucosal, etc.), intramuscular and intravenous administration, nerve blocks, local infiltration at the surgical site, epidural, and PCA administration are also commonly used analgesic methods in orthopedic clinical practice.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
# (I). Nerve Block Analgesia
Nerve block analgesia involves injecting local anesthetics around the peripheral nerves, plexuses, or ganglia to block the conduction of sympathetic, sensory, and motor nerve signals, blocking the transmission of pain signals and achieving muscle relaxation. The advantages of nerve block analgesia are that it has a rapid onset of action, blocks the transmission of local pain signals to the central nervous system, and reduces the dosage of postoperative opioid analgesics. However, it has certain risks, including complications such as nerve and vascular injuries, and requires experienced physicians to effectively implement it.

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
# (II). Local Infiltration Analgesia at the Surgical Site
Local infiltration analgesia at the surgical site is currently often used as one of the analgesic methods for total joint replacement surgery due to its good analgesic effect, few adverse reactions, simple operation, and has been widely promoted in clinical practice. A "cocktail" mixture of analgesic solution, mainly composed of ropivacaine, morphine, and epinephrine (1:1,000) added to 100 ml of sterile normal saline, is used for local infiltration around the knee and hip joint capsules and incisions, with definite analgesic effects and few adverse reactions.

# (III) Epidural Analgesia
Epidural analgesia involves administering local anesthetics and analgesics through the epidural space to block the conduction of sensory nerve impulses along the posterior roots of spinal nerves.

# Multimodal Analgesia
The traditional postoperative analgesia model is individualized, with a single mode of drug use, leading to significant adverse reactions and complications. Today, multimodal analgesia in the perioperative period encompasses the concept of preemptive analgesia, which has been widely recognized in clinical practice. In a study by Xu Yan'an and others on multimodal and preemptive analgesia in the perioperative period of total knee replacement, the use of perioperative oral celecoxib and tramadol combined with intra-articular injection of a cocktail analgesic mixture during surgery was found to significantly alleviate early postoperative knee rest pain and activity pain, accelerate functional recovery of the affected limb after surgery, reduce the use of postoperative analgesics, and decrease the occurrence of adverse reactions caused by postoperative anesthetic drugs.

# Multimodal Analgesia
Weng Wenjie and others have shown that the perioperative multimodal analgesic regimen, which includes preoperative oral celecoxib and morphine, and intra-articular injection of ropivacaine, morphine, methylprednisolone, and adrenaline, and postoperative oral celecoxib, morphine, Tylenol, or Panadol, has complementary mechanisms of action, synergistic analgesic effects, can reduce the dose of each analgesic drug, reduce postoperative complications, and achieve the optimal ratio of efficacy to adverse reactions.

Under the leadership of the department head and the nurse manager, the authority of the entire organizational management has been strengthened, ensuring the implementation of various plans on one hand, and increasing patients' confidence in pain intervention on the other; a team composed of orthopedic physicians, nurses, patients, and their families participates together.

# Summary
An ideal orthopedic perioperative analgesia regimen should be an individualized, multimodal, multipoint analgesia at different phases, achieving a perfect fit between controlling adverse reactions and complications and achieving good analgesic effects. With a thorough understanding of the mechanisms of action of various analgesic drugs, clinical doctors can adopt an individualized multimodal analgesia regimen based on the type of surgery and individual patient differences before, during, and after surgery, which can reduce patient pain, reduce complications, accelerate postoperative functional recovery, and improve patient satisfaction.
