Types of Body Fluids
Intracellular fluid and extracellular fluid
Intracellular fluid is the fluid present within cells, while extracellular fluid is the fluid present outside cells.
Extracellular fluid includes the fluid present in the blood vessels, as well as the tissue fluid present between the tissue cells outside the blood vessels, and also includes some special secretions, such as cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, and gastrointestinal secretions.

# Fluid Transport
The transformation between intravascular and extravascular fluids is such that crystal fluids can freely enter and exit the blood vessels, while colloids, composed of large molecular substances, cannot pass through the vascular wall and therefore can only remain within the blood vessels. This results in an additional colloid fluid within the blood vessels, with the main colloid substances being albumin within the blood vessels.
The osmotic pressure of tissue fluid and crystal fluid within the blood vessels is equal, but there is a difference due to the presence of colloid osmotic pressure within the blood vessels. The presence of this colloid osmotic pressure is the reason why blood pressure is higher than tissue pressure. Therefore, in order to cause fluid flow and transfer between the intravascular and extravascular spaces, regulating colloid osmotic pressure is an important method.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
# The Role of Colloids ---- Blood Pressure
Of course, this is also an important way to increase blood pressure. The mechanism is as follows: when the colloid substances within the blood vessels increase-------the colloid osmotic pressure within the blood vessels increases---------the crystal fluid in the interstitial tissue moves into the blood vessels to reach the hypertonic area---------the total osmotic pressure within the blood vessels decreases-------finally, a new balance is reached between the fluid within and outside the blood vessels-------the fluid within the blood vessels increases-------within the limited space, the pressure on the vascular wall increases-------blood pressure increases.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
#
From the above, it can be seen that the functions of crystals and colloids are different. The function of crystal fluids is to regulate the fluid balance between the extracellular and intracellular spaces of tissue cells, while the function of colloid fluids is to regulate the fluid balance between the intravascular and extravascular spaces. At the same time, the fluid within the blood vessels carries nutrients and oxygen through the vascular wall to reach the tissue fluid. The nutrients and oxygen in the tissue fluid are then transmitted to the tissue cells through various methods. We must understand that all fluids exist and flow to serve the survival of tissue cells. Therefore, we cannot focus our attention solely on the fluids within the blood vessels and consider maintaining normal blood pressure as the only purpose of clinical doctors.

# (I) Water Metabolism
The body fluids within the human body are excreted from the body through four pathways.
 1. Urinary excretion: The daily urine volume is generally about 1000-1500mL. The daily urine volume should be at least 500mL, as the human body produces solid waste of 35-40g per day, and 1g solid waste can be excreted by 15 mL of urine.
2. Evaporation and sweating of the skin: The water lost through the skin each day is about 500mL. This amount of evaporation is relatively constant and does not decrease due to body dehydration. If there is sweating, more water is lost through the skin. If there is fever, the water lost through the skin will increase by 100mL for every 1℃ increase in body temperature.
3. Water loss through exhalation: A normal person loses about 400mL of water through exhalation each day. This amount of water loss is also constant and does not decrease due to body dehydration.
4. Digestive tract drainage: The daily secretion of digestive fluids is about 8200mL, of which most is reabsorbed, and only about 100mL is excreted in feces; gastric juice is acidic, while the rest is alkaline; the potassium in gastric juice is 3-5 times that of plasma.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
#
The total amount of water excreted from the body through various pathways is about 2000-2500mL. Among them, the evaporation of the skin (excluding sweating) and the water exhaled through the lungs are generally invisible and are called insensible losses.
    The water intake of a normal person is equal to the water excreted, and the daily water excretion is the water needed, about 2000-2500mL. These waters mainly come from drinking water of 1000-1500mL and the water contained in solid or semi-solid foods. The water generated by the body's oxidation process (endogenous water) is about 200-400mL. For an adult who cannot eat, 2000mL is the minimum physiological requirement after subtracting the endogenous water if there is no additional water loss.

# Reasons for Glucose Administration
In cases of major surgery, it is necessary to provide some glucose before surgery to perform adequate glycogen reserves. It is also necessary to perform glycogen reserves in surgeries performed in the afternoon, as the fasting time before afternoon surgery is very long, so more glycogen is consumed. At the same time, many anesthesiologists are unwilling to perform glucose administration during surgery, which aggravates the degree of gluconeogenesis in patients, produces many harmful substances, increases the burden on the body, and is unfavorable to the condition.
The glycogen reserve in normal adults is about 300-400 grams.
The glycogen reserve in diabetic patients is lower, so glucose administration should be performed in surgeries in the morning and afternoon. Glucose administration should always be accompanied by insulin during glucose administration. As for the issue of the amount of glucose administration, it can be appropriately controlled, but in the case of critically ill patients with poor food intake, glycogen consumption is negligible. In addition, due to the long duration of surgery, glucose administration is necessary.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
# Precautions for Glucose Administration
4-5g of glucose can be added with 1 unit of insulin, which not only increases glycogen reserves but also does not cause hyperglycemia. This increases the safety of surgery.
500ml of 10% glucose contains 50g of sugar. If necessary, to increase the efficiency of glucose administration, 50% glucose can be added appropriately, but the concentration of sugar should not be too high, and it should be controlled below 20% as good.

This is a very common question.
The fluids for fluid administration include crystal and colloid fluids.
And crystal fluids are divided into salt and sugar.

# Appropriate Colloid?
If you use only crystalloid solutions, isn't it true that (due to the reduction of colloid substances like albumin in intraoperative bleeding) the colloid osmotic pressure within the vascular system decreases, and the pressure difference between the crystalloid osmotic pressure of the tissue fluid and the colloid osmotic pressure decreases? The fluid cannot be retained effectively in the vascular system for a long time, and blood pressure cannot be maintained well. Moreover, if too much fluid is infused, a large amount of fluid will flow into the tissues and not be retained in the vascular system, which can cause various types of tissue edema. Edema in the brain tissue is particularly dangerous. Therefore, it is necessary to replenish an appropriate amount of colloid.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# The Use of Colloids
The function of colloids is to attract a portion of the crystalloid from the tissues into the vascular system, which can:
Maintain blood pressure
Maintain vascular tone
Maintain the balance of fluid between the vascular and extracellular spaces, preventing tissue edema.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# What's Too Much
However, excessive administration of colloid solutions is also not advisable, as this would attract a large amount of water from the tissues into the vascular system, leaving the tissues dehydrated, and simultaneously, blood pressure may increase. But tissue perfusion would necessarily be impaired. Therefore, the ratio of crystalloid to colloid should be proportional.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Just Right
The ratio of crystalloid to colloid should be proportional.
What ratio is appropriate depends on the situation.
It mainly depends on whether there is a deficit of water in the tissues or within the vascular system. If there is a deficit within the vascular system, the colloid ratio should be increased; if there is a deficit in the tissues, the crystalloid ratio should be increased.
The general crystalloid to colloid ratio is 2—4:1.

# Can There Be Too Much Water Between Tissues Without the Cells Being Dehydrated?
The movement of water between the intracellular and extracellular spaces depends on the crystalloid osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid.
When the crystalloid osmotic pressure is high, intracellular fluid moves towards the extracellular space, even if there is a deficit of water within the cells.
Conversely, when the osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid is low, the extracellular fluid moves towards the region of relatively higher osmotic pressure within the cells, even if there is no deficit of water within the cells.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
# How to Regulate the Crystalloid Osmotic Pressure of Extracellular Fluid?
Crystalloid osmotic pressure is formed by metal ions, mainly sodium and potassium ions (sodium is mainly in the extracellular fluid, and potassium is mainly in the intracellular fluid), so we generally use sodium chloride solutions for infusion.
0.9% sodium chloride is isotonic, meaning it has the same osmotic pressure as intracellular fluid.
Glucose has a hypertonic osmotic pressure initially, but it is quickly metabolized in the human body and becomes a hypotonic fluid, so glucose is used to reduce the osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid.

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
# Difference Creates Movement
It is clear that only when there is a pressure difference between the intracellular and extracellular osmotic pressures, there is water movement between the tissue cells, otherwise, there is no such movement, even if the intracellular and extracellular fluids are isotonic. This explains the importance of glucose infusion, as it is the only way to reduce the osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid, and only when the osmotic pressure of the interstitial fluid decreases can water move from the extracellular space to the intracellular space, thus replenishing the intracellular water deficit.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
# Tension
Isotonic solutions refer to solutions where the osmotic pressure caused by the electrolytes is equal to that of plasma, allowing red blood cells suspended in it to maintain normal volume and shape. The term "tension" actually refers to the osmotic pressure caused by particles that cannot pass through the cell membrane.
For example, NaCl cannot freely pass through the cell membrane, so 0.9% NaCl is both isotonic and isotonic solution; however, urea, because it can freely pass through the cell membrane, a 1.9% urea solution is isotonic with plasma, but red blood cells placed in it will immediately hemolyze. Therefore, it is not an isotonic solution. Additionally, 5% glucose is also isotonic, but the glucose in the body is rapidly oxidized and provides energy or is stored as glycogen, quickly becoming a non-tension solution and cannot maintain osmotic pressure, so it is considered a non-tension solution for fluid replacement and partial heat supply.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
# Watering Because of Dehydration, So Always Use Low Tension Fluids
If there is a hyperosmolar dehydration, a low tension fluid (usually 1/3 tension fluid) must be prepared.
If there is a hyposmolar dehydration, a high tension fluid (2/3 tension fluid) must be prepared.
If there is an isotonic dehydration, an isotonic fluid (usually 1/2 tension fluid) must be prepared.

# Why "Salt Before Sugar"
The principle of "salt before sugar" is to initially supplement isotonic saline when unsure of the type of dehydration. The mechanism is as follows: when there is hyposmolar dehydration, the osmotic pressure of the extracellular interstitial fluid decreases, causing water to move out of the cells. Infusing isotonic saline can neutralize the low osmotic interstitial fluid, thereby increasing the osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid, even if it does not reach normal levels.
When the extracellular fluid is hypertonic, isotonic saline can lower the osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid. The opposite effect occurs. Therefore, from this perspective, the principle of "salt before sugar" for fluid administration makes sense.
Because if you infuse glucose first, it may further decrease the already low crystalloid osmotic pressure of the extracellular fluid, increasing the potential for further movement of water into the extracellular space, thus exacerbating tissue cell edema. This can be dangerous (this acute transfer of water is prone to accidents).

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
# Why "Crystalloid Before Colloid"
The mechanism is as follows: during dehydration, it is the tissue cells that are affected, not the vascular system. If you infuse colloid first, the colloid cannot leave the vascular system, which increases the colloid osmotic pressure within the vascular system, which in turn increases the total osmotic pressure within the vascular system. Water from the extracellular space between tissues will flow into the vascular system, thus indirectly increasing the osmotic pressure between tissues. Water will move out of the cells, not correcting the dehydration but rather causing further dehydration.
Additionally, colloid solutions have a high viscosity, and in cases of increased blood viscosity, using colloid first can easily lead to the risk of vascular embolism.

Large volumes of fluid supplementation can easily lead to hypokalemia. However, orthopedic patients generally do not require potassium supplementation on the first postoperative day due to the large amount of cell destruction and subsequent release of blood potassium. # Characteristics of Physiological Dehydration
Under normal circumstances, skin sweating, urine output, and pulmonary insensible evaporation all exist, but this part is included in the physiological requirement amount, which is the source of fluid supplementation for physiological needs (usually 2000ml). We can see that physiological losses are all hypotonic fluids or pure water. Therefore, every day, including ourselves, every individual, is in a state of hyperosmotic dehydration. It is just that we drink water every day to alleviate the thirst caused by hyperosmotic dehydration.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
# Characteristics of Dehydration in Orthopedic Patients
Orthopedic surgical patients generally have such problems with fluid loss, including bleeding and tissue fluid leakage, which are of isotonic fluid loss; the second is the evaporation of water due to the prolonged exposure of the incision, which of course leads to a hyperosmotic change in body fluids; the third is the acceleration of pulmonary insensible evaporation and water loss due to tracheal ventilation under general anesthesia, which also leads to a hyperosmotic change. The only source of fluid supplementation is the intraoperative fluid administration by the anesthetist. Overall, the nature of fluid loss during surgery is hyperosmotic, which is the reason why many patients experience significant thirst after surgery. Therefore, the general principle of fluid supplementation after surgery should be the administration of low-tension or isotonic fluids. Moreover, the total amount of supplementation should be much more than the visible amount of fluid loss, due to the acceleration of insensible evaporation and the presence of insensible evaporation within the incision.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
# Thirst as a Good Indicator
At this point, you need to ask the patient if they have thirst. If there is thirst, it indicates that the patient is dehydrated. If there is no thirst, it indicates that dehydration is not significant or the patient is not dehydrated, or has been overhydrated.

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->
#
Some physicians are worried about the heart function and other issues in the elderly and believe that fluid supplementation should be minimized. This is very wrong because the buffering capacity of the elderly is weak, and insufficient or inappropriate fluid supplementation can easily lead to problems (such as slow blood flow due to inadequate volume, which is prone to embolism, and heart problems due to inadequate volume or water and electrolyte imbalance). We emphasize accuracy rather than the least amount, as placing anyone in an abnormal internal environment is definitely detrimental, regardless of the purpose. Of course, we are not ignoring heart function, and it must be considered. Since a patient has been able to withstand your surgery and has not been placed on the operating table, it means that there is still a certain degree of tolerance, and it is generally not likely to lead to heart failure after a few bottles of fluid. Of course, we still need to take necessary measures, first of all, to accurately calculate the fluid volume, secondly, to administer fluids at a moderate rate, and thirdly, to eat and take oral electrolyte solutions as soon as possible.

# Do Not Ignore the Pulse Pressure Difference
Accurate calculation of fluid volume must start with the anesthetic record, which contains comprehensive records including blood loss, urine output, fluid administration, and composition, as well as the amount of colloid, salt, and sugar. You must also know the blood pressure upon returning to the ward after surgery, including diastolic and systolic blood pressure, and do not ignore the pulse pressure. Low blood pressure often indicates inadequate volume. In this case, attention should be paid to a situation where both systolic and diastolic blood pressures are within the normal range, but the pulse pressure difference is large, indicating inadequate volume. The mechanism is that in the early stage of volume depletion, vascular contraction is good, and under the influence of good vascular tension, systolic blood pressure remains normal. However, in the diastolic phase, due to the decrease in blood volume, blood pressure is low when the vessels dilate, resulting in an increased pressure difference.

# Indicators for Blood Transfusion
Large blood loss
Anemia or hypoalbuminemia
Severe infection
Coagulation abnormalities
The total blood volume of a normal person is approximately 7%-8% of body weight, or equivalent to 70-80ml per kilogram of body weight, of which plasma accounts for 40-50ml. There are 400-500 million red blood cells, 4000-11000 white blood cells, and 150,000-400,000 platelets per cubic millimeter of blood. Additionally, for people of the same weight, those who are thin have slightly more blood volume than those who are overweight, and men have more blood volume than women.

# Definition of CVP
It refers to the pressure at which blood enters the right atrium through the superior and inferior vena cava.
Composition: ① Right ventricular filling pressure ② Intravascular wall pressure
③ Pressure acting on the extravascular wall ④ Venous capillary pressure
It mainly reflects the preload of the right ventricle and blood volume, and does not reflect left heart function.

# Normal Values and Clinical Significance of CVP
CVP < 5cmH2O: Indicates inadequate blood volume
CVP > 15cmH2O: Indicates heart failure, excessive venous vasoconstriction, or increased pulmonary circulation resistance
CVP > 20cmH2O: Indicates congestive heart failure

| CVP | BP | Clinical Significance | Management Method |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Low | Normal | Mild hypovolemia | Appropriate fluid resuscitation |
| High | Low | Cardiac insufficiency/relative volume overload | Cardiac support, vasodilation |
| High | Normal | Vascular constriction, increased PVR | Vasodilation |
