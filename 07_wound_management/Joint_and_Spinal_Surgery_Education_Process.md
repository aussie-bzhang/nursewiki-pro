## Important Notes
Health education may not be conducted when patients have the following conditions, and it should be carried out after the patient's condition stabilizes:

## Preoperative and Postoperative Health Education for Cervical Spine Surgery
Preoperative:
1. Respiratory management: 
   - Quit smoking: reduce tracheal inflammation, avoid sputum blockage leading to asphyxia and endangering life.
   - Prevent colds: avoid coughing causing wound pain, cervical movement, and fever leading to postoperative infection, affecting the surgical outcome.
   - Practice deep breathing and guide effective coughing to prevent postoperative respiratory infection.
2. Position training: 
   - Practice tracheal retraction training one week before anterior cervical surgery, 3-4 times a day, for 5-10 minutes each time. Practice prone position for posterior cervical surgery to improve tolerance during surgery.
   - Train bed-based urination and defecation to prevent postoperative urinary retention and incontinence.
   - Train methods of moving the body and changing positions on the bed.
   - Wear a neck collar of appropriate size.

3. Examinations: Cooperate with the doctor to complete various auxiliary examinations before surgery. Cross-matching of blood is necessary one day before surgery if required, and drug sensitivity tests are performed according to medical advice.

## Preoperative and Postoperative Health Education for Cervical Spine Surgery
Postoperative:
1. Position: 
   - Maintain cervical immobilization, use a cervical collar to fix the neck. When moving the patient, ensure that the head, neck, shoulders, and trunk are on the same level, and the neck is in a natural neutral position. Strictly straighten the patient's body during postoperative turning.
   - Obese patients have the risk of poor drainage and nerve compression, so it is better to sleep in multiple lateral positions, with the pillow for the neck and face at the same height as the shoulder.
   - Replace the dressing over the surgical site if there is bleeding or leakage.

2. Monitoring: Postoperative electrocardiogram monitoring and observation of vital signs until stable, especially for high cervical surgery, pay special attention to bradycardia, blood pressure drop, and unstable respiratory function. Focus on observing respiratory rate, rhythm, depth, and signs of hypoxia.

3. Drainage: Keep the wound drainage tube unobstructed and accurately record the color and quality of the drainage fluid. # Postoperative Recovery Guidance
One, on the first postoperative day (24 hours):
1. Resting Position:  Anterior cervical surgery: After 6 hours of surgery, rest in bed, raise the head of the bed to 30 degrees, which is conducive to reducing incision bleeding. Change positions every 2 hours to prevent pressure sores. Can lie on the back or on the side; when lying on the side, the pillow should be at the same height as the shoulder.  Posterior cervical surgery: Can lie on the back or on the side after 6 hours of surgery. Special attention must be paid to not pressing the posterior part of the neck when lying on the back, without a pillow or towels. Lateral position is preferred to avoid problems with the posterior neck wound and internal cervical structure due to compression, affecting the surgical outcome; it can also avoid the incision drainage tube being pressed, leading to blood accumulation in the wound. When lying on the side, the pillow should be at the same height as the shoulder; a pillow that is too high or too low can cause severe discomfort or pain due to neck twisting. For patients with kyphosis, lateral position is preferred; when lying on the back, attention should be paid to adjusting the height of the neck and back pillow. Change positions every 2 hours to prevent pressure sores.

# Postoperative Recovery Guidance
 2. Neck Stabilization: Wear a neck brace to limit neck movement, avoiding extension and flexion of the cervical spine, including turning the head and nodding. Pay attention to maintaining the neck in a neutral position when turning over, but avoid neck twisting.
3. Diet: After the patient wakes up from general anaesthesia, they can eat and drink 4-6 hours later, mainly easy-to-digest liquid and semi-liquid foods.
4. Postoperative Pain Relief: The pain from cervical surgery incisions is usually mild, and routine oral medication combined with a pain pump is sufficient. Injectable analgesics may be used if necessary.

# Postoperative Recovery Guidance
5. Key Observations: Vital signs, respiratory function, drainage tubes, and limb nerve function.  ECG monitoring—observe blood pressure, pulse, heart rate, and blood oxygen saturation.  Respiratory—observe for symptoms such as sore throat, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and difficulty breathing. Milder cases can be treated with nebulization inhalation and oxygen therapy, which usually resolves within 2-3 days. Tracheotomy may be necessary if necessary.  Drainage tube—observe for patency, the color, quality, and quantity of the drainage fluid; to prevent excessive blood accumulation in the wound causing a hematoma or infection. Adjust the drainage tube if necessary.  Nerve function—observe the sensory and motor function of the limbs, ask and observe the sensory and motor status of the hands, feet, and lower limbs every half hour. If numbness worsens and the range expands, especially if the activity strength and range of the lower limbs decrease, report to the medical staff in a timely manner. Start limb function exercises, which can be combined with massage and elastic stockings to prevent deep vein thrombosis of the lower limbs.

# Postoperative Recovery Guidance
Two, on the second to third postoperative day (24-72 hours):
1. Drainage tube:
The purpose of the drainage is to remove old blood clots from the wound. Generally, the drainage volume is less than 50 milliliters within 24 hours, and the doctor will consider whether to remove the drainage tube based on the patient's condition.

2. ECG monitoring:
If the vital signs such as blood pressure, pulse, heart rate, and blood oxygen saturation are stable, the ECG monitoring can be removed on the second postoperative day. Position and neck stabilization: Can wear a neck brace to side lie, get out of bed, and it is better to get out of bed after the tube is removed. It is best to use a walker to practice walking first, and then gradually walk normally, but avoid extension and flexion of the cervical spine, including turning the head and nodding.

3. Diet:
Mainly consume easily digestible semi-liquid foods. If there are no symptoms such as sore throat, normal diet can be resumed. Eat more calcium-rich, vitamin-rich, and high-protein foods to promote wound healing.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Postoperative Recovery Guidance
4. Respiratory:
Nebulization inhalation can be combined to alleviate respiratory symptoms.

5. Nerve function:
Continue to observe the sensory and motor function of the limbs, especially 3-4 hours after the drainage tube is removed. Pay attention to the changes in the sensory and motor function of the limbs, dizziness, headache, palpitations, chest tightness, nausea, and vomiting, and communicate with the doctor in a timely manner.

6. Re-examine the anteroposterior and lateral X-ray films of the cervical spine to observe the cervical spine alignment and the position of the bone graft and steel plate screws.

# Postoperative Recovery Guidance
Three, on the fourth to twelfth postoperative day:
Wear a neck brace when getting up, side lie, and perform some auxiliary functional exercises of the limbs. During this period, postoperative cervical spine CT and MRI examinations are performed to better understand the patient's condition and judge the surgical outcome, facilitating later rehabilitation and follow-up.

The patient is generally discharged between the 7th and 12th postoperative day. Generally, sutured incisions are removed one week after anterior cervical surgery and 12 days after posterior cervical surgery, and can be removed at the outpatient department or near the home.

# Postoperative Recovery Guidance

Specialized Nursing
1. Start effective cough training as soon as possible after surgery: Instruct the patient to actively cough and expectorate, and when coughing, press the chest, instruct them to take a deep breath, and use an explosive force to expel sputum from the deep lung. Blowing up a balloon can be trained.

2. Position training: Generally, after the patient wakes up from general anaesthesia, they can raise the head of the bed with a neck collar, and when the drainage tube is removed and the condition is stable, they can be assisted by family members to assume a semi-recumbent position. If there are no symptoms such as dizziness, they can get out of bed to move around. Severe unstable fractures require strict bed rest for 3 months.

# Discharge Instructions
1. Discharge escort: Prevent neck trauma, especially during car deceleration activities, which can cause neck anterior and posterior movement and lead to injury. Therefore, lying flat is recommended when returning home after discharge.
2. Head and neck position and stabilization:  Continue to wear the neck collar for 3 months after surgery, pay attention to timely cleaning the collar, adjust the tightness moderately, place a small towel inside to ensure comfort, and prevent skin injury.  Maintain the neck in a neutral position, the normal curvature of the cervical spine.  Prohibit actions such as tilting the head forward, tilting the head back, and rotating the neck.  Avoid prolonged watching TV, using computers, reading newspapers, and prevent neck overexertion.  Avoid high pillows, maintain the neck in a functional position.
3. Discharge follow-up: Generally, a follow-up examination is required within 3 months, once every 6 months, and once a year. If there are symptoms such as redness, pain, and discharge from the wound, visit the doctor in a timely manner, and follow up as needed.

# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
Phase 1: (1-5 days postoperatively)
1. Supine straight leg raising exercise and lower limb flexion-extension exercise: to prevent nerve root adhesion (active movement is the main, passive movement is auxiliary). Start with 30° for the first time, keep for 15 seconds, and gradually increase the angle of the leg lift daily, 10 times per group, 2-3 groups per day.
2. Ankle joint dorsiflexion and plantar flexion exercise: supine position, actively extend and flex the ankle joint to the maximum extent and perform resistance training. Hold each action for 10 seconds, repeat 20 times per group (more than 10 minutes), 3-4 groups per day.

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
Phase 2: (5-14 days postoperatively, mainly lumbar back muscle exercises)
1. 5-point support method: (5-7 days postoperatively) Lie flat on a hard bed, use the head, feet, and elbows to support 5 points, raise the buttocks as high as possible. Hold for 10 seconds, repeat 20 times per group, 2-3 groups per day.
2. 3-point support method: (7-9 days postoperatively) Lie flat on a hard bed, use the head and feet to support 3 points, raise the buttocks as high as possible. Hold for 10 seconds, repeat 20 times per group, 2-3 groups per day.

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
3. 4-point support method: i.e., bridge support method (9-10 days postoperatively) Lie flat on a hard bed, use hands and feet to support the entire body, forming a bridge shape. Hold for 10 seconds, repeat 20 times per group, 2-3 groups per day.
4. Flying Swallow method: (10-15 days postoperatively) Lie prone on a hard bed, extend the head, upper limbs, and lower limbs backward, minimizing the area of the abdomen in contact with the bed, forming a flying swallow shape. Hold for 10 seconds, repeat 20 times per group, 2-3 groups per day.
   (Note: Severe lumbar fractures are unstable fractures. Appropriate lumbar back muscle exercises should be performed to achieve a certain degree of reduction of the fracture. Finally, ambulate with lumbar corsets or other supports.)

# Discharge Instructions
1. Continue to implement the exercises learned in the hospital after discharge, selectively, and the frequency and duration depend on the specific situation. Gradually increase the amount of exercise, with certain intervals during exercise, avoiding overexertion of the waist.
2. Do not use a lumbar corset continuously for more than 3 months to avoid muscle atrophy due to disuse.
3. Avoid vigorous activities and lifting heavy objects within 3-6 months. Try to avoid prolonged sitting, running, jumping, and avoid sleeping on soft beds. When lifting heavy objects from the ground, take a squatting position with knees bent to lift. Establish a good lifestyle, change sitting positions frequently, and strengthen lumbar back muscle exercises for more than half a year to enhance lumbar muscle and spinal stability. Reduce the occurrence of chronic lumbar pain, prevent lumbar injury, and lumbar intervertebral disc herniation follow-up.
4. Enhance nutrition and maintain a good mood.
5. Pay attention to warmth and avoid cold stimulation.
6. For simple lumbar compression fractures, ambulate after 8-12 weeks post-injury, do not bend over, practice bending over after 3 months, and participate in appropriate labor after 4-6 months.

# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
First stage (within 24 hours postoperatively)
1. Instruct family members to massage the calf muscles of the affected limb (from the foot to above the knee joint) to help with blood return to the affected limb and prevent the formation of deep vein thrombosis.
2. Under the guidance of a nurse, perform deep breathing exercises and hip flexion exercises (note that the angle of hip flexion should be as per the doctor's instructions), 2-3 times a day, each for 5-10 minutes. Also, perform ankle flexion and extension exercises.

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
Second stage
4. On the second day postoperatively, patients can assume a sloping bed position with the head of the bed raised no more than 45° to avoid dislocation of the hip joint. Under guidance, perform exercises to tighten and relax the thigh muscles, 10 repetitions per set, 3 sets per day, 3 times a day.
5. From 3 to 5 days postoperatively, if the patient has no fever and the condition is stable, they can get out of bed under guidance, first practicing standing, and if there is no dizziness, they can practice walking with crutches. Note: The angle of hip flexion when getting out of bed should not be too large, and the affected limb should not bear weight. Practice twice a day in the morning and evening, and gradually increase the number of times. Start bearing weight gradually after 3 months.
6. After getting out of bed, perform hip abduction and leg lifting exercises as required, paying attention not to cross the body's midline.

# Perioperative Health Education for Knee Surgery
Postoperative:
1. Position: 
   - The affected lower limb should be elevated in a neutral position to prevent swelling of the surgical limb, with the knee flexed between 15º-30º to avoid external rotation of the limb and compression of the common peroneal nerve.

# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
1. Phase 1: Postoperative 1-3 days
   - Quadriceps static contraction exercises: Use the foot to perform up and down hook and press actions, 10 times every hour, each action lasting for 3 seconds. The healthy knee is flexed, and the affected knee is fully extended to perform bed pressing actions. At this time, the quadriceps muscle contracts, the knee is flattened, and the patella can move slightly up and down. Practice one set every 2 hours, 30 times per set, lasting for 10-15 seconds.

<!-- Slide number: 52 -->
# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
2. Phase 2: Postoperative 4-7 days
   - Active exercises: 
     - The patient holds the upper thigh with both hands and lifts it to perform flexion activities, 5-10 times every 2 hours.
     - The patient lies on the side with the affected limb on top, performing gravity-free knee flexion and extension actions, 5-10 times every 2 hours.
     - The patient sits on the edge of the bed and practices: place the healthy (or one side) foot and lower leg on the affected (or the other side) foot ankle, performing up and down pressing actions. The healthy (or one side) foot hooks the affected (or the other side) heel, assisting the affected (or the other side) lower leg in lifting actions.

# Postoperative Rehabilitation Guidance
3. Postoperative 8-14 days
   - Bed straight leg raising exercises: Raise the leg to 30º, ensuring the knee is extended and the back is flattened, hold for 5-7 seconds, practice 3-4 sets daily, 30 times per set. A soft pillow can be placed under the affected limb to help support, gradually reducing the pillow height. Under the supervision of the patient's family, practice walking on flat ground with crutches, with the knee bearing about 10 kg, practice 3-4 times daily, 10-20 minutes per time.

# Discharge Guidance
⑴ Continue to strengthen quadriceps muscle contraction and relaxation exercises, perform leg extension and lifting activities. Also, strengthen knee exercises such as stepping, cycling, and climbing stairs.
⑵ Avoid strenuous exercise, do not perform jumping exercises, do not make sudden stops or rotations when walking, and maximize the lifespan of the prosthesis.
⑶ Prevent and control infections in a timely manner to prevent bacterial blood-borne transmission leading to joint infection. Add clothes as needed when the weather turns cold to avoid catching a cold.
⑷ Reduce wear and tear on the artificial joint and prevent falls. The patient should use a cane for life, especially when going out, to seek help from others.
⑸ Instruct the patient to strengthen diet, eat more high-protein, high-calcium, and easily digestible foods, but maintain an appropriate weight.
Engage in outdoor activities appropriately, get plenty of sunlight to prevent osteoporosis.
⑹ Postoperative follow-up schedule: Once a month within the first six months. If there are pain or other discomforts, seek medical attention immediately.
