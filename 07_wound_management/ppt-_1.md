Treatment of Uterine Fibroids
Treatment should be considered comprehensively based on the patient's age, fertility requirements, symptoms, and the location, size, and number of fibroids.
Observation and follow-up: Fibroids are small, asymptomatic. Generally, no treatment is required, especially for women approaching menopause. Uterine fibroids can often shrink or gradually disappear after menopause, and should be followed up every 3-6 months.
Medication: Fibroids smaller than 2 months of gestational uterine size, mild symptoms, near menopausal age, or patients with poor general condition who are not suitable for surgery.
Surgical treatment: Uterus larger than 10 weeks of gestational size; heavy menstrual bleeding, secondary anemia; bladder or rectal compression symptoms; rapid growth of fibroids; failure of conservative treatment; infertility or repeated miscarriage after excluding other causes. Surgical approaches can be abdominal, vaginal, or hysteroscopic and laparoscopic surgery, with procedures including: fibroid resection, hysterectomy.

Perioperative Nursing Care for Uterine Fibroids
Perioperative period: It includes the time before, during, and after surgery, and generally for elective major surgery, the perioperative period is from admission to about one month after surgery.
The purpose of perioperative nursing: To improve the patient's tolerance to surgery, reduce the occurrence of postoperative complications, and promote the smooth recovery of the body.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Preoperative Nursing
The period from the patient's admission to the time of surgery is known as the preoperative period.
The main nursing work during this period is:
         Assessment and guidance of physical and mental status, preparation of local skin, preparation of the digestive tract, preparation of the bladder and vagina.
Psychological support:
         Patients are bound to have various concerns before surgery, often manifested as insomnia, anxiety, increased heart rate, elevated body temperature, and blood pressure. Patients always hope that the surgeon is highly skilled. In response to this psychological state, the author actively introduces the qualifications of the surgeon, carefully answers and explains the various questions raised by the patient, so that they can eliminate tension, fear, and other unease, and welcome surgery in a good mental state.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Preoperative Guidance
A comprehensive assessment of the patient must be conducted before surgery, and targeted guidance should be provided
1. Patients who are to undergo hysterectomy must understand that they will not have menstrual periods after surgery, and that temporary amenorrhea may occur due to temporary changes in sex hormone levels caused by the operation (for fibroid resection), or that they can no longer become pregnant.
2. Explain to the patient in simple and understandable language the necessity of enema, skin preparation, vaginal preparation, TCT examination, necessary fractional curettage, preoperative fasting, preoperative medication, retention of urinary catheter, and early mobilization, as well as their role in preventing postoperative complications and promoting recovery.
3. Carefully manage preoperative comorbidities
For example: anemia, malnutrition, hypoalbuminemia, etc. The preoperative nutritional status directly affects the postoperative recovery process, and attention should be paid to guiding the patient to consume a high-protein, high-vitamin, high-calorie, and low-fat diet, as well as foods rich in iron, such as pork liver, eggs, red dates, green vegetables, etc. The nurse can assist in formulating a diet plan or menu according to the patient's dietary habits to ensure that the body is in the best preoperative nutritional condition.

Nursing Care on the Day Before Surgery
On the day before surgery, carefully check medical orders and make the following preparations:
1. The range of skin preparation should be from the bottom of the sternum to the upper 1/3 of the thigh, both sides to the midaxillary line, and the perineum (note the navel). Attention should be paid to using soap and hot water to clean, and to not scratch the skin when shaving.
2. Gastrointestinal preparation: On the evening before surgery, consume easily digestible liquid food such as millet congee, and fast from food on the morning of surgery to prevent aspiration pneumonia and asphyxia due to nausea and vomiting during surgery, as well as to prevent postoperative bloating. Clean the intestines with warm soap water enema (the amount is about 500-1000ml, depending on the patient's tolerance) on the evening before surgery to empty the intestines, which is conducive to surgery.
3. Other: Perform blood typing, blood cross, and blood matching according to the patient's physical condition. Take sedative-hypnotic drugs as prescribed the night before surgery to ensure that the patient can have sufficient sleep and rest before surgery.

Nursing Care on the Morning of Surgery
Generally, it is advisable to visit the patient as soon as possible, check vital signs, inquire about the patient's self-perceived feelings, and notify the doctor immediately if there are any abnormal conditions such as menses.
Remove the patient's removable dentures, hairpins, jewelry, and valuable items and hand them over to the family for safekeeping. Long-haired patients should braid their hair.
Vaginal preparation: Use iodophor original liquid cotton balls to wipe the vagina in the morning.
Routine placement of urinary catheter to maintain unobstructed drainage.
Thirty minutes before surgery, administer the basic anesthetic drug as prescribed. After the patient arrives in the operating room, prepare the anesthetic bed according to the type of surgery and anesthetic method, and prepare monitoring equipment and emergency supplies.

Postoperative Nursing
Basic Requirements
1. Closely observe and record changes in the patient's condition, promptly identify problems, cooperate with the doctor's examination, and handle them in a timely manner.
2. Take various nursing measures to alleviate the patient's pain and discomfort, allowing for a quick recovery.
3. Have predictive care (predictive nursing) to prevent various complications that may occur after surgery.
4. Help the patient solve temporary difficulties after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
Common Symptoms and Complications Postoperatively
Wound pain, nausea, vomiting, bloating, urinary retention, constipation, anorexia, dizziness, insomnia, cough, fever, vaginal bleeding and discharge, abdominal incision infection, hematoma, and dehiscence, vaginal stump infection, internal hemorrhage, respiratory tract infection, urinary tract infection, pelvic inflammation, thrombophlebitis, and bedsores.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
General Nursing
1. General Nursing: Check the wound dressing, intravenous access, pressure sores, secure the drainage tubes, maintain unobstructed drainage, and understand the intraoperative situation.
2. Positioning: Place the patient in the appropriate position according to the anesthesia method, usually in a semi-recumbent position the following morning after surgery. Semi-recumbent positioning is beneficial for abdominal drainage, can relax the abdominal wall muscles, reduce incision tension, and alleviate pain. After assuming the semi-recumbent position, the abdominal organs descend, and the diaphragm's range of movement increases, which enhances lung capacity and facilitates coughing and expectoration. Semi-recumbent positioning keeps the urethral orifice at the lowest position of the bladder, reducing the residual urine volume in the bladder and thereby reducing the risk of urinary tract infection. Regardless of the position, after the anesthesia wears off and the patient becomes conscious, the patient should be assisted in turning at least every two hours to reduce the chance of adhesions.

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
Observation of Condition
1. Pay attention to the patient's facial color, mental state, expression, and vital signs changes, and make rounds every 15-30 minutes until the condition is stable.
2. Observe the incision for bleeding, oozing, or dressing detachment, and if any, find the cause promptly.

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
Observation of Condition
3. Observe changes in urine output:
During the separation of adhesions, the traction of the bladder and ureter can affect the postoperative urinary function. Therefore, postoperatively, it is important to maintain the patency of the indwelling catheter and carefully observe the urine output and nature. The postoperative patient should have an hourly urine output of at least 50ml, usually the catheter is removed 48 hours after a subtotal hysterectomy, 72 hours after a total hysterectomy, and 24 hours after myomectomy. Before removal, the patient should be instructed to clamp the catheter at regular intervals to exercise bladder function and prevent urinary retention. If the hourly urine output is less than 30ml, accompanied by a gradual decrease in blood pressure, a rapid pulse, the patient is restless or reports back pain or a feeling of rectal prolapse, consider there may be intra-abdominal hemorrhage.
4. Vaginal bleeding: There may be a small amount of vaginal bleeding 2-3 days after surgery, dark red in color; for patients who have undergone a hysterectomy, there may be a small amount of pale red bleeding 10-14 days after surgery due to the absorption of the catgut suture at the vaginal stump, lasting for about a week, which does not require special treatment.
In the observation, any abnormal condition should be reported to the physician promptly to take appropriate measures.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
Dietary Care
If there is no nausea or vomiting 6 hours after surgery, clear liquid can be given. Subsequently, change to semi-liquid or soft food according to the recovery of intestinal function. Before the intestinal function recovers, avoid milk and soy milk to prevent intestinal gas production and bloating.

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
Early Mobilization
Early mobilization after surgery helps promote the recovery of the entire body, improve blood circulation, prevent thrombosis, promote gastrointestinal motility, prevent bloating and constipation, promote the recovery of urinary function, and prevent urinary retention, etc.
When getting up for the first time, family members or nurses should support the patient in standing and pay attention to the patient's facial color and pulse to prevent orthostatic hypotension (the three-step process of getting up).

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
Discharge Instructions
Abstain from sexual activity and bathing in the bath for 2 months after surgery, avoid strenuous exercise, heavy physical labor, and prolonged standing for 3 months, maintain smooth defecation, and return for a follow-up examination one month after surgery. If any discomfort or abnormal symptoms occur at any time, seek medical attention promptly.
