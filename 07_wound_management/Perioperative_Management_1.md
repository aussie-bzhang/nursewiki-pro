#
Perioperative period: Generally refers to the period from admission to the decision to undergo surgery and the basic recovery after surgery, which varies in duration depending on the size of the surgery.
Surgery is an important means of treating diseases in surgery, but surgery and anesthesia are both invasive and can bring varying degrees of psychological and physiological burden to patients.
Comprehensive examination of patients, taking appropriate measures according to the situation, making adequate preoperative preparations, enabling patients to have sufficient psychological preparation and good physical conditions, in order to ensure safer surgery and reduce postoperative complications, and promote early recovery.

# 第一节 Preoperative Preparation
Preoperative preparation refers to the period from admission or the decision to undergo surgery to the start of anesthesia and surgery, using various measures to make the physiological function of patients undergoing surgery approach normal, improve their tolerance to surgery, and lay a foundation for the smooth progress of surgery and the reduction of postoperative complications, as well as early recovery.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->

Elective surgery: Can be performed at an appropriate time while making full preoperative preparations.

Surgical classification

Time-limited surgery: Necessary preoperative preparations should be made in a timely manner, and surgery should be performed within a certain period.

Emergency surgery: Surgery that needs to be performed very quickly after diagnosis is confirmed.

# One General Preparation
(One) Psychological preparation
Patient's psychological preparation: Eliminate fear and anxiety
1. Conduct thorough research and discussion on the diagnosis, surgical methods, possible complications and preventive measures.
2. Explain to the patient and family the importance of surgery, the possible effects, risks, potential complications, postoperative recovery process, and prognosis, gain the patient's trust and cooperation, and sign the informed consent form.
3. Instruct the patient on how to maintain good psychological quality to ensure the success of the surgery.

         1. Exercise to adapt to changes after surgery
                ① Practice urination and defecation in bed
                ② Teach the patient the correct methods of coughing and expectoration
                ③ Stop smoking 2 weeks before surgery
          2. Correct water, electrolyte, and acid-base balance disorders

4. Prevent infection
Indications for prophylactic use of antibiotics:
(1) Surgery involving infected foci or incisions close to the region
(2) Gastrointestinal surgery; surgery involving large blood vessels
(3) Operations with long duration and significant trauma
(4) Open wounds, contaminated or with extensive soft tissue injury, the interval between trauma and debridement is long, or debridement is difficult and incomplete
(5) Cancer surgery;
(6) Organ transplantation surgery; surgery requiring the implantation of artificial products

# Two Special Preparation
One Anemia and Malnutrition
         1. Hypoproteinemia patients can be transfused with albumin, plasma, etc., striving to reach Hb 100g/l, and albumin 30g/l.
         2. For those who are allowed to make longer preparations, parenteral or enteral nutritional support can be provided.

Two Hypertension
1. Hypertension < 160/100mmHg can undergo surgery
2. For patients with high blood pressure, appropriate antihypertensive drugs should be selected before surgery to maintain blood pressure at a stable level, but it is not required to be reduced to normal before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Three Cardiovascular Disease
     1. Long-term low-protein diet and diuretic use, electrolyte disorders must be corrected.
     2. Severe anemia should be corrected with blood transfusions in small and frequent doses to improve the patient's oxygen-carrying capacity.
     3. Arrhythmia patients should control heart rate within the normal range. Elderly patients with coronary heart disease with heart rate below 50 beats per minute can be corrected with atropine, and a temporary pacemaker can be used if necessary.
     4. Acute myocardial infarction patients cannot undergo surgery within 3 months of onset; those without angina pectoris for more than 6 months can be operated under strict monitoring.
     5. Heart failure patients: surgery can be performed 3-4 weeks after heart failure is controlled.
     6. Patients taking anticoagulant drugs for a long time need to have INR tested before surgery, and surgery should be stopped if abnormal.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
㈣ Respiratory Dysfunction
1. For patients with a history of smoking, stop smoking 2 weeks before elective surgery and encourage them to practice deep breathing and coughing.
2. Use bronchodilators such as ephedrine and aminophylline and inhaled isoproterenol to have a good effect on obstructive pulmonary dysfunction, which can increase lung capacity.
3. Use expectorants for patients with thick sputum.
4. For patients with chronic inflammation, appropriate antibiotics should be used 3-5 days before surgery to control infection, perform ultrasonic nebulization inhalation, drain sputum, and inhale oxygen at low flow.
5. For patients with frequent asthma attacks, dexamethasone can be taken to reduce bronchial mucosal edema; elective surgery should be postponed during the acute phase of asthma.
6. Perform surgery after acute respiratory tract infections are completely controlled, and antibiotics should be used in emergencies.
7. Severe pulmonary dysfunction and concurrent infection can only be operated on after symptoms are improved.
8. Pay attention to preoperative medication to avoid respiratory depression and difficulty in expectoration.

#
⑤ Liver Disease
1. Provide adequate preoperative preparation to improve liver function.
2. Increase protein supply.
3. Supplement a variety of vitamins, especially vitamin K.
4. Serum albumin should reach 35g/L, and the prolongation of prothrombin time should be corrected.
5. Acute hepatitis, except for emergency rescue, is generally not suitable for surgery; patients with obvious malnutrition, ascites, and jaundice are generally not suitable for any surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
⑤ Renal Disease
1. Further examination and treatment of complications before surgery to reduce postoperative complications.
2. Maximize renal function improvement before surgery.
3. Control infection, and dialysis if necessary.
4. Avoid using nephrotoxic drugs.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
⑤ Adrenal Cortex Insufficiency
1. Start using corticosteroids 2 days before surgery.
2. Choose the method and timing of drug discontinuation according to the patient's condition during and after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
⑤ Diabetes Mellitus
1. Control blood sugar and correct fluid imbalance before surgery.
2. Use antibiotics before contaminated surgery.
3. Stop antidiabetic drugs and long-acting insulin before surgery and switch to insulin to control blood sugar.
4. Avoid prolonged fasting before surgery to prevent ketogenesis.
5. Monitor blood sugar after surgery and adjust insulin dosage.

1. Inquire carefully about the medical history, physical examination, and blood tests.
2. Perform appropriate treatment and consult with relevant departments before elective surgery.
3. Blood products must be administered in emergency surgery.

# Four Preparations for Emergency Surgery
Focus on asking medical history and physical examination
Emergency preoperative preparation
For patients with multiple injuries, treat life-threatening losses or complications
For patients in shock, antishock treatment is required
Do not delay the operation due to preoperative preparation
For critically ill patients, minimize movement and complex examinations

# Routine Management
First, Positioning
General anesthesia: supine position, head turned to one side.
Lumbar puncture: supine position without pillow for 12 hours after surgery to reduce headache.
Epidural anesthesia: should lie flat for 4-6 hours, no need to remove the pillow.
Different postoperative positions:
Craniocerebral surgery: take a 15-30-degree head-high foot-low slope position.
Cervical and thoracic surgery: mostly adopt a high semi-recumbent position, which is conducive to breathing.
Abdominal surgery: mostly adopt a low semi-recumbent position to reduce the tension of the abdominal incision.
Spinal or gluteal surgery: often adopt supine or prone position.
For shock patients: raise the lower limbs by 15-20 degrees, and raise the head and trunk by 20-30 degrees.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Routine Management
Second, Management of Catheters and Drainage Materials
Commonly used drainage materials include: cigarette drain, rubber sheet drain, rubber tube drain, double lumen drain, T-tube drain, gastrointestinal decompression tube drain, and urinary catheter drain, etc.
The specific choice is determined according to the surgical site, condition, and purpose.
Regularly check for blockage, kinking, and dislodgment of drainage tubes. If the drainage fluid is thick, negative pressure aspiration can be used. Change the dressing in a timely manner and observe and record the changes in drainage volume and color.
The removal of drainage materials: decide according to the specific situation

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
# Third, Activity
Postoperative activity should be started as soon as possible, and the amount and range of activity should be gradually increased.
Bedridden activity: after the anesthesia has worn off and the patient has become conscious, deep breathing exercises, effective expectoration, turning over, and limb flexion and extension exercises can be performed.
Out-of-bed activity: generally starts 2-3 days after surgery. First, sit on the edge of the bed and perform deep breathing and coughing, then stand and walk by the bed, gradually increasing the range, frequency, and duration of activity.
Advantages of early mobilization:
Increase lung ventilation volume, reduce pulmonary complications;
Improve blood circulation, prevent venous thrombosis;
Promote intestinal peristalsis and bladder contraction function recovery, reduce bloating and urinary retention.
Not suitable for early mobilization:
Special fixation and restraint in orthopedic surgery, shock, heart failure, severe infection, craniocerebral surgery, post-thoracotomy, liver repair surgery, abdominal hernia repair, and renal surgery.

# IV. Diet and Intravenous Fluids
Non-abdominal surgery
After local anaesthetic minor surgery, there is no strict dietary restriction.
Patients can eat after 4-6 hours post-lumbar or epidural anaesthesia.
Patients under general anaesthesia should only eat when the anaesthesia has worn off and there is no nausea or vomiting.

Abdominal surgery
After gastrointestinal surgery, fasting and fluid restriction are generally required for 24-48 hours. The intestinal function recovers on the 3rd to 4th day, and eating can resume after the anus has passed gas. Start with easily digestible fluids, gradually progressing to semi-liquid foods, and finally returning to normal diet. During fasting, water, electrolytes, and nutrition are supplemented intravenously.

**(I) Pain**
The pain is most severe within 24 hours postoperatively and subsides after 2-3 days.
If the incision pain persists or worsens, be alert for incision infection.
Explain the cause and duration of incision pain. Secure the drain to prevent displacement and incision traction pain. Instruct the patient to press on the wound area when turning over, taking deep breaths, or coughing. Distract the patient's attention. Administer analgesics orally or by intramuscular/subcutaneous injection as prescribed. Acupuncture: Acupuncture at the points Quchi, Hegu, Neiguan, Zusanli, and Sanyinjiao can relieve pain.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
**(II) Fever**
1. **Causes**:
   - Surgical fever: Within 3 days postoperatively, the temperature rise is about 1 degree.
   - Within 24 hours postoperatively: Metabolic or endocrine abnormalities, hypotension, atelectasis, and transfusion reaction.
   - Between 3-6 days postoperatively: Be alert for infection.
2. **Management**: Clear diagnosis and targeted treatment.

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
**(III) Nausea and Vomiting**
1. **Causes**: The most common cause is anaesthetic reaction. Other causes include diabetic acidosis, hypoxia, intracranial hypertension, uremia, hypokalemia, hyponatremia, acute gastric dilatation, etc.
2. **Management**: Assist with positioning, tilting the head to one side. Encourage deep breathing and active swallowing to suppress the vomiting reflex. Treat according to different causes. Acupuncture at the points Neiguan, Zusanli, Zhongwan, and Tianshu can improve symptoms.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
**(IV) Abdominal Distension**
1. **Causes**: Gastrointestinal function suppression and intestinal tract gas.
   - Other causes include paralytic ileus, mechanical ileus, and hypokalemia.
2. **Management**: Gastrointestinal decompression and anal tube evacuation. Abdominal heat application and anal tube evacuation. Use gastrointestinal motility drugs. In cases of abdominal infection leading to ileus or confirmed mechanical ileus where non-surgical treatment is ineffective, reoperation may be required. Strychnine sulfate 0.5mg at the point Zusanli by point injection; Dahuang Decoction enema; oral fennel seeds; external application of芒硝 on the abdomen.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
**(V) Hiccup**
1. **Causes**: Stimulation of the diaphragm by mechanical or inflammatory factors, usually transient but sometimes stubborn, affecting the patient's rest and incision healing.
2. **Management**: Compression of the supraorbital margin, or acupuncture at the points Tianchu, Neiguan, Zhongwan, Zusanli, etc. Inhale CO2, aspirate gas and fluid from the stomach. In cases of refractory hiccup after upper abdominal surgery, prevent subdiaphragmatic infection. Refractory hiccup may require cervical diaphragmatic nerve block or traditional Chinese medicine treatment.

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->
**(VI) Urinary Retention**
1. **Causes**: Reflex spasm of the bladder sphincter caused by incision pain. The micturition reflex is suppressed after general anaesthesia or intraspinal anaesthesia. Patients are not accustomed to urinating in a lying position.
2. **Management**: Provide comfort, alleviate concerns, and assist with urination. Use methods such as listening to running water, massaging the lower abdomen, heat application, and helping the patient sit on the edge of the bed or stand to urinate. If ineffective, perform sterile catheterization and insert a urinary catheter for 1-2 days. Acupuncture at the points Guanyuan, Qihai, Zhongji, Shuidao, Sanyinjiao, and Yanglingquan.

**Section III: Prevention and Treatment of Postoperative Complications**

<!-- Slide number: 39 -->
# I. Postoperative Hemorrhage
**Etiology and Clinical Manifestations**:
Incomplete or inadequate hemostasis during surgery, such as the suture line loosening of ligated vessels; coagulation dysfunction in the patient.
The following situations require close attention:
   1. Drainage blood exceeding 100ml per hour, lasting for several hours.
   2. Abdominal distension or progressive respiratory difficulty, severe swelling at the surgical site, and unexplained acute anemia.
   3. Early postoperative signs of hemorrhagic shock.

<!-- Slide number: 40 -->
**Preventive Measures**:
Ensure thorough hemostasis during surgery, flush with saline at the end of the operation, and tie off each bleeding point carefully. For larger vascular bleeding, suture or double suture for hemostasis.
Actively prevent infection postoperatively to reduce secondary hemorrhage.
In case of postoperative hemorrhage, blood transfusion should be administered immediately, and preparation for reoperation for hemostasis should be made.
After rehemostasis, continue to closely monitor to prevent recurrent hemorrhage.

<!-- Slide number: 41 -->
# II. Pulmonary Complications
**Etiology and Pathology**:
Commonly occurs after major thoracic or abdominal surgery, especially in the elderly, long-term smokers, and those with acute or chronic respiratory tract infections.
Anaesthetic and sedative agents used during and after surgery suppress the respiratory tract's sputum expectoration function.
Incision pain, postoperative gastrointestinal distension, and prolonged bed rest.
Postoperative wound pain restricts respiratory movement, causing secretions to accumulate at the base of the lungs, alveoli, and bronchi, blocking the bronchi, leading to atelectasis. Atelectasis causes hypoxia. Accumulation of secretions is prone to infection.

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
**Clinical Manifestations**:
Begins with restlessness, tachypnea, and tachycardia within 2-3 days postoperatively.
Severe cases may be accompanied by cyanosis, hypoxia, and even hypotension.
Patients often have cough, but thick sputum is difficult to cough up.
Infection may cause fever and an increase in the total white blood cell count.
Percussion of the affected side of the lung is dull, and respiratory sounds are absent, sometimes presenting as tube-like respiratory sounds.
Chest X-ray or透视 can confirm the diagnosis.

<!-- Slide number: 43 -->
**Preventive Measures**:
Stop smoking 2 weeks before surgery, practice deep breathing, and treat primary infections;
Suction aspiration before extubation under general anaesthesia, appropriate tightness of chest and abdominal binders, warming to prevent upper respiratory tract infection;
Effective gastrointestinal decompression to reduce the impact of gastrointestinal distension on respiration.
Clearing sputum is the key, including turning and patting the back, positioning sputum expectoration, and expectoration.
Coughing by pressing on the trachea, hand pressing on the sides of the wound, and nebulized expectoration.
Sputum aspiration with a bronchoscope, and tracheostomy aspiration.
Ensure adequate fluid intake; in cases of pneumonia, appropriate antibiotics may be used.

# 三. Urinary Tract Infection
Etiology and Pathology: Secondary to urinary retention. Cystitis is the most common. Bladder infection can retrograde upwards, causing pyelonephritis.
Clinical Manifestations: Bladder irritation signs: frequent urination, urgency, and dysuria, sometimes accompanied by difficulty in urination. If chills, fever, and renal area pain occur, it indicates that there is already an infection in the renal pelvis.
Preventive Measures:
Proper prevention and treatment of urinary retention are key to reducing urinary tract infections.
For those already infected, alkalinize the urine, increase fluid intake. Local physiotherapy, hot compresses, and oral antispasmodic drugs can be used simultaneously, and systemic antibiotics can be administered. Indwelling catheterization should be performed for residual urine of 500 ml or more, and strict aseptic technique should be followed.

# Four. Incision Infection
Etiology and Pathology:
Due to the reaction after tissue destruction, local exudation, and absorption of hematoma, it is called surgical absorption fever. It returns to normal within 2-3 days and does not require special treatment.
Three to four days after surgery, if the incision pain does not subside, even worsens, or subsides again and then re-worsens, and is accompanied by an increase in body temperature.
Exogenous infection, operating room air, surgical instruments, dressings, and other supplies. Rough surgery, extensive tissue damage, incomplete hemostasis, hematoma in the wound, dead space, foreign body retention, poor wound protection, or dressing slippage, decreased resistance, etc.

# Five. Incision Dehiscence
Etiology and Pathology:
About 1 week after abdominal surgery
Old age, weakness, malnutrition, chronic anemia, etc., poor incision healing
Local tension is too great, incision hematoma and suppurative infection
Sutures are too fine, not tightly tied, the peritoneum is torn during suturing
Sudden coughing, strenuous defecation, and vomiting, postoperative gastrointestinal distension.

# Six. Deep Vein Thrombosis of the Lower Limb
Severe consequences, should be paid attention to: early can cause acute pulmonary embolism, late can complicate lower limb deep vein dysfunction.
Etiology: venous wall injury, slow blood flow, and increased blood coagulability.
High-risk groups: elderly, long-term bed rest, obesity, oral contraceptives, hip or pelvic surgery, malignant tumors, and varicose veins, etc.
Clinical Manifestations:
Initially, it is mostly deep vein thrombosis in the lower leg, manifested as pain and tenderness in the gastrocnemius area.
Subsequently, some patients may extend upwards to involve the iliac-femoral vein, manifested as lower limb swelling, pale skin, accompanied by superficial vein varicosity, and tenderness in the popliteal or femoral canal.
Severe cases may involve both deep and superficial veins in the lower limb, manifested as thrombophlebitis.
If complications occur, chills, fever, tachycardia, and an increased white blood cell count may occur.

# Seven. Acute Liver Function Insufficiency
Etiology: due to massive liver cell necrosis and severe liver function damage caused by general anesthesia, surgery, shock, infection, etc., severe cases can lead to liver failure. Manifested as jaundice, ascites, altered consciousness, even hepatic encephalopathy. It is divided into: pre-hepatic, hepatic cellular, and post-hepatic.

# Eight. Stress Ulcer
Almost all major surgeries, severe diseases, especially when complicated with shock, severe infection, and multiple organ dysfunction, the gastric and duodenal mucosa appears diffuse and superficial ulcers, leading to massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding.
Treatment: 1. Eliminate the cause, blood transfusion, fluid resuscitation, control infection.
      2. Place a gastric tube, aspirate the gastric contents, ice water + adrenaline or local hemostatic drugs.
      3. Systemic use of acid-suppressing drugs.
      4. Gastroscopy
      5. Surgery

# Management of Infected Incisions
- If no abscess has formed: change dressings, physiotherapy, and hot compresses, while using anti-inflammatory and symptomatic treatment.
- If an abscess has formed: open the incision, ensure adequate drainage, strengthen dressing changes until healing, and simultaneously use a sufficient and effective amount of antibiotics, and enhance nutritional intake.
