# Overview

Chronic tonsillitis often develops from recurrent episodes of chronic tonsillitis or due to poor drainage of the tonsillar crypts, leading to the proliferation of viruses within the crypts and subsequent chronic inflammation. It can also occur after certain acute infectious diseases.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
# Etiology
1. Streptococcus and Staphylococcus are the main pathogenic bacteria.
2. Recurrent acute tonsillitis causes necrosis of the epithelium in the crypts, with bacteria and inflammatory exudates accumulating, leading to poor drainage and the development of the condition.
3. Secondary to other infections.
4. Associated with autoimmune reactions.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# Pathological Changes
Hyperplastic - More common in children. The tonsillar lymphoid tissue hyperplasia, lymphoid follicles increase, connective tissue increases, tonsils chronically congested and enlarged.

Fibrotic - More common in adults. Tonsillar lymphoid tissue atrophy, interstitial fibrotic scar tissue hyperplasia, crypt orifices blocked, tonsils become smaller and tough.

Cryptic - The main lesion is deep within the tonsillar crypts, lymphoid follicles present chronic inflammation, lymphoid tissue scarification. Due to the crypt orifices being blocked by scar tissue, drainage is poor, leading to significant expansion of the crypts, or a large amount of desquamated epithelium, bacteria, lymphocytes, and leukocytes accumulating to form pus clots. Due to the severity of the condition, complications are more likely to occur, also known as chronic purulent tonsillitis.

# Physical Examination
Examination shows chronic congestion of the fauces and tonsils, mucosa dark red.
Surface may be smooth, or uneven, or lobulated. Sometimes linear scars, expanded crypt orifices, caseous plugs, or submucosal yellowish-white spots may be seen.
Using a tongue depressor to compress the tonsils laterally on the fauces, secretions may溢出 from the crypt orifices.
Tonsils often adhere to the anterior and posterior arches due to inflammatory changes; enlarged lymph nodes can be felt under the mandible.

# Treatment Measures
Currently, tonsillectomy (tonsillectomy) remains the main treatment method.

Other methods such as crypt irrigation, electrocautery, immunotherapy, etc., are uncertain in efficacy and are only recommended for patients with surgical contraindications.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# Indications
(1) Recurrent acute episodes of chronic tonsillitis.
(2) History of peritonsillar abscess.
(3) Overly large tonsils causing difficulty in swallowing, breathing, leading to nutritional disorders.
(4) Patients with rheumatic fever, glomerulonephritis, arthritis, rheumatic heart disease, etc., suspected of tonsils as foci.
(5) Due to tonsil and adenoid hypertrophy affecting the function of the eustachian tube, causing chronic effusive otitis media, which is ineffective after conservative treatment.
(6) Corynebacterium diphtheriae carriers, ineffective after conservative treatment.
(7) Unexplained persistent low-grade fever, while chronic tonsillitis exists.
(8) Various benign tonsillar tumors, for malignant tumors, careful case selection should be made.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# Contraindications
(1) Generally, surgery is not performed during acute tonsillitis episodes, and it should be 3-4 weeks after inflammation subsides.
(2) Hematological diseases, hypertension, cardiac insufficiency, active pulmonary tuberculosis, etc., are not suitable for surgery.
(3) Surgery is not recommended when systemic symptoms such as rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis are not controlled.
(4) Temporarily not suitable for surgery during poliomyelitis, influenza, menstrual periods, and pregnancy.
(5) In families with a high incidence of immunoglobulin deficiency or autoimmune diseases, and in patients with a white blood cell count below 3000.

# VII. Preoperative Nursing
1. Upon admission, the patient is sent to the designated bed. Introduce the ward environment and relevant regulations to the patient. Introduce the attending physician and nurse. Establish a nurse-patient relationship to alleviate preoperative anxiety.

2. Measure body temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, and weight.

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
# Preoperative Nursing (2)
3. Maintain a clean, comfortable, quiet, and fresh-air environment in the ward.

4. Male adult patients should have their beards shaved before surgery. Strengthen oral hygiene before surgery, gargle with mouthwash 4-6 times a day.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
# Preoperative Nursing (3)
5. Psychological care: For pediatric patients, try to do a good job of安抚 work before surgery to ensure cooperation with medical staff.

6. For patients with rheumatism, rheumatic heart disease, arthritis, or nephritis, antibiotics should be used 3 days before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
# Preoperative Nursing (4)
7. Assist in checking coagulation, and for those with prolonged coagulation time, oral vitamin C and vitamin K should be administered.

8. NPO (nothing by mouth) for local anesthesia patients on the day of surgery; for children, NPO for 4-6 hours; for general anesthesia patients, NPO for 12 hours before surgery and 8 hours before drinking, and ensure the patient has an empty bowel and bladder before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# Preoperative Nursing (5)
9. On the evening before and the morning of surgery, administer sedatives, and intramuscularly inject atropine 30 minutes before surgery. The former is to ensure the patient has a good rest and stable emotions; the latter is to relax the muscles and reduce saliva secretion. Note that the patient may experience dizziness and blurred vision after intramuscular injection of the preoperative needle, and should rest in bed. The time for intramuscular injection of the preoperative needle has now been changed to after entering the operating room.
10. Remove and properly store the patient's dentures before entering the operating room.
11. Prepare a bedside bowl, prepare an anesthetic bed, oxygen, and a multi-lead ECG monitor.

# General Nursing
1. Position: Postoperative patients under general anesthesia should lie flat with the head elevated and turned to one side to allow oral secretions and blood to flow out naturally, preventing aspiration. General anesthesia patients who have become conscious or local anesthesia patients can lie on their side or semi-reclining.
2. Measure body temperature 4 times a day. Patients may have a low fever (around 38℃) within 48 hours postoperatively, which is a normal postoperative absorption fever. If a high fever is detected, notify the doctor immediately.

3. For patients with respiratory distress, closely monitor respiration. If a sudden change in condition is detected, emergency treatment can be administered first, and the doctor should be notified immediately.

<!-- Slide number: 30 -->
# Nursing for Bleeding
On the day of surgery, instruct the patient to rest quietly, speak less, cough gently, and gently spit out oral secretions and blood without swallowing; at the same time, apply cold compresses to the neck to reduce bleeding.
For patients under general anesthesia who are not yet conscious or sleeping, frequent swallowing should be observed for wound bleeding.
Closely monitor the patient's facial color, pulse, and blood pressure.

# Nursing for Pain
Psychological care: Postoperative incision pain is a normal phenomenon and is within the range of tolerance.
Pain can be relieved by distracting attention with methods such as listening to music or chatting.
Apply cold compresses to the angle of the mandible or neck 24 hours after surgery to stop bleeding and relieve pain. If necessary, intramuscular analgesics can be administered.

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
# Wound Care (1)
Observe the wound, a white membrane begins to form 6 hours after surgery, is fully formed within 24 hours, and gradually peels off in about 10 days. Pay attention to the color of the mucosa in the throat, for swelling, pseudomembrane, and purulent secretions.
If the white membrane is grayish white and there is a strong halitosis, it indicates incision infection and should be treated as soon as possible.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
# Wound Care (2)
Start rinsing the mouth the day after surgery, rinse after each meal. First, rinse out food residue with water, then rinse with mouthwash.
Choose mouthwash based on the patient's oral pH value, commonly used ones include chlorhexidine gluconate—broad-spectrum antibacterial, 0.1% metronidazole—anti-anaerobic, and Cepaiyi Ganyin liquid—anti-inflammatory, to reduce bleeding.
Method: Hold a certain amount of water in the mouth, close the mouth, and vigorously move the cheeks and lips, move the tongue, so that the water can fully contact the wound, teeth, gums, and mucosa, repeatedly rinse the oral cavity so that all parts of the oral cavity can be rinsed. This can remove food debris and bacteria trapped in the gaps between the teeth.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
# Wound Care (3)
Start "three more" the day after surgery: encourage more talking, more rinsing, and more drinking and eating to increase resistance and prevent scar contracture.
Administer medication accurately and on time as prescribed, and rationally use antibiotics.

<!-- Slide number: 38 -->
# Health Education
1. Avoid eating hard and rough foods for two weeks after surgery, and eat nutritious and light soft foods.
2. Rinse the mouth after eating to maintain oral cleanliness.
3. Pay attention to rest, appropriate exercise, and a balance between work and rest to improve the body's resistance.
4. Inform the patient that the exfoliation of the white membrane from the mouth is a normal phenomenon and there is no need to be alarmed.
5. Avoid colds, coughs, and other respiratory infections. If there are symptoms such as fever, throat pain, and blood-stained secretions in the mouth, seek medical attention promptly.
