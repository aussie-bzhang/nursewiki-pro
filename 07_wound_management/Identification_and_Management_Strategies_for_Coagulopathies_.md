# I. Surgical Management of Patients with Hereditary Hemostasis Defects
1. Hemophilia A (hemophilia A)
   Hemophilia B (hemophilia B)
Patients with hemophilia face "spontaneous bleeding" every day and should generally avoid surgery.
Any surgical procedure performed on hemophilia patients should be considered a "heroic act" with a "sign" of "disaster," as the outcome is often "catastrophic," and most patients die from "bleeding."

2. Types of coagulation factors to be supplemented for hemophilia-like patients undergoing surgery:
Inputting whole blood can only increase FVIII to 10% and FIX to 20%, which is not advisable ("World War II")
Cryoprecipitate can increase FVIII to any expected level (1960s)
FVIII and FIX concentrates can increase FVIII and FIX to any expected level (1970s)

Calculation of dosage for coagulation factor replacement therapy:
    Calculated based on body weight or plasma volume
    Total blood volume = 7% of body weight
    Plasma volume = Total blood volume × (1 - hematocrit)
    The expected increase in FVIII:C activity

Example: A severe hemophilia A patient with VIII:C <1%, plasma volume of 3000ml, who wants to undergo abdominal surgery, requiring FVIII:C activity to reach 100%, then needs to input 3000U FVIII:C (1U/ml can reach 100% VIII:C activity), which is the loading dose, and a continuous maintenance dose is needed.

        The input of coagulation factors should be maintained at a trough concentration of 30% and a peak concentration of 60%, maintaining for 3-4 days

5. Basic guidelines for surgical management of patients with hereditary coagulation function defects
(1) Clear diagnosis
(2) Investigate the presence of coagulation factor inhibitors, hepatitis viruses, HIV infection
(3) Surgical indications are the same as those for patients with normal hemostasis mechanisms
(4) Develop a comprehensive surgical plan
(5) Consult with blood bank doctors, hematologists, anesthesiologists
(6) Avoid intramuscular administration, especially before surgery

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
(7) Avoid aspirin and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs
(8) Calculate and determine the appropriate coagulation factor dosage (preoperative)
(9) Frequent monitoring of coagulation-related parameters
(10) Repeated continuous infusion of the required coagulation factors, maintaining the patient's hemostasis required coagulation factor levels for as long as possible
(11) Consider the use of other auxiliary hemostatic drugs (such as antifibrinolytic drugs) or "decompression anesthesia"

# II. The Impact of Surgery on Physiological Hemostasis
1. Large vascular hemorrhage (arterial or venous), usually stopped by physical methods, including: burning, compression, suture, packing, etc.

2. Microvascular hemorrhage or thrombosis, depending on the balance of the "complete hemostasis system" being "interrupted," including:
(1) Damaged vessels release tissue factor (TF)
(2) Activated FVII activates the extrinsic coagulation pathway
(3) Excessive thrombin generation induces platelet aggregation
(4) Platelets adhere/aggregate to the damaged vascular endothelium and release platelet contents to strengthen platelet-mediated hemostasis
Hemostasis               "Over-"             Thrombosis

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
3. "Signals" of Surgical Thrombosis:
Mechanism: (1) Vascular tissue injury releases sufficient TF and IL-6, TNF, initiating and activating the extrinsic coagulation pathway
(2) In the first few hours postoperatively or post-traumatically, tPA in the circulating blood increases to twice the normal level, and then decreases to normal within 24 hours
(3) PAI-1 rapidly increases post-trauma, reaching 4-5 times the normal level, and then gradually decreases (>24 hours). High levels of PAI-1 inhibit fibrinolytic activity, leading to "fibrinolytic shutdown"
(4) In surgery, trauma, or burn, excessive thrombin generation, excessive consumption of AT-III, and a tendency to form thrombi.

4. In addition to focusing on bleeding issues postoperatively, attention must also be paid to the prevention and management of thrombosis, especially in surgical procedures involving orthopedic and joint surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass surgery, and orthotopic liver transplantation.

# Three: Preoperative Hemostasis Mechanism Screening
1. Basic options for preoperative hemostasis mechanism screening: Prothrombin Time (PT), Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (APTT), Platelet Count

Foreign data shows that the incidence of excessive bleeding in surgical patients is only 3%.

One set of data shows: In a group of 4499 preoperative patients, 2% had excessive bleeding, with a sensitivity of 18% and specificity of 90% for hemostasis screening tests, a positive predictive value of 3%, and a negative predictive value of 98%. There was no significant correlation between the results of preoperative initial screening tests and the bleeding during surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
(1) In the United States, preoperative coagulation mechanism screening for various types of surgery costs $30 billion, with limited clinical value.
(2) Mayo clinics: In 1000 surgical patients, without preoperative coagulation mechanism testing, the mortality rate from bleeding and major bleeding during surgery was 0%.
(3) Reasons for preoperative coagulation mechanism screening test:
           a. Habit: 52%
           b. "Defensive medicine": 45%
           c. Revenue generation

# Key Points:
Detailed medical history inquiry is more important, including:
(1) Whether there are patients with easy bleeding in the family
(2) Whether there has been a tendency to bleed excessively during previous surgical procedures or childbirth (including major and minor surgeries, biopsies, tooth extractions)
(3) For patients who have undergone previous surgical procedures, whether the surgeon needs to re-examine the wound site, and whether the surgeon was sought due to excessive bleeding?
(4) Whether female patients have a history of prolonged menorrhagia? Whether iron therapy has been received due to anemia caused by menorrhagia?

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
(5) Whether there is a tendency to develop subcutaneous bruising, is it single or multiple? Are the bruises limited to the thigh or other areas? Do the bruises protrude above the skin surface (hematoma)?
(6) Whether there is spontaneous epistaxis, occasional or frequent?
(7) Whether blood transfusions or plasma products have been received? For what reason?
(8) Whether there is a tendency to develop bruising or excessive bleeding during trauma, car accidents, organized or spontaneous sports activities.

# Four: Invasive Testing in Patients with Abnormal Coagulation Mechanism
1. Liver biopsy
A large case series study shows (McGill):
   Over a period of 21 years, in 9212 liver biopsy cases, if the patient's Prothrombin Ratio (PTR) is <1.5, and platelet count ≥55,000/ul, the number of bleeding cases after liver biopsy is 32, with an incidence of 0.03%, and there is no correlation between any coagulation test result and postoperative bleeding.

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
A smaller case series study shows (McVay and Toy):
   In 175 patients, PTR <1.5 (equivalent to INR 1.8), APTT ratio <1.5, platelet count ≥50,000/ul, 6 cases of puncture bleeding occurred (3.4%), and there is a certain correlation between coagulation abnormalities in liver cancer patients and bleeding after biopsy. There is no significant improvement in the incidence of bleeding with preoperative administration of FFP or platelets.

If careful: FFP or platelets can be administered preoperatively, but the clinical effectiveness and rationality lack evidence-based medical data.

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
2. Paracentesis and thoracentesis
   In 108 patients who underwent paracentesis or thoracentesis, PT and APTT were prolonged (twice the normal level), and platelets were low to 50,000/ul. In 10% of cases, 4uFFP transfusion was administered preoperatively, and in 90% of cases, it was not. The results showed that postoperative bleeding (defined as a decrease in Hb of 2g/dL) was not significantly related to preoperative FFP transfusion or preoperative coagulation mechanism testing.

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
3. Bronchoscopy
   For patients with bronchoscopy and transbronchial biopsy with abnormal hemostasis tests, the reported data show that there is no correlation between hemostasis test results and postoperative bleeding, even in hematopoietic stem cell transplant patients where platelet count < 20,000/ul. The risk/benefit ratio suggests that the "benefit" of puncture or biopsy outweighs the risk of bleeding, although caution should still be exercised.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
4. Lumbar puncture
   A study of 5223 lumbar puncture patients (pt < 100,000/ul), where 941 patients had platelet counts of 0-50,000/ul, no severe complications were found after puncture. If platelet count > 10,000/ul, lumbar puncture is recommended without prophylactic platelet transfusion.
