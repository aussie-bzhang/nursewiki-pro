# Perioperative Management
Wu Ping'an

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->
#
Strict before surgery, cautious during surgery, skilled after surgery

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->
#
Concept  Generally refers to the period from admission to surgery and the basic recovery after surgery, which varies in duration depending on the size of the surgery.
Surgery is an important means of surgical treatment of diseases, but surgery and anesthesia are both injurious, and patients undergoing surgical treatment are inevitably subject to varying degrees of psychological stress.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
#
Comprehensive examination of the patient, adopt corresponding measures according to the situation, prepare for surgery in advance, so that the patient has sufficient psychological preparation and good physical condition, in order to accept surgery more safely.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
#
After surgery, adopt comprehensive treatment measures to prevent possible complications, restore physiological function as soon as possible, and help the patient recover as soon as possible.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# 第一节   Preoperative Preparation
Preoperative preparation is closely related to the severity and urgency of the patient's disease and the size of the surgical scope. It is classified according to the time limit of surgery.
Emergency surgery: Requires surgery to be performed as soon as possible. For example, splenic rupture and splenectomy, incisional hernia reduction and repair, and gastric and duodenal ulcer perforation repair.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
#
Time-limited surgery: For example, radical surgery for various malignant tumors (gastric cancer radical surgery), and thyroidectomy for hyperthyroidism after taking iodine, although the time of surgery can also be chosen, there should be a certain limit, and it should not be delayed for too long.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
#
Elective surgery: For example, general benign tumor surgery (inguinal hernia repair).

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
#
Preoperative assessment  Detailed inquiry of medical history, comprehensive physical examination, necessary routine and special examinations, in order to find problems, estimate the patient's tolerance to surgery, correct it before surgery, and prevent and treat it during and after surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
#
The patient's tolerance to surgery can be summarized into two categories: (1) Good tolerance. Refers to the minor impact of surgical diseases on the whole body, or there is a certain impact, but it is easy to correct; the patient's overall condition is good, important organs have no organic lesions, or their function is in a compensatory state. This type of patient only needs general preparation before surgery. (2) Poor tolerance. The patient's overall condition is poor, or there are organic lesions in important organs, and the function is near or has lost compensation. This type of patient needs active and detailed special preparation, and surgery can only be performed after the overall condition is improved.

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
#
General preparation  Mainly includes psychological and physiological aspects.
Psychological preparation  The necessity of surgery; the possible effects of surgical treatment; the risks, possible complications, postoperative recovery process, and prognosis; the patient should be explained in a proper and reassuring manner, and cooperation and trust should be obtained from the patient. For the patient's family, a detailed introduction should be made, including the necessity and method of surgery, possible adverse reactions, complications, and unexpected situations during and after surgery, as well as postoperative treatment and prognosis estimates.

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
#
Physiological preparation  Adjusting the patient's physiological state, so that the patient can safely go through the surgical and postoperative treatment process in a better state.
Exercises to adapt to postoperative changes: Including practicing urination in bed before surgery, teaching the patient the correct methods of coughing and expectoration before surgery, stopping smoking 2 weeks before surgery.
Blood transfusion and fluid replacement: All patients with water, electrolyte and acid-base imbalance and anemia should be corrected before surgery. At the same time, prepare blood for use during surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
#
Prophylactic use of antibiotics: Operations involving infected foci or incisions close to infected areas; intestinal surgery; operations with long duration and large wound surfaces; open surgery; cancer surgery; operations involving great vessels; implantation of artificial materials; organ transplantation surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
#
Gastrointestinal preparation: 12 hours of fasting before surgery, 4 hours of fasting before drinking, to prevent vomiting asphyxia or aspiration pneumonia; if necessary, use gastrointestinal decompression. For gastrointestinal surgery, consume liquid diet 1-2 days before surgery; if colonic or rectal surgery is performed, clean enema or colonic lavage should be performed at night and in the morning before surgery, and oral intestinal antibacterial drugs should be taken 2-3 days before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
#

Other: On the night before surgery, sedatives can be administered.
   If the patient has a fever unrelated to the disease or women's menstruation, the surgery date should be delayed.
   Before entering the operating room, urine should be voided; for operations with a long duration or pelvic surgery, a urinary catheter should be placed; for gastrointestinal surgery, a gastric tube should be placed to remove removable dentures.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
#
Special preparation
Malnutrition  Malnourished patients often have hypoproteinemia. They are prone to tissue edema, affecting healing; and their resistance is low, prone to infection. If it is below 30g/L, plasma and albumin should be transfused to correct it.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
#
Hypertension  Patients with blood pressure below 160/100mmHg do not need special preparation. For those with high blood pressure, the stress of anesthesia and surgery can lead to complications such as cerebral vascular accidents and heart failure, so appropriate antihypertensive drugs should be selected before surgery to keep the blood pressure stable at a certain level, but it is not required to return to normal before surgery. For patients with a sudden increase in blood pressure before entering the operating room, the anesthesiologist should be involved in the treatment, and the choice of surgery or delayed surgery should be made according to the condition and nature of the surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
#
Cardiovascular disease  The type of cardiovascular disease is related to the tolerance to surgery.
      Noncyanotic heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, hypertensive heart disease, normal rhythm without heart failure trend  Good
      Coronary heart disease, atrioventricular block  Poor. Adequate preoperative preparation is required
     Acute myocarditis, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure  Very poor, surgery should be postponed except for emergency rescue

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
#
Preoperative preparation注意事项: Patients with long-term low-salt diet and diuretic drugs, and those with water and electrolyte imbalance, should be corrected before surgery. For patients with severe anemia, preoperative blood transfusion should be performed in small and frequent doses to correct anemia. For patients with arrhythmia, if it is occasional ventricular premature contraction, general treatment is not required;

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
#
If there is atrial fibrillation with a fast ventricular rate, or if there is confirmed coronary heart disease with bradycardia, effective internal medical treatment should be used to control the ventricular rate as close to normal as possible.

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
#
Patients with acute myocardial infarction should not undergo elective surgery within 6 months. After 6 months without angina pectoris episodes, surgery can be performed under good surveillance conditions. For patients with heart failure, it is best to perform surgery after 3-4 weeks of heart failure control.

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
#
Respiratory dysfunction  The main manifestation of respiratory insufficiency is dyspnea after slight exertion. Asthma and emphysema are the most common chronic obstructive pulmonary dysfunction. All patients with pulmonary dysfunction should undergo blood gas analysis and pulmonary function tests before surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 23 -->
#
Preoperative preparation for pulmonary diseases: Stop smoking for 2 weeks; for patients with obstructive respiratory diseases, use bronchodilators; for those with ongoing wheezing, elective surgery should be postponed; for severe pulmonary insufficiency and concurrent infection, surgery should only be performed after infection control; for acute respiratory tract infections, if elective surgery is required, it should be postponed until 1-2 weeks after recovery;

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
#
Liver disease  Hepatitis and liver cirrhosis are the most common liver diseases. Mild liver function impairment does not affect the tolerance to surgery; for patients with severe liver function impairment or approaching decompensation, a longer period of strict preparation is required before elective surgery can be performed; for patients with severe liver function impairment, such as severe malnutrition, ascites, jaundice, or acute hepatitis patients, surgery is often not recommended.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
#
Renal disease  Patients with mild to moderate renal function impairment can tolerate surgery well after medical treatment; for those with severe impairment, surgery can be performed after dialysis treatment. Urea: mild 7.5 mmol/L—14.3 mmol/L, moderate 14.6 mmol/L—25 mmol/L, severe 25.3 mmol/L—35.7 mmol/L.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
#
Diabetes  Preoperative blood glucose levels should be controlled, water and electrolyte imbalances and acidosis corrected, and antibiotics used. Blood glucose can be controlled in a slightly elevated state (5.6 mmol/L-11.2 mmol/L).

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
#
Coagulation disorders  Medical history, physical examination, preoperative examination. Appropriate treatment.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
#
Prevention of deep vein thrombosis in the lower limbs  Age over 40, obesity, thrombosis history, varicose veins, smoking, major surgery, prolonged general anesthesia, and hemodynamic abnormalities. Preventive anticoagulation should be used.

                Postoperative management
Preparation in the ward should be made according to the type of surgery;
For patients undergoing minor to moderate surgery, vital signs should be measured every 2-4 hours on the day of surgery; for major surgery or those at risk of internal bleeding or tracheal compression, vital signs should be measured every 30 minutes and recorded. For patients with unstable conditions or those undergoing special surgery, they should be sent to the ICU.

<!-- Slide number: 30 -->
#
Postoperative bed position  The bed position of patients after laparotomy depends on the anesthetic method, surgical site and method, as well as the patient's overall condition. Before the patient regains consciousness from general anesthesia, they should lie flat with the head turned to one side to prevent aspiration of vomit. After epidural or spinal anesthesia, the patient should lie flat for 6 hours to reduce the occurrence of post-anesthetic complications such as headache. For chest, abdominal, and neck surgery, if the condition permits, a semi-recumbent position is often adopted, which is beneficial for respiration and circulation. When there is infection in the abdominal cavity, a semi-recumbent position is also beneficial for the accumulation of inflammatory exudates in the pelvis, preventing the occurrence of subphrenic abscess. If a residual abscess forms in the pelvis, surgical drainage is also more convenient. After cranial surgery, a slope position with the upper body elevated 15-30 degrees is better, which can reduce the occurrence of cerebral edema. After spinal or gluteal surgery, supine or fetal position is often adopted.

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
#
Activity and ambulation  For general surgery under local anesthesia, as long as the condition permits, it is advisable to start activity as early as possible after surgery. For seriously ill patients and those after major surgery, deep breathing exercises and limb flexion and extension exercises can be performed under the guidance and assistance of medical staff the day after surgery, and the amount and range of activity can be gradually increased. If there are no contraindications, patients can gradually sit up and walk out of bed with support the next day, and the time can be gradually extended. It is also beneficial to tap the patient's back while sitting and have the patient cough hard, which helps with lung expansion. Early activity can improve respiration and circulation, reduce the chance of pulmonary complications and deep vein thrombosis in the lower limbs, and also facilitate the rapid recovery of gastrointestinal and bladder function.

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
#
Diet and fluid intake During the period of fasting, a certain amount of glucose, saline, and electrolytes should be infused peripherally into the patient every day. The total fluid intake for adults is 2500-3500 milliliters per day, of which isotonic saline should not exceed 500 milliliters, and the rest of the fluid is supplemented by 5% and 10% glucose solutions. If the patient is still unable to eat after 3 days, 3-4 grams of potassium can be infused intravenously per day. If there is a large amount of additional loss (such as gastrointestinal decompression, biliary fistula, or pancreatic fistula), it should be replenished accordingly. For patients with severe hypoproteinemia after surgery, intermittent administration of amino acid preparations, human serum albumin, and plasma can be beneficial for the healing of surgical wounds. For patients with chronic anemia due to chronic hemorrhage, blood transfusion should be continued after surgery to ensure the success of the surgery.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
#
Suture removal time  The time for suture removal can be determined according to the site of the incision, local blood supply, and the patient's age.
       Reference time: 4-5 days after surgery for the head, face, and neck
                           6-7 days after surgery for the lower abdomen and perineum
                             7-9 days after surgery for the chest, upper abdomen, back, and buttocks
                              10-12 days after surgery for the limbs (near the joint area can be appropriately extended)
                              14 days after surgery for tension sutures

(Seven) Management of drainage
1. Cavity drainage
Thoracic cavity drainage: After 24 hours without bubbles, if the drainage fluid is less than 50-60ml, the tube can be clamped for 24 hours and then removed.
Abdominal cavity drainage: The drainage fluid decreases, generally removed 2-3 days after surgery.

Drainage from hollow organs.
Gastrostomy tube: Recovery of intestinal peristalsis and passage of gas.
Biliary duct T-tube: 12-15 days after surgery.

After the analgesic effect of pain relief wears off, the incision begins to hurt, reaching its peak within 24 hours and lasting approximately 48-72 hours. The degree of pain is related to the size, location of the surgery, and the patient's tolerance. Pain not only affects the patient's rest and hinders recovery from the disease but may also trigger the occurrence of some complications. To reduce incision pain, patients who have undergone abdominal surgery often dare not take deep breaths or cough, which affects lung expansion and increases the risk of pulmonary complications; postoperative pain in the perineum and anal area is more severe, which can lead to difficulties in urination. There are many methods to relieve incision pain, generally oral analgesics for routine surgery, and for major surgeries, intramuscular injection of Durogesic 50-100 mg 1-2 times within 1-2 days after surgery. For anal surgery, analgesics should be administered once every 4-6 hours. When incision pain gradually worsens 4-5 days after surgery, the possibility of incision infection should be considered.

#
Nausea and Vomiting Nausea and vomiting after surgery are common reactions during the recovery process of anesthesia and may also be side effects of analgesics such as morphine. With the disappearance of the effects of anesthetics and analgesics, nausea and vomiting can stop, and no special treatment is needed. However, frequent vomiting may also be one of the early symptoms of certain complications. When there is paroxysmal abdominal pain with vomiting, one should consider the possibility of mechanical bowel obstruction. Treatment should be targeted; if there are no special circumstances, appropriate sedatives or antispasmodics can be administered.

#
Hiccups Hiccups occurring after surgery are mostly transient, with most patients stopping on their own, but some can be very stubborn. Continuous hiccups not only affect the patient's rest but are also extremely detrimental to incision healing. The main cause of hiccups is the stimulation of the diaphragm by mechanical or inflammatory factors, and some patients may also be induced by improper eating. Persistent hiccups may be a symptom of subdiaphragmatic infection and should be further investigated. For the treatment of hiccups, one can first try acupuncture at points such as Tianchu, Jiwei, Neiguan, and Zusanli, or by compressing the supraorbital nerve or the eye, which may sometimes be effective. Sedatives, tranquilizers, and antispasmodics can also be administered, and in extremely rare cases, the phrenic nerve may need to be blocked to control the hiccups.

#
Urinary Retention Urinary retention often occurs in patients who have undergone anal, rectal, and pelvic surgery, and can also be caused by general anesthesia or spinal anesthesia. The former is due to reflex spasm of the bladder sphincter caused by incision pain, and the latter is due to suppression of the micturition reflex. A few patients may not be accustomed to lying down to urinate, feeling a sense of urination in the lower abdomen but unable to urinate. Treatment methods: If the condition permits, assist the patient in changing positions (either lying on the side or standing) to urinate, or perform physical therapy, heat application, and massage in the bladder area to promote urination. Ammonium carbamide can also be used, with a dose of 0.25 mg injected intramuscularly. If general measures are ineffective, catheterization should be performed under sterile conditions, and the catheter should be removed 2-3 days after surgery. Postoperative complications management

# Prevention and Treatment of Postoperative Complications
(One) Postoperative Hemorrhage
Judgment (early, late)
1. When the dressing covering the incision is wet with blood, examine the wound. If there is continuous bleeding, or if bleeding is observed after removing some sutures.

2. The amount of blood drained from hollow organs or drainage tubes exceeds 100 ml per hour.

#
3. Various clinical manifestations of shock

   If the signs and monitoring indicators of shock do not improve, or continue to worsen, or improve temporarily and then恶化 after sufficient blood and fluid administration

   Treatment: Consider reoperation for hemostasis

#
# (Two) Incision Complications
1. Wound Infection: Manifestations: Local redness, swelling, heat, pain, and tenderness at the wound, with secretion, accompanied by or without fever and increased white blood cells
   Treatment: Selective use of appropriate antimicrobial agents for treatment and drainage
2 Incision Dehiscence: Partial (including internal dehiscence) and complete

     Treatment: Immediate or elective suture

#
# (Three) Pulmonary Complications
Pulmonary Atrophy, Pneumonia

Manifestations: Fever, tachycardia, possible deviation of the trachea towards the affected side, dull or solid percussion sounds, localized moist rales on auscultation, weakened, disappeared, or tubular breath sounds, pulmonary atrophy signs on chest X-ray

#
Treatment: ① Exercise deep breathing and turn over more frequently
      ② Avoid restrictive fixation or binding that limits breathing after surgery
      ③ Reduce secretions in the alveoli and bronchi
      ④ Encourage coughing, use positioning or medication as necessary, and perform tracheotomy if necessary to facilitate the removal of bronchial secretions
      ⑤ Prevent aspiration of postoperative vomiting or oral secretions
      ⑥ Concurrent antibiotic treatment if inflammation occurs

#
# (Four) Urinary Tract Infection
Manifestations: Frequent urination, urgency, dysuria, sometimes difficulty in urination, sometimes accompanied by fever and renal pain.
Urine examination shows red blood cells and pus cells, a large number of white blood cells, and bacterial growth in interrupted urine culture.
Treatment: Select antibiotics, maintain urine volume, and ensure smooth urination.

# Incision Classification:
  ① Clean Incision (Class I incision), refers to aseptic incisions that can be sutured
  ② Potentially Contaminated Incision (Class II incision), refers to sutured incisions that may be contaminated during surgery, such as subtotal gastrectomy. Areas of the skin that are difficult to sterilize completely, wounds within 6 hours, those sutured after debridement, and those re-opened after new sutures also belong to this category.

#
  ③ Contaminated Incision or Infected Wound (Class III incision), refers to incisions adjacent to an infected area or where tissues are directly exposed to infectious materials, such as appendectomy for appendiceal perforation, surgery for intestinal obstruction with necrosis.

#
Incision Healing Classification:
  ① Class A Healing, represented by the character "甲", refers to excellent healing with no adverse reactions
  ② Class B Healing, represented by the character "乙", refers to inflammation at the healing site, such as redness, swelling, hardness, hematoma, effusion, etc., but without suppuration
  ③ Class C Healing, represented by the character "丙", refers to suppuration of the incision, requiring incision and drainage or other treatments
Record: As I/甲.
