Perioperative Nursing

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->

**Introduction**
Surgery primarily involves surgical treatment, which can cure diseases while also potentially causing complications and sequelae. As a surgical nurse, to promote the recovery of patients during the perioperative period, the following responsibilities must be fulfilled:
1. Comprehensive preoperative assessment of the patient's physical and mental condition to ensure that the patient has good physical and mental conditions to tolerate surgery.
2. Ensuring patient safety and the smooth implementation of surgery during the operation.
3. Assisting patients in recovering various physiological functions as soon as possible after surgery, preventing complications, and promoting early recovery; returning to the family and society.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->
**Concept of Perioperative Period**
The perioperative period is a whole process surrounding surgery. It starts from the patient's decision to undergo surgical treatment and continues until the completion of the surgical treatment and the basic recovery of the patient.

# **Preoperative Nursing**
**(I) Preoperative Period:**
           This period refers to the time from the patient's decision to undergo surgery to the time the patient is sent to the operating table.

**(II) Nursing Focus:**
      Assess and correct psychological and physiological problems that may increase the risk of surgery (anxiety, fear, and concerns about postoperative prognosis), provide psychological support to the patient, guide the patient in adapting to postoperative changes (bedside urination, position training, etc.), and identify existing and potential problems (blood sugar, blood pressure control, etc.).

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# **Nursing Assessment**
**(I) Health History (medical history and health status, surgical history, medication history, drug allergy history, etc.)**
**(II) Physical and Mental Status:**
           1. **Physiological Status** (age, nutritional status, fluid balance status, infection, function of important organs)
           2. **Psychosocial Status** (psychological status, family and social status)

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# **Nursing Assessment**
**(III) Auxiliary Examinations**
1. Laboratory (blood, urine, stool routine, coagulation time, prothrombin time, blood type, cross-matching test, blood electrolytes, liver and kidney function, blood sugar, urine sugar)
  2. Imaging: chest X-ray, ultrasound, CT, MRI, angiography, etc.
  3. Electrocardiogram
  4. Pulmonary function, blood gas analysis
  5. Special examinations: IVP, cystoscopy, gastroscopy, etc.

# Nursing Evaluation
(1) Whether the patient's anxiety and fear about surgery have been alleviated or relieved.
(2) Whether the patient understands the disease and the knowledge of cooperation before and after surgery.
(3) Whether the nutritional status has improved.
(4) Whether water, electrolyte imbalance and acid-base disturbance have been corrected.
(5) Whether the patient has received sufficient rest and sleep before surgery.
(6) Whether the patient has not developed infection or the infection has been controlled.

          ② Reduce physical and psychological pain and discomfort

# Nursing Assessment
(1) General condition
            Understand the anesthetic method, surgical method, intraoperative management, intraoperative blood loss, fluid and blood transfusion volume, urine output, and medication, the type and placement of drainage tubes, their function, and skin condition, etc.

(2) Important organ function
            Observe consciousness, pupils, respiratory rate, pulse, temperature, blood pressure, skin condition, and urination to assess organ function.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Nursing Assessment
(3) Surgical fever:
           Due to the body's response to surgical trauma, the patient's body temperature may slightly rise, generally not exceeding 38°C, and gradually return to normal within 1-2 days.

(4) Anesthetic recovery
           Assess the patient's consciousness, respiratory and circulatory function, limb movement and sensation, and skin color to judge whether the anesthetic has awakened and the degree of awakening.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
# Nursing Assessment
(5) Incision and drainage
            Dressing fixation, secretion, bleeding, infection, whether the drainage is unobstructed, the nature, color, and amount of drainage fluid.

(6) Emotional response
           Concern about the surgical outcome and prognosis, causing anxiety, depression, and even negative and pessimistic disappointment.

# Nursing Measures
(1) Positioning and transfer
          1. Handover the patient: Secure the drainage tube, minimize movement, and avoid postural hypotension and drainage tube dislodgment.
2. Position: Routine patients after anesthesia recovery, the head of the bed is raised to 30 degrees. Advantages: ① Beneficial for blood circulation and patient breathing, increasing lung ventilation; ② Relaxes the abdominal muscles, reducing abdominal incision tension; ③ Allows inflammatory exudates to flow to the pelvis, preventing the formation of subdiaphragmatic abscess; ④ Beneficial for drainage tube drainage

# (2) Maintaining respiratory and circulatory function
    1. Close observation of vital signs
             Full anesthesia or major surgery every 15-30 minutes, and then change to 1-2 hours after the condition is stable, and record it well.
    2. Maintain a clear airway
           Prevent tongue prolapse, encourage deep breathing, effective coughing to promote sputum and lung expansion, frequent turning, nebulization for thick sputum, and assisted expectoration (preventing pulmonary infection).
    3. Oxygen therapy
   4. Prevent hypotension Prevent postural hypotension when sitting up or standing

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
# (3) Maintaining gastrointestinal function
    1. Insertion of gastric decompression tube:
         Maintain the patency of the gastric tube and ensure effective decompression.
    2. Encourage movement:
    Turn over, bed exercises, and early ambulation to promote peristalsis
    3. Promote peristalsis:
    Administer ultrasonic drugs, abdominal massage, apply traditional Chinese medicine, and if necessary, follow the doctor's instructions to give rectal suppositories, rectal evacuation, or enema
4. Oral care:
  Provide good oral care for the patient, especially for unconscious patients. Apply glycerin to protect dry lips, and maintain oral hygiene.

# (IV) Supplementation of Nutrition and Water and Electrolyte Imbalance
    ④ After abdominal surgery and gastrointestinal surgery, the patient should be fasting for 24-48 hours postoperatively. On the 3rd to 4th day, intestinal function recovers, and after anal exhaust, the patient should start with small amounts of liquid diet, gradually increasing to full liquid diet. On the 5th to 6th day, semi-liquid diet should be initiated, and generally, soft food can be introduced on the 7th to 9th day. Normal food can be started from the 10th to 12th day.
    ⑤ Encourage the intake of high-protein, high-calorie, and high-vitamin diets for those who can eat (beneficial for wound healing, prevention of constipation, etc.).

  2. Fluid Replacement:
        Patients who cannot eat should receive intravenous fluid and nutritional supplementation.

  (V) Enhancing Patient Comfort

1. Pain Management

After anesthesia wears off, patients may experience incision pain, which is most severe within 24 hours and gradually diminishes after 2-3 days.

① Reassure and encourage the patient to eliminate fear of pain;

② Take appropriate measures based on the cause of pain, such as pain due to bloating and bladder distension, which can be relieved after肛管排气 and induction of urination; pain due to cast compression can be relieved by opening the cast window or cutting it; 

③ For minor surgery, analgesics can be taken orally, and for major surgery, intramuscular injection of pethidine is often required for 1-2 days. Attention should be given to administering analgesics when the pain begins, as their effectiveness is better than when the pain is severe. For patients with low blood pressure, the dosage of analgesics should be reduced.

2. Nausea and Vomiting Management
Postoperative nausea and vomiting are often due to anesthetic reactions and can stop spontaneously after the anesthesia wears off. If it persists, a comprehensive analysis of the patient's condition should be conducted to determine if there is a disturbance in water and electrolyte metabolism, diabetic acidosis, uremia, increased intracranial pressure, acute gastric dilation, or intestinal obstruction.

    ① Turn the patient's head to the side during vomiting to prevent aspiration;

    ② Observe and record the frequency, volume, color, and characteristics of the vomit;

    ③ Clean the vomit and strengthen oral care;

    ④ For vomiting without obvious cause, follow the doctor's orders to administer sedatives or antiemetics.

3. Bloating Management
       It is often caused by anesthesia and gastrointestinal dysfunction after abdominal surgery.
     Management:
   ① Encourage early ambulation to promote gastrointestinal function recovery;
   ② Avoid excessive fluid intake (as excessive salt solution can delay gastrointestinal function recovery)
   ③ Early recovery of oral intake, etc. (chewing gum "pseudo-feeding" therapy can promote intestinal motility recovery and shorten the postoperative ileus time)
④ For those with hypokalemia or peritonitis, appropriate treatment should be given.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->

(V) Enhancing Patient Comfort
4. Urinary Retention Management
       It is often caused by anesthesia, not being accustomed to urinating in bed, and incision pain.
   Management:
    ① Reassure and encourage the patient, alleviate anxiety and tension
    ② Change the position; assist the patient to sit on the edge of the bed or get out of bed to urinate if the condition permits; (protect privacy)
    ③ Apply heat to the lower abdomen and massage to induce urination
    ④ If the above measures are ineffective, perform catheterization.

(VI) Incision Care

        Proper incision care is beneficial for incision healing and prevention of incision infection.

        ① Keep the dressing clean and dry, and replace the dressing promptly if there is incision bleeding or leakage. Apply pressure dressing to stop bleeding, and if there is significant bleeding in extremity incisions, apply a tourniquet first and then further treatment. If there is a significant amount of bleeding, notify the physician immediately to determine the cause and handle it promptly;

        ② For unconscious, restless patients, and children, apply restraints to prevent them from pulling off the dressing; replace the dressing immediately after defecation or urination; 

        ③ Follow the doctor's orders to use antibiotics, administer the correct dosage and timing, and prevent incision infection;

        ④ If there are signs of infection such as redness, swelling, hardness, and tenderness at the incision, take local heat therapy and physical therapy measures to promote inflammation absorption.

(VIII) Guidance for Early Ambulation
1. Benefits:
            Increase lung capacity, reduce pulmonary complications, improve overall blood circulation, promote gastrointestinal function recovery and incision healing, prevent pressure sores, and reduce the formation of lower limb venous thrombosis, etc.
2. Principles:
       Early ambulation, strive to get out of bed and ambulate within a short period of time.
3. Caution:
            However, patients with shock, heart failure, severe infection, bleeding, extreme weakness, or those who have undergone joint surgery and need to limit activity should not emphasize early ambulation.

# [Assessment]
(One) Whether the patient's postoperative vital signs are stable.
(Two) Whether the fluid balance is maintained, and no electrolyte imbalance has occurred.
(Three) The patient has no postoperative discomfort.
(Four) The patient's nutritional status has improved.
(Five) Whether the patient's activity tolerance has increased.
(Six) Whether the patient can recite relevant postoperative health knowledge and actively cooperate with treatment and care.
(Seven) The patient's mood is stable and actively cooperates with treatment and care.
(Eight) No complications have occurred, or complications are detected and handled in a timely manner.

# [Summary]
The success of surgery depends on whether the preoperative preparation is sufficient. Therefore, medical staff should attach great importance to preoperative preparation, which includes the preparation of medical personnel, patients (psychological and physiological), and surgical equipment. Postoperative observation and management are important measures for preventing and treating various postoperative complications, consolidating the efficacy of surgery, and promoting the patient's early recovery. Medical staff should be proficient in postoperative condition observation, promptly detect and correctly handle postoperative discomfort, and prevent complications.
