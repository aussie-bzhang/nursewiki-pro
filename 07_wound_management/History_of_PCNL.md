# History of PCNL
In 1955, Goodwin used a puncture needle for percutaneous renal puncture to treat hydronephrosis. In 1976, Femstram successfully performed percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). Since then, percutaneous nephrolithotomy has been widely used in the treatment of renal calculi. In 1983, Whitfield completed the first percutaneous nephrolithotomy renal pelvis ureteral junction incision, expanding the application range of percutaneous nephrolithotomy. In the 1980s, with the popularization of ESWL and ureteroscope technology, percutaneous nephrolithotomy entered a low ebb. In recent years, it has been recognized that ESWL has a damaging effect on the kidneys, and the percutaneous renal puncture micro-fenestration technique has been established, with more effective laser and ultrasonic lithotripsy methods, and percutaneous nephrolithotomy technology has gained new development.

Our hospital was the first in the province and the reclamation area to carry out this technology in 2008. At present, we have completed more than 500 high-quality nephrolithotomy surgeries, and basically eliminated open stone removal surgery.

# Indications
The main treatment method for renal calculi and upper ureteral calculi:
① Renal calculi ≥2.5cm, especially cast stones
② Renal calculi failed after extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL)
③ Various recurrent renal calculi; renal calculi with renal pelvis ureteral junction stenosis
④ Upper ureteral calculi

### Notes:
Ureteral mid-lower segment calculi can be treated with ureteroscopy.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
# Contraindications
① Hemorrhagic diseases of the whole body
② Acute infection
③ Severe kyphosis and severe cardiovascular and pulmonary dysfunction, making it impossible to lie prone
④ High-positioned kidneys with liver or spleen enlargement
⑤ Ischemic heart disease

# Hemorrhage
Influencing factors: stone load, channel number, expansion method, patient constitution

Significant hemorrhage <8%, blood transfusion rate 5-18%

Classification: intraoperative hemorrhage, postoperative hemorrhage, delayed hemorrhage

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# Intraoperative Hemorrhage
Puncture hemorrhage: intercostal vessels, segmental vessels, interlobar vessels

Lithotripsy hemorrhage: injury to the collecting system, calyx neck tear

Expansion hemorrhage: expander too deep

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# Prevention of Intraoperative Hemorrhage
Puncture the renal calyx dome

Do not deepen the expansion process

Keep the angle of movement of the mirror body small during operation

Do not have too many channels

Exclude coagulation function and blood system disorders before surgery

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->

# Postoperative Hemorrhage
Causes: catheter stimulation, stone residue, secondary infection

Management: absolute bed rest, clamp the renal造瘘管, anti-inflammatory hemostasis, transfusion of fresh plasma, thrombin complex, etc. For those with bladder blood clot formation, perform continuous bladder irrigation with normal saline or blood clot removal under cystoscopy. If conservative treatment is ineffective, timely renal artery embolization should be performed.

# Infection
This is a complication that can lead to the most severe consequences. Clinically, many physicians believe that severe urinary sepsis caused by serious infection is more可怕 than bleeding and injury after experiencing the severe complications brought about by infection.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# Risk Factors
1. Positive urine culture before surgery
2. Renal insufficiency
3. Prolonged surgery time or excessive perfusion pressure
4. High intrarenal pressure within the collecting system (studies have shown that when the intrarenal pressure exceeds 30mmHg and lasts for more than 10 minutes, the fever rate and pain index are higher than those with low-pressure irrigation)

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# Prevention Measures
1. Increase the operating channel
2. Maintain unobstructed fluid outflow during surgery and reduce intrarenal pressure
3. Administer antibiotics preoperatively to patients with staghorn calculi and urinary tract infections
4. Shorten the duration of surgery
5. EMS and other vacuum equipment
6. Timely, adequate, effective, and selective antibiotic therapy postoperatively

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# Injury to Adjacent Organs
**Lung**
  Incidence: 2.3-3.1%, 10% above the 12th rib. Ultrasound-guided puncture or end-expiratory puncture can reduce the incidence.
  Management: Stop surgery, place chest closed drainage if there is significant hemothorax or pneumothorax.

**Colon**
  Incidence: 0.2-0.8%, horseshoe kidney and advanced age are two high-risk factors for colon injury. Lack of retroperitoneal fat and a history of abdominal surgery are also predisposing factors.

**Liver and Spleen**
  Rare, case reports. Carefully review preoperative imaging, and carefully observe puncture and ultrasound during surgery.

# Electrolyte and Water Balance Disturbance
Fluid extravasation and absorption are a characteristic of PCNL.

High perfusion pressure, poor drainage, and perforation or tearing of the collecting system can lead to fluid extravasation into the retroperitoneum, abdomen, and chest, causing retroperitoneal effusion, ascites, and pleural effusion.

Retroperitoneal and abdominal effusions can cause paralytic ileus and pseudomembranous peritonitis.

The large absorption of perfusion fluid can cause circulatory overload, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis, similar to TUR syndrome, which can pose a life-threatening risk.

Fluid absorption may also be related to non-infectious fever.

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Prevention Measures
1. The puncture channel should pass through the renal parenchyma, puncture the renal pelvis, and avoid direct puncture of the renal pelvis.
2. Maintain the patency of the percutaneous renal channel. For patients with large calculi and expected long surgery times, appropriate lithotripping equipment should be used, and the percutaneous renal channel should be appropriately expanded to reduce surgery time and the possibility of excessive intrarenal pressure.
3. If endoscopic electroresection and electrocoagulation procedures are not performed, use intravenous normal saline for perfusion fluid to avoid using distilled water, which can lead to electrolyte imbalance.
4. For patients with fluid extravasation, closely monitor blood electrolytes, limit fluid intake, and appropriately use diuretics (mannitol and furosemide).
5. The use of antibiotics can prevent secondary infection due to urinary extravasation.

#
The complications of PCNL have some characteristics different from those of open surgery, with bleeding and infection fever being the most common. Although the incidence of severe complications is low, they are diverse. It is necessary to fully understand the characteristics of each complication, make adequate preoperative preparations, perform standardized operations during surgery, master surgical skills, and detect and treat complications in a timely manner after surgery to effectively prevent the occurrence and development of PCNL complications and make them truly "minimally invasive".

# Advantages of EMS Ultrasound Lithotripsy
Lithotripsy clears stone particles at the same time, shortening surgery time.
Negative pressure aspiration is opened during lithotripsy, which prevents stone displacement, does not generate heat, and causes no thermal injury.
It avoids the increase in intrarenal pressure during lithotripsy, lowering it to a negative pressure state, which is conducive to avoiding the absorption of toxins and pyrogens during lithotripsy, reducing the occurrence of fever, bacteremia, and sepsis, and reducing fluid extravasation and its resulting perinephric infection complications.
It improves the safety of treating infectious calculi and calculi with concurrent infection.

# Discussion
  Although PCNL has many advantages, due to surgical needs, operational errors, and patient factors, complications are easy to occur postoperatively. It suggests that clinical prevention measures should be taken in advance to "prevent before it happens" rather than dealing with it after it occurs.
