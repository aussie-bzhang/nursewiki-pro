# Overview
   Pelvic fractures are characterized by local pain, swelling, subcutaneous ecchymosis in the perineum, inguinal or lumbar region, difficulty in limb movement and turning over, and shortening and deformity of the affected lower limb. Fractures occur in areas including the sacrum, coccyx, ilium, pubis, and ischium. The pelvis is a complete closed bone ring, consisting of two iliac bones and the sacrum. The front is connected by the pubic symphysis, and the back is formed by the articular surface of the ilium and sacrum to form the sacroiliac joint. The pelvic structure is strong, and injuries are often caused by high-energy external forces. Compression, collision, or falls from a height are the main causes of pelvic fractures. Fractures can also be caused by strong muscle contraction leading to avulsion fractures; gunshot wounds can cause open fractures. The most serious complications of pelvic fractures are traumatic hemorrhagic shock and associated injuries of pelvic cavity organs, with a high mortality rate if not treated properly, and a high disability rate of about 50% to 60%.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# Etiology
1   Direct violence is the main cause of pelvic fractures, such as traffic accidents, crushing injuries, and falls from a height. It can also be caused by strong muscle contraction leading to fractures at the iliac spine, iliac tubercle, and ischial tuberosity.
2   Stress violence acting on the lateral side of the pelvis first causes fractures at the weak areas of the anterior ring of the pelvis, such as the superior and inferior pubic rami. The continued stress causes the iliac wing to converge, resulting in fractures or dislocations at the sacroiliac joint or its vicinity. The lateral stress causes the pelvis to be compressed on the opposite side and deformed.
3   When violence acts on the posterior side of the pelvis, it causes the iliac wing to flip outward, first fracturing the pubic and ischial rami or separating the pubic symphysis. The continued stress causes the iliac wing to flip outward even more, resulting in injuries to the sacroiliac joint or its vicinity. The deformation of the pelvic ring is due to the flip or twist of the iliac wing on the injured side, separating the contralateral half-pelvis.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# Classification of Fractures
A-type: Stable fractures, such as fractures of the anterior ring of the pelvis, the pelvic margin, or the sacrum and coccyx, without affecting the integrity of the pelvic ring.
B-type: Partially stable fractures, where both the anterior and posterior rings of the pelvis are damaged, resulting in rotational instability and vertical stability fractures.
C-type: Rotational and vertical instability fractures.

# Clinical Manifestations
       Local manifestations
     (1) Pain: Patients have significant pain, which worsens during movement and turning over.
     (2) The skin and soft tissues in the pelvic area have contusions, crush injuries, open wounds, and other signs of forceful impact.
     (3) Pain, swelling, limited movement, and crepitus at the site of injury.
     (4) Pelvic separation, positive compression test, asymmetry of the pelvis on both sides, elevated iliac crest on the injured side, shortening of the lower limb, positive "4" sign test, and unequal umbilical to spina distance when the sacroiliac joint is completely dislocated.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
# Clinical Manifestations
Systemic manifestations
  Severe pelvic fractures with hemorrhagic shock can present with drowsiness, rapid and weak pulse, cold extremities, pale skin, and low blood pressure. Associated injuries with pelvic fractures include rectal and anal injuries, female reproductive tract injuries, urethral and bladder injuries, nerve injuries, and more.

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# Diagnosis
1   X-ray examination: The main method for diagnosing pelvic fractures, which can show the type and displacement of the fracture.
2   CT scan: Has the following advantages: (1) Can detect fractures that cannot be shown on X-ray films. (2) Can clearly and stereographically display the displacement of the half-pelvis. (3) Particularly applicable to acetabular fractures. (4) For pelvic fractures requiring internal fixation, CT can accurately show the reduction status, whether the internal fixation position is appropriate, and the progress of fracture healing.
Ultrasound examination: To understand the condition of abdominal and pelvic visceral organs and large blood vessels.
Magnetic resonance imaging: Can detect soft tissue injuries such as muscles, tendons, ligaments, and nerves in the pelvic area and hidden fractures.

# Non-surgical Treatment
Indications
Stable fractures of the pelvic ring, such as avulsion fractures and fractures with no obvious displacement of the pelvic ring at one site.
Fractures with two sites of injury to the pelvic ring but instability, with no or minor displacement shown on imaging.
Patients who require early treatment and need to be bedridden or traction treated, with satisfactory reduction shown on imaging after treatment.
Patients with contraindications to surgery or multiple injuries that are not suitable for surgical treatment.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
Methods
Symptomatic treatment, resting on a hard board bed for 3-4 weeks, taking a relaxed muscle position for muscle avulsion fractures, placing the patient with iliac spine fractures in a flexed hip position; placing the patient with ischial tuberosity fractures in an extended knee position.
Pelvic belt traction fixation, the suspension belt weight should be appropriate to lift the buttocks off the bed surface, and change to plaster shorts fixation after 5-6 weeks.
     Manual reduction, limb bone traction

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Surgical Treatment
External fixator fixation: Suitable for unstable fractures with obvious displacement, especially in cases with concurrent circulatory instability, in order to achieve the effect of fixing the pelvis and controlling bleeding, and to alleviate pain and facilitate the movement of the injured patient; rotational instability fractures; open unstable fractures.
Open reduction and fixation: Suitable for cases with more than 1cm of fracture displacement after non-surgical treatment, more than 3cm of pubic symphysis separation, displaced fractures involving the acetabulum, and multiple injuries.

# Nursing
 （一）     Non-surgical treatment and preoperative nursing
   1   Emergency nursing
 （1）Emergency care: After admission, establish two venous access lines quickly, and the infusion channel should be established in the upper limb or neck, rather than the lower limb, to avoid ineffective entry of fluid into the blood circulation. Timely blood transfusion and fluid infusion, and perform venesection if necessary, to rapidly and effectively replenish fluids.
（2）Rapid and effective hemostasis and pain relief are the key to rescue. Patients with pelvic fractures often present with hemorrhagic shock, and timely reduction and fixation of the fracture site can prevent further vascular injury, alleviate pain.

# Nursing
2  Observation of Condition    Closely monitor vital signs, abdominal pain, distension, urination, and defecation. Report any abnormalities to the doctor promptly. Observe the distal extremities of both lower limbs for sensation, movement, color, dorsalis pedis pulse, and swelling.

3   Preoperative Preparation  Complete routine preoperative preparation, and prepare the perineum for skin preparation.

4   Pain Management

<!-- Slide number: 24 -->
  5   Psychological Care   Actively communicate with the patient, help them adapt to the unfamiliar environment, inform them about the disease and surgical knowledge, and alleviate their anxiety.

  6   Diet Management   Ensure adequate nutrition, consume high-protein, high-calorie, high-vitamin, pectin-rich, and coarse fiber foods such as eggs, lean meat, fish soup, milk, tomatoes, etc.

  7   Positioning   The patient must remain in bed and rest absolutely. For fractures that do not affect the integrity of the pelvic ring, the patient can alternate between supine and lateral positions, with the healthy side down when in the lateral position. Prohibit sitting, and after one week, the patient can assume a semi-recumbent position. For fractures that affect the integrity of the pelvic ring, the patient should lie flat on a hard board bed and should minimize movement. If movement is necessary, it should be done by several people lifting the patient evenly to avoid pain and increase bleeding. Try to use an air-filled bed to reduce the number of turns and prevent pressure sores, maintaining the limb in a functional position as the principle.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
  8   Symptom Management
  (1) Pressure Sores   Maintain effective traction with the pelvic harness, with the traction force raising the buttocks by 5 cm from the bed surface. Place pads inside the harness on both sides of the pelvis to prevent pressure sores.
  (2) Constipation   Encourage the patient to drink plenty of water, consume more vegetables and fruits, and eat easily digestible foods. Perform abdominal massage frequently to promote peristalsis. If necessary, take a mild laxative to facilitate defecation. It is necessary to evacuate the accumulated stool in the intestines the day before surgery to facilitate surgical procedures and reduce postoperative bloating.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
   9  Nursing for Two Different Treatment Methods
   (1) Pelvic Harness Traction   Keep the harness flat, complete, and free of wrinkles, maintain an appropriate width, and do not move up or down. Be careful not to contaminate during urination and defecation. Observe the blood circulation in both lower limbs and maintain the limb in a functional position.
   (2) Lower Limb Traction   Traction is usually performed on both lower limbs simultaneously because if only one side is tractioned, it can easily cause the pelvis to tilt, resulting in limb adduction deformity, affecting future walking function, and can cause back pain and hip pain.

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
# Postoperative Nursing
1   Observation of Vital Signs    Closely monitor vital signs. Due to the significant blood loss during surgery, pay particular attention to changes in blood pressure and heart rate, and promptly identify signs of hemorrhagic shock. Observe the distal extremities of both lower limbs for sensation, movement, color, dorsalis pedis pulse, and swelling.

2   Positioning    Remain in supine position with the head turned to one side for the first 6 hours postoperatively to prevent vomiting and aspiration. Sleep on an air-filled bed.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
# Postoperative Nursing
Diet Management    Refrain from drinking and eating for the first 6 hours postoperatively. After 6 hours, consume liquid foods. From postoperative day 1 to 3, consume semi-liquid foods. After anal排气, consume soft foods. Refrain from consuming milk and sweets for 3 days postoperatively to prevent intestinal bloating. Drink plenty of water and consume more vegetables and fruits.
Psychological Care    Provide more guidance and encouragement to the patient, obtain the support of the family, and increase the patient's positive attitude towards treatment.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
5   Wound Observation
         Observe the condition of the wound dressing. If there is bleeding or exudate, replace it promptly to maintain a clean and dry dressing to prevent infection. Observe the blood circulation of the affected limb. Properly secure the drainage tube to prevent twisting, folding, or detachment, maintain an appropriate negative pressure in the negative pressure drainage bottle to facilitate timely drainage of wound blood, and closely observe the color, quantity, nature, and record the drainage fluid.

 （3）Bladder and Ureteral Injury

 （5）Neurological Injury

# Nursing for Hemorrhagic Shock
1）  Minimize patient movement as much as possible. If movement is necessary, have 3-4 people place the patient on a flat stretcher, with coordinated and gentle movements to avoid increasing bleeding and exacerbating shock.
2）Two venous channels for fluid resuscitation and oxygen administration
3）Strengthen monitoring of vital signs, central venous pressure, and urine output
4）Correctly and promptly collect specimens to ensure the accuracy of laboratory tests

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
# Nursing for Posterior Pelvic Hematoma
           Observe for abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, bowel sounds, and peritoneal irritation, and measure abdominal circumference at regular intervals to determine whether there is a posterior pelvic hematoma, abdominal organ injury, or bladder injury.

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
# Nursing for Bladder and Ureteral Injury
1）  Observe the patient for hematuria, difficulty urinating, oliguria, or anuria to determine the condition of bladder and ureteral injury.
2）Urethral injury care   Properly secure the catheter to prevent it from falling off. Maintain a通畅 urine flow. Encourage the patient to drink more water to facilitate urine excretion.
3）Perineal care   Keep the perineum clean, perform daily warm water irrigation of the perineum, and use iodine alcohol swabs to disinfect the external orifice of the urethra.

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->
Rectal and Anal Injury: Check for anal pain, tenderness, and bleeding. If necessary, perform an anal examination to determine the location of rectal injury.

Neurological Injury: Pay attention to numbness and motor disorders in the perineal area and lower limbs to determine if there is lumbar sacral or sciatic nerve injury.

Pressure Sores: To prevent fracture displacement, do not move or change positions arbitrarily, but avoid prolonged pressure on the local skin to prevent pressure sores. Use 50% safflower alcohol to massage the compressed skin every 2 hours, and use pressure-relieving devices reasonably to prevent pressure sores.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
7.  Functional Exercise
          If the patient's pain is not significant 6 hours after surgery, guide them to perform ankle joint movements of the affected limb and encourage active movement of the limb. From postoperative day 5, guide the patient to perform isometric contractions of the quadriceps muscles.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
# Health Education
Strengthen the publicity of traffic accident prevention, pay attention to safety during outdoor activities.
Strengthen the education of personnel engaged in high-altitude and underground work, pay attention to the safety and standardization of operations to reduce the occurrence of danger.
During on-site first aid and patient transport, pay attention to local protection, provide proper fixation to prevent exacerbation of trauma.
During medical knowledge education for patients, explain the significance of self-care, eliminate excessive dependence, and greatly mobilize the patient's subjective initiative to restore self-care ability. Provide detailed and specific self-care guidance, such as eating, washing face, and brushing teeth.

<!-- Slide number: 37 -->
Discharge Instructions

1. Continue with the prescribed medication as advised by the doctor, schedule regular follow-up appointments, and seek medical attention for any discomfort.

2. Enhance nutrition by consuming a diet rich in calcium, such as bone broth, and refrain from smoking and drinking alcohol to promote fracture healing.

3. Follow the rehabilitation plan for functional exercises to prevent muscle atrophy and joint stiffness; for fractures that do not affect the integrity of the pelvic ring, early bed exercises can include upper limb extension and lower limb muscle contraction activities; after one week, semi-recumbent and sitting exercises can be performed, along with hip and knee extension movements; between 4 to 6 weeks, patients can stand and walk slowly, gradually increasing the amount of activity, followed by practicing normal walking and squatting. For fractures that affect the integrity of the pelvic ring without complications, patients should lie on a hard bed and perform upper limb exercises; after two weeks, start practicing semi-recumbent positions and exercises for lower limb muscle contraction to maintain muscle strength and prevent joint stiffness; after three weeks, perform hip and knee exercises in bed, gradually transitioning from passive to active exercises; between 6 to 8 weeks, remove traction and crutches for walking; after 12 weeks, gradually discontinue the use of crutches and walk independently.

4. One month and three months after discharge, schedule follow-up appointments to check for any displacement of internal fixation and the healing status of the fracture.
