The tissue remains ischemic, hypoxic, and malnourished, leading to soft tissue ulceration and necrosis.**

**Stages of Decubitus Ulcer**

I **Erythema and Edema Phase**: After the body is locally compressed for a long time, local blood circulation is poor, the tissue in the compressed area is ischemic and hypoxic, the small arteries reactively dilate, making the local area congested, and the local skin shows erythema and mild edema. If the compression continues, the skin turns purple.

**Prevention is the key!!**

II **Inflammatory Edema Phase**: Capillary permeability increases, and local blisters of varying sizes appear. The dermis and subcutaneous tissue also swell further, the skin becomes red and congested, and the hardening of the tissue is more obvious.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->

**Stages of Decubitus Ulcer**

III **Ulcer Phase**
Shallow Ulcer Phase: The ulcer is superficial, and the depth does not exceed the full thickness of the skin. Clinical manifestations include irregular ulcer edges, pale base (due to impaired blood supply), poor growth of granulation tissue. Thick and hard scar tissue can also be found around the wound edges.

IV **Deep Ulcer Phase**: The shallow ulcer develops deeper, involving fascia, muscle, and even bone. Clinical manifestations include the wound showing black necrotic appearance; if there is concurrent bacterial infection, the wound discharge often has an odor, and is purulent.

Preventive measures
1. Dietary care: For patients in the early stage of fracture, consume easily digestible, light food, and eat in small portions. Guide patients to eat high-calorie, high-protein, high-fiber, and vitamin-rich foods. Reasonably match coarse and fine grains in three meals a day, ensuring that the amount of vegetables and fruits in daily diet is not less than 250 grams, as fiber can increase the physiological stimulation of the digestive tract, increase the weight and volume of feces, enhance the stimulation of the intestinal wall, strengthen peristalsis, shorten the time for the passage of intestinal contents, increase the water content of feces, soften the feces, and facilitate defecation.
2. Drink plenty of water: In cases where the condition or anesthesia allows, restore the patient's intake of food and water as soon as possible. Drink a cup of dilute salt water of 300-400 milliliters 6 hours after the operation, and drink a cup of warm water on an empty stomach every morning. Before going to bed, mix two spoons of honey with water and take it, and drink at least 3000 milliliters of water every day, as water acts as a lubricant, and food fiber can fully absorb water in the intestines to expand, soften the feces, increase the volume and weight of the feces, stimulate peristalsis, induce defecation reflex, and promote defecation.
3. Appropriate exercise: Encourage orthopedic patients to exercise the unharmed limb, and perform abdominal massage in the direction of intestinal peristalsis. Teach patients to do anal retentive exercises, etc., which are helpful to promote intestinal motility and prevent constipation.
4. Cultivate the habit of regular defecation: In daily life, develop good regularity, eat and defecate at regular times, use the toilet at regular times, and reduce personnel movement when the patient defecates, providing a good environment for the patient (such as using a screen to block), protecting the patient's privacy. In addition, rely on conditioned reflexes to help patients establish good defecation habits, and tell the patient to inform the nursing staff or family members when they have a defecation urge, avoiding suppression of defecation.

Stress Ulcer

Stress ulcer is a relatively common postoperative complication in patients with fractures, with mild presentations possibly including hematemesis and melena, while severe presentations may lead to hemorrhagic shock and threaten life. Therefore, stress ulcer in patients with fractures after surgery should not be overlooked. Effective prevention of stress ulcer and active control of gastrointestinal bleeding are of great significance for controlling the condition and improving the prognosis of patients.

2026/5/11

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->

**Causes**

Stress ulcer occurs in severe trauma, where the body is in a state of shock, hypoxia, and hypovolemia. Under the action of the nervous-endocrine system, blood is redistributed; it prioritizes the supply to the so-called "life organs" such as the heart and brain, leaving the gastrointestinal organs in a state of hypoxia, thereby destroying the gastric mucosal barrier function.

Due to mental stimulation after trauma and the use of drugs and foods that irritate the gastric mucosa, there is a disturbance in the blood circulation of the gastric mucosa, abnormal secretion of the gastrointestinal tract, and damage to the gastric mucosa, leading to ulcers.

Pain caused by trauma, local bleeding acting on the central nervous system, increases the secretion of adrenaline, the release of histamine, and stimulates the hypersecretion of gastric acid and pepsin. Both act on the mucosa, resulting in mucosal erosion, necrosis, and even perforation of the gastrointestinal tract.

**Urinary System Infection**

Orthopedic patients undergoing surgery are more common, and patients with lower limb surgery or lumbar surgery often use epidural anesthesia or general anesthesia, and need to have a urinary catheter inserted. The insertion of a urinary catheter is a major risk factor for hospital-acquired urinary tract infections.

2026/5/11

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->

**Catheterization Nursing**

1. Prevent urinary tract infection: (1) Maintain cleanliness of the urethral orifice. Female patients use a cotton ball soaked in disinfectant to wipe the vulva and urethral orifice, and male patients use a cotton ball soaked in disinfectant to wipe the urethral orifice, glans, and prepuce, 1-2 times a day. (2) Change the urine bag at regular intervals, empty the urine bag in time, and record the urine volume. (3) Change the catheter once a week, and the replacement cycle of silicone catheters can be extended as appropriate.
2. Encourage patients to drink plenty of water to achieve the purpose of natural irrigation of the urinary tract.
3. Train bladder reflex function, which can be done by intermittently clamping the catheter, opening it once every 3-4 hours, so that the bladder fills and empties at regular intervals, promoting the recovery of bladder function.
4. Pay attention to the patient's complaints and observe the urine, and treat it promptly if the urine is cloudy, precipitated, or contains crystals. Perform a urine routine test once a week.

2026/5/11

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->

**Daily Nursing**

(1) Drink plenty of water consistently
　The urine excreted by the kidneys has a flushing effect on the bladder and urethra, which is conducive to the excretion of bacteria. Drinking plenty of water and urinating every 2-3 hours can prevent the reproduction of bacteria in the urinary tract, reduce the incidence of urinary tract infection, and is the most practical and effective method of prevention. During the acute stage or remission stage of the disease, drinking plenty of water is also conducive to the recovery of the disease. Drinking tea or using light bamboo leaves as a beverage also has a certain preventive effect.
(2) Pay attention to personal hygiene
　Female genitalia and the urethral orifice are inhabited by a large number of bacteria, which are the prerequisite for the occurrence of urinary tract infection. Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to the cleanliness of the genitalia, to frequently clean and change underwear, and to pay special attention during menstruation, pregnancy, and puerperium. Infants should be changed frequently.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) refers to the abnormal coagulation within the lower limb venous cavity. The blood components such as fibrin, platelets, and red blood cells form thrombi in the deep venous blood vessels of the lower limb, blocking the venous cavity and causing venous return obstruction. If not treated promptly, it can lead to complete or partial loss of lower limb function, resulting in disability, and can also cause fatal pulmonary embolism.

Clinical manifestations - Symptoms

1. Unexplained dyspnea and shortness of breath, especially after exertion, which is the most common symptom of PTE.
2. Chest pain.
3. Syncope, which can be the only or initial symptom of PTE.
