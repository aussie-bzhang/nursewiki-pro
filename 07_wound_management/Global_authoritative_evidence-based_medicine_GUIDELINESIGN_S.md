Purpose of Preventive Use of Antibiotics in the Perioperative Period

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Purpose of Preventive Medication

The purpose of preventive medication is to prevent surgical site infections (SSI), including superficial incision infections, deep incision infections, and infections of organs and spaces involved in surgery. It cannot prevent infections in other postoperative sites, such as postoperative pulmonary infections, urinary tract infections, and catheter-related bloodstream infections.

Definition, Classification, and Diagnostic Criteria of SSI

SSI (Surgical site infections) Definition: Infections occurring in the incision or deep organs or spaces during the perioperative period (such as incision infection, brain abscess, peritonitis)
(Surgical site infections (SSIs) are infections of the incision or organ or space that occur after surgery.)

The concept of SSI is broader than incision infection but narrower than postoperative infection.
SSI accounts for about 15% of all hospital infections.
It accounts for about 35% to 40% of hospital infections in surgical patients.

Diagnostic Criteria of SSI (CDC)

Infections occurring within 30 days after surgery that involve only the skin or subcutaneous tissue of the incision, and meet one of the following conditions:
1. Purulent fluid is present in the superficial tissue of the incision.
2. Pathogens are cultured from the fluid or tissue of the superficial tissue of the incision.
3. Symptoms or signs of infection are present, including local erythema, swelling, fever, pain, and tenderness, with the superficial layer of the incision opened by the surgeon.
The following situations do not constitute superficial incisional tissue infection:
1. Pustules at needle holes (limited to minor inflammation and slight secretion at the site where the suture passes).
2. Infections at the site of episiotomy or circumcision, or around the perineum after perineal surgery.
3. Infected burn wounds, and escharated II and III degree burn wounds.

Superficial Incisional Tissue Infection

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Diagnostic Criteria of SSI (CDC)

Infections occurring within 30 days after surgery in patients without implants, and within 1 year after surgery in patients with implants, that involve deep soft tissue (such as fascia and/or muscle layer) and meet one of the following conditions:
1. Purulent fluid is drained or aspirated from the deep tissue of the incision, but the fluid is not from the organ/cavity part.
2. The deep tissue of the incision spontaneously opens or is opened by the surgeon. At the same time, the patient has symptoms or signs of infection, including local fever, swelling, and pain.
3. Evidence of abscess or other infection in the deep tissue of the incision is found through direct examination, reoperation, pathology, or imaging.

Surgical organs are sterile parts of the human body, with no inflammation, no injury, and no involvement of respiratory, digestive, urinary, and reproductive organs that are in direct contact with the external environment. The surgical site is not contaminated, and it is usually not necessary to use preventive antimicrobial agents.
However, preventive medication may be considered in the following situations: ① Large surgical scope, long surgery time, increased risk of contamination; ② Surgery involving important organs, where infection can cause serious consequences, such as cranial surgery, cardiac surgery, etc.; ③ Implantation surgery, such as artificial heart valve implantation, permanent cardiac pacemaker placement, artificial joint replacement, etc.; ④ Patients with high-risk factors for infection, such as elderly, diabetes, low immune function (especially those receiving organ transplantation), malnutrition, etc.
Surgical sites exist with a large number of human resident flora, which may contaminate the surgical site during surgery and lead to infection. Therefore, such surgeries usually require preventive use of antimicrobial agents.
Surgical procedures where the surgical site has been severely contaminated. Such surgeries require preventive use of antimicrobial agents
Antimicrobial agents have been used for therapeutic purposes before surgery, and continued during and after surgery. This does not fall under the category of preventive application.
Preventive use of antimicrobial agents during the perioperative period should be decided based on a comprehensive consideration of factors such as the type of surgical incision, degree of surgical trauma, possible types of contaminating bacteria, duration of surgery, risk of infection and severity of consequences, evidence-based medicine on the preventive effect of antimicrobial agents, impact on bacterial resistance, and economic evaluation. However, the preventive use of antimicrobial agents cannot replace strict disinfection and sterilization techniques and meticulous aseptic operations, nor can it replace other preventive measures such as intraoperative warming and blood glucose control.

Preventive use of antimicrobial agents and SSI incidence
1. The subjects of the literature 3 were all patients who underwent clean surgery in the Department of Neurosurgery of the Tian Tan Hospital Affiliated with Capital Medical University from September 1, 2011, to September 23, 2011, and the same period in 2010, a total of 679 cases. The incidence of postoperative surgical site infection (SSI) was statistically analyzed, and risk factors were analyzed. The results showed that during the period of restricted preventive use of antibiotics in 2011 (the proportion of preventive use of antibiotics was 36.6%), the incidence of postoperative SSI was 16.5%, significantly higher than the level of 5.0% in the same period in 2010 (the proportion of preventive use of antibiotics was 80.7%).
The use of preventive antibiotics is an independent factor for reducing the incidence of postoperative SSI (OR value of 0.3, P<0.001).
Preventive use of antibiotics can reduce the incidence of postoperative SSI by 65.0% (P<0.01), especially the incidence of postoperative incision infection can be reduced by 81% (P<0.01), which can shorten the median hospital stay by 2 days, reduce the median hospitalization cost by 7%.
Conclusion: The perioperative preventive use of antibiotics in neurosurgery can greatly reduce the incidence of postoperative SSI, is an independent protective factor for postoperative SSI, can significantly reduce hospital stay and hospitalization costs, and has good economic and health effects.

Zhang, Ji, Gao, et al. The impact of perioperative preventive use of antibiotics on surgical site infection in neurosurgery [J]. Chinese Journal of Neurosurgery, 2015, 31(10):984-987.

2. Inguinal hernia repair is a common surgical procedure, with the surgical incision classified as a clean incision, i.e., Class I incision. It is generally not recommended to use antibiotics for the prevention of SSI. Literature reports that the probability of SSI for this surgery is 0-9%. Currently, there is no consensus internationally regarding the use of preventive antibiotics in tension-free inguinal hernia repair. Literature 4 conducted a systematic review of domestic and international randomized controlled clinical trials to explore the preventive effect of antibiotics on SSI in open tension-free inguinal hernia repair.

(1) Methods: Search the Cochrane Library of Clinical Trials, PubMed, EMbase, China Biomedical Literature Database, and China Academic Journal Network Full-text Database. The search period was from the establishment of the database to March 2012.

(2) Study Population: Adults with inguinal hernia, including primary and/or secondary, direct and/or indirect, unilateral and/or bilateral, undergoing open tension-free hernia repair for hernia repair, excluding incisional hernia and laparoscopic hernia repair.

(3) Intervention Measures: The intervention group received preventive antibiotic use before surgery. The control group received a placebo before surgery.

(4) Outcome Measures: The primary outcome was the rate of surgical site infection. SSI was assessed for surgical patient incisional infections according to the American CDC standards, with a postoperative follow-up time of ≥30 days. Secondary outcomes were the incidence of deep surgical site infection and superficial surgical site infection.

(5) Literature Search Results: A total of 108 relevant literature were retrieved, and finally, 7 RCTs (Randomized Controlled Trials) met the inclusion criteria, all of which were in English.

Preventive Antibiotic Use and SSI Incidence
Literature 4 conducted a meta-analysis from three aspects on the preventive antibiotic use group and the control group, i.e., the incidence of SSI, SSSI, and DSSI. The results of 7 studies involving 2569 patients showed that for patients with inguinal hernia undergoing open tension-free hernia repair, the use of preventive antibiotics before surgery can effectively reduce the incidence of SSI and SSSI, but has little effect on the incidence of DSSI. In 2011, the Ministry of Health of China strictly controlled the preventive use of antibiotics for Class I surgical incisions, with an overall usage rate not exceeding 30.0%. As a result, many hospitals have stipulated that hernia repair surgery should not use antibiotics. However, the meta-analysis results indicate that for patients undergoing mesh repair hernia repair, the clinical use of antibiotics before surgery can reduce the incidence of superficial surgical site infection.

On the other hand, the final decision to administer perioperative antibiotics for preventive use should not only be based on the effectiveness of antibiotics in preventing surgical site infection but also consider the magnitude of the effect (e.g., as shown in this meta-analysis: NNT=45, indicating that antibiotic use can prevent 1 case of superficial incisional infection every 45 cases), the likelihood of infection without antibiotic use, the severity of complications from infection, the economic analysis of antibiotics, and the potential risks of using antibiotics, such as adverse drug reactions and the consequences of inducing drug-resistant bacteria. Therefore, Literature 4 believes that for patients with ordinary open tension-free hernia repair, due to the limited preventive effect of antibiotics and the risk of inducing drug resistance with widespread use, it is not advisable to routinely recommend the use of antibiotics. However, if patients have high-risk factors, such as elderly age, diabetes, and impaired immune function, it should be encouraged to use them regularly.
Hu Mengbo, Huang Naishi, Hu Bijie, et al. Meta-analysis of the efficacy of preventive antibiotics in tension-free inguinal hernia repair for preventing surgical site infection [J]. Chinese Journal of Hospital Infection, 2012, 22(12):2647-2650.

Timing of Administration
★Administration too early is ineffective, as it is shooting in the dark.
★Antibiotics should be administered 0.5 to 1 hour before the skin and mucous membrane incision or at the start of anesthesia. The surgery should begin after the infusion is completed to ensure that the local tissue at the surgical site has reached a drug concentration sufficient to kill bacteria that may be contaminated during the surgical procedure. Vancomycin or fluoroquinolones, which require a longer infusion, should be administered 1 to 2 hours before the surgery. If the surgery duration exceeds 3 hours or more than twice the half-life of the drug used, or if the adult blood loss exceeds 1500ml, an additional dose should be administered during the surgery.
★The ASHP/IDSA/SIS/SHEA guidelines recommend "additional administration of the drug beyond two half-lives when surgery is performed," which provides specific recommended intervals for each commonly used drug, making it more specific than the recommendations in China and worthy of reference. If the patient experiences prolonged or excessive bleeding during the surgery or in situations that may shorten the half-life of the antibacterial drug (such as extensive burns), additional administration should also be considered. However, it is not recommended to administer additional doses when liver or kidney function is impaired, which may increase the half-life of the antibacterial drug.

Timing of Administration
1. Literature 6 analyzed the benefits of administering antibiotics before cesarean section and after umbilical cord ligation for both mothers and newborns.
Method: Computerized searches of the Cochrane Library, MEDLINE, EMbase, PUBMED, China Science and Technology Journal Full-text Database, VIP Electronic Journal Full-text Database, Wanfang Database, and manual searches of related conference proceedings. Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.0.
Results: Five studies were included, involving a total of 1425 patients.
The meta-analysis results suggest that there is no significant difference in wound infection rates between preoperative administration and post-umbilical cord ligation administration [OR=0.59, 95%CI(0.33, 1.07), P=0.08]; the incidence of endometritis is lower in the preoperative administration group compared to the post-umbilical cord ligation group, with a significant difference [OR=0.47, 95%CI(0.27, 0.81), P=0.007]; there is no significant difference in the overall incidence between the two groups [OR=0.91, 95%CI(0.41, 2.00), P=0.82]; there is no significant difference in neonatal sepsis between preoperative administration and post-umbilical cord ligation administration [OR=0.86, 95%CI(0.49, 1.49), P=0.59]; there is no significant difference in neonatal suspected sepsis between the two groups [OR=0.85, 95%CI(0.60, 1.21), P=0.37]; there is no significant difference in neonatal admission to the RICU between the two groups [OR=0.95, 95%CI(0.62, 145), P=0.81].
Conclusion: Preoperative administration of antibiotics for cesarean section is more beneficial for mothers than post-umbilical cord ligation administration, with no significant difference for newborns.
Zhao Yiming, Hu Yongfang. Systematic evaluation of the timing of prophylactic administration of antibacterial drugs during cesarean section [C]// National Maternal and Child Health Pharmacology Conference. 2011.

Selection of Drug Types
1. Consider the type of surgical incision, possible types of contaminating bacteria, their susceptibility to antibacterial drugs, and whether the drug can reach an effective concentration at the surgical site.
2. Choose a drug that is highly targeted against possible contaminating bacteria, has sufficient evidence of effective prophylaxis, is safe, easy to use, and reasonably priced.
3. It is preferable to choose a single antibacterial drug for prophylactic use, avoiding unnecessary combination use. Prophylactic use should target the possible contaminating bacteria along the surgical pathway. For example, for cardiovascular, head and neck, chest and abdominal wall, extremity soft tissue surgery, and orthopedic surgery, antibacterial drugs targeted against Staphylococcus aureus are usually chosen. For colorectal and pelvic surgery, antibacterial drugs targeting Gram-negative bacteria and anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides fragilis should be chosen.
4. For patients with a history of cephalosporin allergy, vancomycin, teicoplanin, and clindamycin can be used against Gram-positive bacteria; aztreonam, fosfomycin, or aminoglycosides can be used against Gram-negative bacilli.
5. For surgical sites where infection can cause serious consequences, such as heart valve replacement surgery and artificial joint replacement surgery, if there is a possibility of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) colonization before surgery or if the incidence of MRSA is high in the institution, vancomycin, teicoplanin can be used for prophylactic infection, but the duration of drug use should be strictly controlled.
6. It should not be arbitrary to select broad-spectrum antibacterial drugs as prophylactic drugs during the perioperative period. Given the high resistance rate of Escherichia coli to fluoroquinolones in China, the use of fluoroquinolones as prophylactic drugs during the perioperative period should be strictly controlled.

The 2015 edition of the "Guidelines for Clinical Application of Antimicrobial Agents" requires that the prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents in clean surgery not exceed 24 hours, and the time for cardiac surgery can be extended to 48 hours as needed. The prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents in clean-polluted surgery and polluted surgery is also 24 hours, and it can be extended to 48 hours if necessary. Prolonging the duration of prophylactic use does not necessarily improve the prophylactic effect and increases the opportunity for resistance to antimicrobial agents if the duration of prophylactic use exceeds 48 hours.
2. The ASHP/IDSA/SIS/SHEA guidelines recommend shortening the postoperative course of antibacterial drugs as much as possible, with most recommendations for only one dose before surgery or not more than 24 hours, while the use of prophylactic antibacterial drugs should consider the presence of intravascular catheters and drainage tubes.
3. For severe contamination or existing infection or organ perforation (class IV incision), antibiotics should be continued for therapeutic purposes after surgery and should not be used as prophylaxis.

Prophylactic Drug Regimen
[Yang Zhiying et al. 2000] A multicenter, randomized controlled study was conducted in 15 hospitals across the country to observe the effect of long-term and short-term antibiotic use in the prevention of surgical site infection. 731 patients undergoing abdominal surgery were randomly divided into two groups: short-term antibiotic use group (postoperative medication for 1 day) and long-term antibiotic use group (postoperative medication for 3 days) to prevent surgical site infection. There were no differences in age, gender, and type of surgery between the two groups. The prophylactic antibiotics were netilmicin or combined with metronidazole. The infection rate in the short-term use group was 0.84% (3/358), and the infection rate in the long-term use group was 2.68% (10/373). There was no significant difference between the two groups (P>0.05).
Conclusion: Short-term application of antibiotics during the perioperative period can effectively prevent postoperative surgical site infection, and it is also superior to long-term use from the perspective of pharmacoeconomics and rational drug use.
① Type I surgery: Re-administration of medication within 24 hours after surgery; Type II surgery: Re-administration of medication 8 hours after the first dose, followed by medication within 24 hours.
② Long-term use group: Medication is administered once according to the above method before surgery. Type I surgery: Continue medication for 3 days after surgery. Type II surgery: Re-administration of medication 8 hours after the first dose, followed by continued medication for 3 days.
Observation indicators: Postoperative abdominal and wound infection rates (SSI); side effects such as hearing abnormalities, liver and kidney function abnormalities, and allergic reactions after medication.

How to Prevent SSI
Preventing SSI involves not only drug control but also the control of infection factors.

Intraoperative Factors

Operating room environment, surgical attire, and disinfection curtains
Patient Factors

Diabetes, smoking, hormone use, malnutrition, long-term hospitalization before surgery, nasal colonization with Staphylococcus aureus before surgery, perioperative blood transfusion
Preoperative Factors

Preoperative antiseptic shower, hair removal, skin preparation, hand and forearm disinfection, prophylactic use of antimicrobial agents, management of infected or colonized personnel
Postoperative Factors

Wound care, discharge plan

7. Randomized controlled trials have shown that the application of antimicrobial dressings on the surgical site after primary suture in the operating room has no significant benefits or harms for the prevention of SSI. (No recommendation strength/unresolved issues)

Summary
1. Strengthen the monitoring and management of prophylactic antibiotic use during the perioperative period to reduce the incidence of postoperative surgical site infections (SSI).
2. Fully assess various infection risk factors during the perioperative period.
3. Both "drug control" and "infection control" must be stringent in the prevention of SSI during the perioperative period.
