# Perioperative Concept
    Specifically refers to the period from the time of determining surgical treatment to the basic completion of treatment related to this surgery, approximately 5-7 days before surgery to 7-12 days after surgery.

# Surgical Classification
Elective Surgery: The timing of surgery has a significant impact on treatment outcomes, allowing sufficient time to complete all preoperative preparations to reduce postoperative complications, such as inguinal hernia repair.

Conditional Surgery: Although the timing of surgery can be chosen, it should not be delayed for too long. Preoperative preparations should be completed as soon as possible. Examples include radical resection of various malignant tumors.

Emergency Surgery: For life-threatening diseases, necessary preparations should be completed as soon as possible, and emergency surgery should be performed in the shortest possible time to save the patient's life, such as splenic rupture.

Respiratory Preparation
Smoking Cessation: 2 weeks before surgery
Antibacterial Treatment: Applicable to patients with pulmonary infection and sputum production
Deep Breathing: Abdominal surgery training thoracic breathing, chest surgery training abdominal breathing
Effective Coughing: Sit or semi-reclining, slightly forward tilt of the upper body, cough lightly several times first, then take a deep breath and cough strongly.

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Urine Training  Postoperative patients may experience urinary retention due to the effects of anesthesia and surgery, as well as the lack of habit of urination in bed. Preoperative training should be carried out.
Rest  Sufficient rest plays an indispensable role in the patient's recovery.
Other Preparations  Preoperative blood typing and cross-matching tests should be performed for patients undergoing major and medium-sized surgeries; measure vital signs in the morning on the day of surgery. If the patient has an elevated body temperature or blood pressure, or if the female patient has her menstrual period, notify the physician in time. If necessary, delay the surgery; remove dentures, hairpins, glasses, watches, jewelry, and other items before the patient enters the operating room; empty the urine. For patients with a long surgery time or those undergoing pelvic surgery, indwelling catheterization should be performed to keep the bladder in a空虚 state, so as not to injure it during surgery; prepare the necessary surgical items and bring them into the operating room with the patient.

# Surgical Site Skin Preparation
Skin preparation is an important link in preventing incisional infection.
Skin preparation includes hair removal and skin cleaning.
The time for skin preparation should be as close as possible to the start of surgery. If the time for skin preparation has exceeded 24 hours, it should be prepared again.

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Health Education
Diet  Eat foods rich in protein, energy, vitamins, and dietary fiber before and after surgery.
Rest  Combine work and rest, take appropriate rest, ensure sufficient sleep. This can promote appetite, improve the nutritional status of the body, and enhance the immune function.
Prevention of Infection  Prevent upper respiratory tract infection. The patient should not leave the hospital carelessly and keep warm. Try to avoid or reduce visits from family members and friends with recent respiratory tract infections to prevent cross-infection.
Prevention of Postoperative Complications  The patient should be trained in effective coughing and bed self-urination before surgery; those with a smoking habit should stop smoking for 2 weeks.

Abdominal Distension Care
Cause: suppression of gastrointestinal function; peritonitis or hypokalemia

Prevention: encourage early ambulation;
             start not eating high-sugar foods and dairy products.
Treatment: gastrointestinal decompression, anal tube exhaust, hypertonic solution low-pressure
             enema, etc.;
             for non-gastrointestinal surgery, use drugs to promote gastrointestinal motility recovery;
             for mechanical intestinal obstruction, if non-surgical treatment does not alleviate, complete preoperative preparation and perform surgery again.

# Fever Care
After surgery, due to the body's response to surgical trauma, the body temperature of the postoperative patient may slightly rise, but generally not more than 38℃, which is clinically known as surgical fever or postoperative absorption fever
If the fever persists for 3-6 days after surgery, it indicates the presence of infection or other adverse reactions
Treatment Methods
Surgical fever does not require special treatment

For high fever, physical cooling, such as ice bag cooling, alcohol sponge bath, etc., and necessary application of antipyretic analgesics (Anodyne 2ml im or Lomotil 0.9 im)

# Urinary Retention Care
Cause
              Systemic infection
         Postoperative suppression of micturition reflex due to spinal anesthesia, and incision pain causing bladder and posterior
         urethral sphincter reflex spasm
         Patients not accustomed to bedridden urination, etc.
     If the patient has not urinated for 6-8 hours after surgery, and there is a dull area on the suprapubic area when percussion, it can be diagnosed as urinary retention.
  Treatment Methods
   1. No contraindications: assist the patient to sit on the edge of the bed or stand to establish micturition reflex, such as listening to running water sound, lower abdominal hot compress
     2. Self-massage
     3. Catheterization. Note: if the first catheterization volume exceeds 500ml, the catheter should be left in place for 1-2 days. The first urine volume should not exceed 800-1000ml

Pain Care
   Generally, the pain is most severe within 24 hours after surgery, and gradually decreases 2-3 days later.  If the pain is persistent or increases again, be alert to the possibility of incision infection

  Treatment Methods
   For minor surgery, oral analgesics (Fortral 30mg po) have a good effect on skin and muscle pain.  For major surgery, intramuscular injection of pethidine is often required within 1-2 days.  If necessary, use a pain pump for analgesia

# Pneumonia and Atelectasis
Cause: smoking history
              Acute or chronic respiratory tract infection before surgery
              Postoperative factors causing respiratory tract infection (anesthetic agents, 
              endotracheal intubation and oxygen inhalation causing increased bronchial secretions)
              Severe postoperative pain, or high incision in the chest or abdomen
              Lack of activity after surgery
              Use of analgesic drugs

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
# Pneumonia and Atelectasis
Clinical manifestations: early postoperative fever, increased respiratory and heart rate, decreased, or absent breath sounds on auscultation, localized moist rales
Treatment Methods:
           Preoperative deep breathing exercises, smoking cessation, and treatment of bronchitis or chronic pulmonary infection;
             Aspiration of bronchial secretions before extubation in general anesthesia surgery;
             Prevent aspiration of vomit and secretions after surgery;
             Thoracic and abdominal bandages should be wrapped loosely to avoid fixation or binding that restricts breathing;
             Turn and pat the back and position for expectoration;
       Deep breathing, self-coughing to expectorate sputum;
       Ultrasonic atomization;
       Antibiotic treatment.

Postoperative Hemorrhage
Occurs in surgical incisions, hollow organs, and body cavities
Wound dressing soaked in blood or a small amount of blood drained from the drainage tube (small bleeding)

### Clinical Manifestations
Hemorrhagic shock manifestations (large amount of bleeding)
Minor bleeding: Change dressings, apply pressure bandaging, use hemostatic agents (Liqi Zhiji, Zhizhi Fangsuan, etc.)

### Management Methods

Large amount of bleeding: Replenish blood volume, surgical exploration and hemostasis

# Incision Complications
Causes
Poor physical condition, chronic anemia, malnutrition, or obesity
Hematoma or dead space in the incision
Poor incision protection after surgery
Severe abdominal distension after surgery increases abdominal wall incision tension
Severe coughing, sneezing, vomiting, and increased abdominal pressure from forceful defecation
Inadequate suture technique

### Incision Dehiscence
Commonly seen after abdominal surgery and near joint areas
Occur commonly within one week after surgery
Contraindication: Do not immediately return abdominal contents
Complete dehiscence
Classification

Partial dehiscence
Management Methods
Complete dehiscence: Immediately cover the incision and protruding organs with sterile saline gauze, followed by surgical treatment

Partial dehiscence: Secure the incision with butterfly strips and apply abdominal bandage for compression

### Discharge Instructions

Diet: Consume a balanced diet with appropriate calories, protein, and rich vitamins. Patients who have had gastric resection should eat in small, frequent meals.

Rest and Activity: Pay attention to the combination of work and rest, engage in light physical activities such as walking to gradually recover strength: Do not lift heavy objects within 6 weeks after surgery.

Medication and Treatment: Patients should take medication as prescribed and in the correct dosage. To avoid and delay tumor recurrence, and to extend survival, tumor patients should continue chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

Follow-up and Review: If patients have a body temperature >38°C, the wound drainage has an odor, the incision is red and swollen, or there are abnormal abdominal pain, bloating, or cessation of defecation and flatus after discharge, they should seek medical attention promptly. General patients should follow up at the outpatient department 1-3 months after surgery to understand the degree of recovery and incision healing. Tumor patients should follow up at the outpatient department 2-4 weeks after surgery to formulate a continued treatment plan.
