Julius Caesar (Caesar the Great)

### Notes:
Named after Julius Caesar
Legend has it that the first successful cesarean section was performed to deliver Julius Caesar, a famous Roman emperor; the persistence of this legend gave rise to the name of the procedure. However, given the fact that his mother survived from his birth, most authorities doubt Caesar really was born in this manner, because surviving cesarean section was virtually unknown until the twentieth century.

【Amniotic Fluid Embolism】
AFI incidence rate 1:8000-1:80000, 25% die within 1 hour after onset, with late intervention by experienced obstetricians.
Laboratory diagnosis is delayed, diagnosis is clear, and it is already late or death.
Typical AFI is sudden and severe, even with treatment, life cannot be saved.
The real rescue is for those with mild symptoms, often with atypical prodromal symptoms or one manifestation of typical symptoms.
Emphasizing the identification of atypical AFI is the key to reducing mortality.

US AFI National Clinical Diagnostic Criteria
The diagnosis of AFI mainly depends on clinical symptoms and signs.
Acute hypotension or cardiac arrest.
Acute hypoxia, manifested as dyspnea, cyanosis, or respiratory arrest.
Coagulation disorders, laboratory data of fibrinolysis or unexplained severe hemorrhage.
The above symptoms occur within 30 minutes of cervical dilation, delivery, cesarean section, or postpartum.
Finding fetal particulate matter in the pulmonary vessels is no longer a diagnostic criterion, but only supportive of the diagnosis.
For atypical cases, diagnosis is confirmed after excluding other causes.

### Notes:
Currently internationally recognized
Lack of other meaningful explanations for the above symptoms

Wet Lung and Hyaline Membrane Lesions

Idiopathic Respiratory Distress Syndrome

Selective cesarean section, no uterine contractions, fetal head and chest wall not compressed
Easier to have amniotic fluid
or meconium aspiration

Excessive fluid retained in the alveoli, forming wet lung

### Notes:
The fetal alveoli are filled with fluid, and during normal labor, about 1/2 to 2/3 of the alveolar fluid is squeezed out through the narrow birth canal when the chest is compressed. After starting to breathe, air enters the alveoli, and the remaining alveolar fluid is absorbed by the capillaries of the alveolar wall. If there is too much fluid in the alveoli, absorption is delayed and affects gas exchange, causing respiratory distress. Children born by cesarean section have not been compressed through the birth canal, and at least 1/3 of the fetal lung fluid cannot be expelled, and some cannot breathe spontaneously after birth, making them prone to complications such as neonatal asphyxia and hyaline membrane disease.

Endometriosis

Cesarean section abdominal scar endometriosis and pelvic endometriosis
More common in mid-trimester cesarean section for fetal extraction, preterm cesarean section (more common in iatrogenic preterm deliveries caused by cesarean sections without medical indication).
Causes: During surgery, microscopically active endometrial fragments are left behind and implanted in the abdominal incision, continuing to grow.
Pain related to menstruation, gradually enlarging mass visible on the abdominal wall, without redness and fever.
Early excision of the lesion site is recommended.

### Notes:
The longer the course is delayed, the wider and deeper the lesion invades, even invading the peritoneum.

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
Scar Pregnancy

Women with a history of cesarean section have a pregnancy where the gestational sac is located in the cesarean section scar, completely surrounded by uterine muscle and fibrous tissue, and not communicating with the uterine cavity.
Causes: The embryo invades the damaged scar site.
Scar pregnancy accounts for 6.11% of ectopic pregnancies in women with at least one cesarean section history.
The outcome is dangerous, early misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis is easy, and as the gestational age increases, it can lead to uterine perforation, rupture, and bleeding, which can be fatal in severe cases.

## Pelvic Adhesions or Chronic Pelvic Pain
Pelvic adhesions are a common complication following cesarean section.
Common symptoms include pelvic pain, infertility, sterility, and discomfort during sexual intercourse.
Chronic Pelvic Pain
67.2% of patients have a history of cesarean section, and the risk ratio (OR) for chronic pelvic pain is 3.7, indicating that a history of cesarean section is a major cause of chronic pelvic pain.

## Sensory Integration Disorder
Medically known as "sensory integration disorder."
Occurs in newborns who have not been squeezed through the birth canal.
Weak hand-eye coordination, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and a higher risk of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism later in life.
It weakens cognitive and adaptive abilities to varying degrees, thereby delaying the process of socialization.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
## Neurological and Psychiatric Disorders
Cesarean section delivery is associated with a 10-fold higher risk of early-onset schizophrenia (onset before 22 years of age) compared to late-onset schizophrenia.
Cause: Cesarean section leads to dysfunction of dopamine neurotransmitters in the central nervous system.
Cesarean section mothers lack prolonged skin-to-skin contact with their infants, causing a delayed establishment of the lactation reflex and the use of "artificial feeding."

## Immune Function Deficiency
The levels of immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C4) in cesarean section infants are significantly lower than those in vaginally delivered infants.
Low resistance to infection and a higher risk of infectious diseases.

# Mechanism and Management of Shoulder Dystocia (SHS)

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
# Mechanism and Management of Shoulder Dystocia (SHS)
1. After the large uterus compresses the inferior vena cava (indicated by the red blade tip in the diagram), blood pressure often drops sharply, and in cases of very low blood pressure, it may even compress the aorta, which is due to a sudden decrease in venous return blood volume. At the same time, there is a slight reduction in cardiac volume.
2. The distal blood flow of the segment of the inferior vena cava compressed by the large uterus can pass through the azygos vein, return to the heart via the superior vena cava, thus achieving partial compensation of venous return blood volume, and the blood pressure drop may not be significant. However, there are many anatomical anomalies in the azygos vein (generally unpredictable), so for patients with poor azygos vein compensatory return, the sharp drop in blood pressure is often particularly obvious, which obviously increases the risk.
3. To alleviate the compression of the large uterus on the inferior vena cava, cesarean section patients who have received epidural anesthesia should be placed in a right lateral decubitus position with the head elevated 20 degrees, so that the uterus shifts to the left away from the inferior vena cava.

<!-- Slide number: 43 -->
# Mechanism and Management of Shoulder Dystocia (SHS)
    (Left image) Normally, in the third trimester of pregnancy, when lying in the supine position, the entire large uterus is pressed on the inferior vena cava (indicated by blue on the right) and the abdominal aorta (indicated by red on the left) in front of the spine. Pregnant women cannot adapt to this interference of reduced venous return blood volume and will automatically change to a lateral position.
 (Right image) When the pregnant woman is in a lateral position, the large uterus moves away from the inferior vena cava and abdominal aorta in front of the spine.

<!-- Slide number: 44 -->
# Position for Anesthetized Pregnant Women - Left 20-30 Degrees
In the case of full-term labor, after the epidural anesthesia takes effect, the supporting effect of the abdominal muscles on the large uterus is eliminated. In the supine position, the large uterus is enough to completely flatten the inferior vena cava and abdominal aorta, so it is particularly prone to a sharp drop in blood pressure. In patients with good collateral flow through the azygos vein, compensation can be achieved; however, if the azygos vein compensatory collateral flow is poor, the patient can fall into a severe shock state. Therefore, it is emphasized in clinical practice that after epidural anesthesia, the patient must be placed in a right lateral decubitus position with the head elevated 20 degrees (or the operating bed tilted 20-30 degrees to the left) to allow the inferior vena cava to move away from the compression of the large uterus.

<!-- Slide number: 45 -->
# Prevention and Management of Low Blood Pressure Syndrome in Lying Supine Position in Parturients
1. Before cesarean section, it is routine to carefully inquire about the patient's preferences and changes in position during pregnancy, especially for patients with large fetus, twins, obesity, and asthenic parturients.
2. Before the administration of epidural anesthesia, 1000ml of crystalloid solution should be infused rapidly via the upper extremity vein to expand blood volume, and 10mg of ephedrine can be injected intramuscularly if necessary to prevent hypotension.
3. After the administration of local anesthetic, the operating bed should be tilted to the left by 20° to 30° to relieve the pressure of the uterus on the inferior vena cava.
4. In case of SHS, the following measures should be taken: ① Oxygen inhalation via a face mask; ② Increase the rate of intravenous infusion, and if the patient is receiving intravenous infusion in the lower limbs, immediately switch to the upper extremity vein to enhance the return of the superior vena cava; ③ Intravenous injection of ephedrine, but avoid methoxamine or neosynephrine, which have synergistic effects with oxytocin, to prevent a sudden rise in blood pressure; ④ Try to deliver the fetus as soon as possible to relieve the pressure of the uterus on the inferior vena cava; ⑤ Prepare for the rescue of neonatal asphyxia.

## Abnormal Uterine Bleeding
1. Large varicose veins on the incision surface can be ligated in advance.
2. Select an appropriate incision size and position to avoid forceful delivery of the fetus.
3. When suturing the two sides of the incision, extend beyond 0.5cm to avoid the retraction of blood vessels and prevent ligation.
4. In case of incision tearing, quickly clamp the top of the tear and the bleeding blood vessels, and suture to stop the bleeding promptly.
5. After the delivery of the fetal shoulder during the operation, oxytocin should be administered routinely, and prostaglandin preparations should be used appropriately for those with uterine atony tendency.

<!-- Slide number: 47 -->

Bleeding at the placental detachment surface should be sutured with an 8-shaped suture using absorbable thread, paying attention not to penetrate the uterus and misligate surrounding tissues.
For patients suspected of having coagulation dysfunction, prepare blood products and necessary hemostatic agents before the operation.

<!-- Slide number: 48 -->
## Organ Injury
For patients with a history of abdominal surgery, inflammation, and complications, preoperative discussion is required.
Ensure adequate anesthesia and analgesia to avoid bloating.
Routine placement of a urinary catheter before the operation.
For those estimated to have abdominal adhesions, try to incise the peritoneum at the head end or make a small incision in a thin peritoneal layer under direct vision to extend the incision, to avoid injury to the bladder and intestines.
For adhesions of the intestines, identify the direction of the intestines, and release the adhesions before cutting the uterus.

<!-- Slide number: 49 -->

When bleeding occurs due to incision tearing, do not clamp and suture blindly; if there is a hematoma in the broad ligament, separate the anterior and posterior leaves of the broad ligament, and identify the direction of the ureter.
When a total hysterectomy is required, the bladder should be freed to the posterior orifice of the cervix before the uterus is transected to avoid injury to the ureter.
Before closing the abdomen, check the lateral posterior walls of the lower segment of the uterus to avoid ignoring hematomas, tears, and intestinal injuries.

<!-- Slide number: 50 -->
## Postoperative Adhesions
Shorten the operation time to avoid tissue drying.
Reduce tissue damage during the operation.
Suck out the abdominal effusion before closing the abdomen to prevent the precipitation of fibrin in the blood, causing adhesions.
Apply anti-adhesive agents.
Encourage early postoperative activity to promote peristalsis.

<!-- Slide number: 51 -->
## Endometriosis of the Incisional Wound
Pay attention to protect the abdominal incision during the operation.
Use disposable gauze to clean the uterine cavity.
Suture the uterine incision without introducing the endometrium.
Wash the incision with saline before suturing the abdominal wall to avoid endometrial implantation.

<!-- Slide number: 52 -->
## Scar Pregnancy
Avoid cesarean section without indication.
Suture the layers of the uterine incision correctly to restore the anatomical structure.
Improve surgical skills to reduce incisional bleeding, hematoma, and necrosis.
Prevent infection of the uterine incision.
For those with a history of cesarean section, perform early color Doppler ultrasound examination to determine the location of the gestational sac after the second pregnancy.

<!-- Slide number: 53 -->
## Amniotic Fluid Embolism
Do not cut the amniotic membrane at the same time as the uterine wall is cut.
Make a small incision in the amniotic membrane when there is no uterine contraction, use a suction device to remove the amniotic fluid at the incision, then expand the amniotic membrane incision and deliver the fetal head.
The incision should be large enough to prevent an increase in uterine cavity pressure due to resistance when compressing the fundus.
After the fetus is delivered, remove the amniotic fluid, and then deliver the placenta.
Be highly vigilant for abnormal opening of the uterine blood sinus, such as placenta previa, placental abruption, and uterine rupture.

## Perioperative Management
Preoperative color Doppler ultrasound or MRI examination
Observe the healing of the uterine incision in late pregnancy, measure the thickness of the incision.
Observe the relationship between the placental position and the uterine incision, and whether there is implantation.
Carefully identify anatomical relationships during intraoperative manipulation
Due to adhesions between the abdominal wall, peritoneum, omentum, intestines, and bladder with the uterus, it is difficult to open the abdomen, try to find normal peritoneum, and carefully incise.
If severe adhesions cannot be identified, extend the incision upwards.

### Notes:
Less than 3mm, there is a risk of uterine rupture.

Scar incision management
Use the incision method at both ends of the original scar incision
If it is difficult to separate the adhesions or to avoid varicose veins, the uterus incision may be oblique, and the fetus should be delivered slowly or with forceps to avoid tearing.
Suture technique
Avoid suture being too dense and tight, causing tissue necrosis.
Avoid incomplete hemostasis or leaving dead spaces that lead to hematomas.

Less than 3mm, there is a risk of uterine rupture.
