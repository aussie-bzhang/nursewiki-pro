**Overview**

**Perioperative Period:** The period before, during, and after surgery is referred to as the perioperative period.

*Preoperative Period:* From the patient's decision to undergo surgery to the patient being transported to the operating table.
*Intraoperative Period:* From the patient being placed on the operating table to the patient's surgery being completed and being transferred to the recovery room (observation room) or surgical ward.
*Postoperative Period:* From the patient being transferred to the recovery room or surgical ward to the patient's basic recovery and discharge.

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->

**Classification of Surgery**

According to the timing of surgery, it is divided into three categories:

*Elective Surgery:* The timing of the surgery does not affect the therapeutic effect. For example, repair of recurrent inguinal hernia.
*Time-Limited Surgery:* The time for surgery can be chosen, but the time for preoperative preparation cannot be arbitrarily extended. For example, malignant tumors.
*Emergency Surgery:* Requires surgery to be performed as quickly as possible. For example, various traumas, acute hemorrhage, and acute abdomen.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->

**Classification of Surgery**

According to different surgical purposes, it is divided into four categories:

*Diagnostic Surgery:* Helps the doctor determine or confirm a suspected diagnosis.
*Treatment Surgery:* Removes, repairs, or corrects lesions, injuries, or congenital malformations.
*Palliative Surgery:* Alleviates the symptoms of incurable diseases.
*Aesthetic Surgery:* Improves appearance, such as rhinoplasty, breast augmentation, and double eyelid surgery.

**(III) Special Patient Preparation: (correcting malnutrition, correcting dehydration and acidosis, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, liver and kidney dysfunction, taking iodine before thyroid surgery)**
**(IV) Morning Nursing on the Day of Surgery:**
   ① Four measurements, fever, and menstrual onset change the date; ② Check skin preparation, changing clothes, fasting, and no drinking; ③ Follow the doctor's instructions for enema or gastric tube insertion; ④ Empty the bladder or insert a urinary catheter; ⑤ Remove dentures or jewelry; ⑥ Preoperative medication; ⑦ Send the patient and items to the operating room; ⑧ Prepare the bed unit.

**（Five）Emergency Surgery Preparation:**

1. Strive to save every second, quickly establish an intravenous access, and correct shock.
2. Simply cover the wound.
3. Refrain from eating and drinking, prepare the skin, and conduct drug sensitivity tests.
4. Urgently check blood and urine routine, coagulation time, blood type, and cross-matching test.
5. Administer preoperative medication, instruct the patient to urinate, and transport to the operating room.

**Section Two: Postoperative Nursing**

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->
**[Nursing Assessment]**

**(One) General Condition**
Understand the anesthetic method, surgical method, intraoperative management, intraoperative blood loss, fluid and blood transfusion volume, urine output, and medication, the type and placement of the drainage tube, and its function.

**(Two) Function of Important Organs**
Observe consciousness, pupils, respiratory rate, pulse, temperature, blood pressure, skin temperature and color, peripheral blood circulation, and urination.

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->
**[Nursing Assessment]**

**(Three) Surgical Fever:**
Due to the body's response to surgical trauma, the patient's body temperature may slightly rise, generally not exceeding 38°C, and gradually return to normal within 1-2 days.

**(Four) Anesthetic Recovery:**
Assess the patient's consciousness, respiratory and circulatory function, limb movement and sensation, and skin color, and make a comprehensive judgment on whether the anesthetic has awakened and the degree of awakening.

<!-- Slide number: 22 -->
**[Nursing Assessment]**

**(Five) Incision and Drainage:**
Dressing fixation, secretion, bleeding, infection, whether the drainage is unobstructed, the nature, color, and quantity of the drainage fluid.

**(Six) Emotional Response:**
Caring about the surgical outcome and prognosis, experiencing anxiety and depression, and even negative and pessimistic disappointment.

**[Nursing Measures]**

**(One) Positioning and Transfer**
1. Receiving the patient: Secure the drainage tube, minimize movement, and avoid postural hypotension and drainage tube detachment.
2. Positioning: First, follow the anesthetic position, then follow the surgical position (general anesthesia, lumbar anesthesia, other anesthesia, craniocerebral, neck and chest, abdominal, spinal and gluteal, extremities, shock)
   **Appendix: Advantages of semi-recumbent position:**
   1. Benefits blood circulation and patient breathing, increases lung ventilation volume;
   2. Relaxes the abdominal muscles, reduces the tension of the abdominal incision;
   3. Allows inflammatory exudates to flow to the pelvic cavity, preventing the formation of subdiaphragmatic abscesses.

<!-- Slide number: 25 -->
**[Nursing Measures]**

**(Two) Maintaining Respiratory and Circulatory Function**
1. Monitor vital signs closely: every 15-30 minutes for patients with general anesthesia or major surgery, and change to 1-2 hours after the condition is stable, and make good records. It is best to enter the ICU for monitoring.
2. Maintain a clear airway
   Prevent tongue prolapse, encourage deep breathing, effective coughing to promote sputum and lung expansion, frequently turn over, use nebulization for thick sputum, and provide suction.
3. Oxygen therapy
4. Prevent hypotension
   Infusion; prevent sitting up and standing up to cause postural hypotension

<!-- Slide number: 26 -->
**[Nursing Measures]**

**(Three) Maintaining Digestive Function**
1. Insertion of nasogastric tube:
   Keep the nasogastric tube unobstructed and effective in decompression.
2. Encourage movement:
   Turn over, bed exercises, and early ambulation to promote peristalsis
3. Promote peristalsis:
   If there is still no peristalsis 3-4 days after surgery, follow the doctor's instructions to give glycerin suppositories, rectal gas, or enema.

<!-- Slide number: 27 -->
**[Nursing Measures]**

**(Four) Supplementation of Nutrition and Water-Electrolyte Imbalance**
1. Fasting and eating:
   1. Non-abdominal surgery, local anesthesia, and minor surgery can eat immediately after surgery.
   2. 6 hours after spinal anesthesia, food can be taken according to the patient's needs.
   3. For patients under general anesthesia, they should wait until the anesthesia is清醒, without nausea and vomiting, and then give a liquid diet first, and then change to semi-liquid or regular food.

<!-- Slide number: 28 -->
**[Nursing Measures]**

**(Four) Supplementation of Nutrition and Water-Electrolyte Imbalance**
   4. Abdominal surgery and gastrointestinal surgery: fasting for 24-48 hours after surgery, start with a small amount of liquid diet 3-4 days after the recovery of intestinal function and anal exhaust, gradually increase to full liquid diet, and start with semi-liquid diet on the 5th to 6th day. Generally, soft food can be eaten from the 7th to 9th day, and regular food can be started from the 10th to 12th day.
   5. Encourage the intake of high-protein, high-calorie, and high-vitamin diets for those who can eat.

<!-- Slide number: 29 -->
**[Nursing Measures]**

**(Four) Supplementation of Nutrition and Water-Electrolyte Imbalance**
   2. Fluid replacement:
      For those who cannot eat, body fluids and nutrients should be supplemented intravenously.

   3. Record fluid intake and output: record 24-hour fluid intake and output and monitor electrolytes to assess the status of water, electrolytes, and nutritional metabolism. If there are abnormalities, supplementation is provided.

**[Nursing Measures]**

## (V) Enhancing Patient Comfort
1. **Pain Management:**
   After anesthesia wears off, patients may experience incision pain, which is most severe within 24 hours and gradually subsides 2-3 days later.
   **Management:** Identify the cause (compression, urinary retention, infection), provide appropriate treatment, explanation, and symptomatic care.

2. **Nausea and Vomiting Management:**
   Commonly caused by anesthesia, water and electrolyte metabolic disorders, diabetic acidosis, uremia, increased intracranial pressure, acute gastric dilation, or intestinal obstruction.
   **Management:** Address the underlying cause, prevent aspiration, and provide antiemetics.

<!-- Slide number: 31 -->
## Nursing Measures

## (V) Enhancing Patient Comfort
3. **Abdominal Distension Management:**
   Often due to anesthesia and abdominal surgery-induced gastrointestinal dysfunction.
   **Management:**
     1. Encourage early ambulation to promote gastrointestinal function recovery;
     2. Consider fasting, continuous gastrointestinal decompression, and anal tube evacuation;
     3. For non-gastrointestinal surgery, intramuscular injection of neostigmine may be used;
     4. For those with hypokalemia or peritonitis, appropriate treatment should be provided.

<!-- Slide number: 32 -->
## Nursing Measures

## (V) Enhancing Patient Comfort
4. **Urinary Retention Management:**
   Often due to anesthesia, not being accustomed to urinating in bed, and incision pain.
   **Management:**
     1. Reassure and encourage the patient, alleviate anxiety and tension;
     2. Change position; induce urination;
     3. Apply heat to the lower abdomen, massage, and intramuscular injection of carbachol;
     4. If ineffective, perform catheterization.

## (VI) Incision Care
1. **Protecting the Wound:**
   1. Pay attention to aseptic operation and timely replacement of wound dressings;
   2. Prevent the patient from pulling off the dressing;
   3. Use antibiotics as prescribed;
   4. When there are signs of infection, local hot compresses and physical therapy should be taken to promote inflammation absorption.

2. **Classification of Surgical Incisions:**
   (Clean I, Contaminated II, Polluted III)

3. **Classification of Incision Healing:**
   (Class A, Class B, Class C healing)

## Nursing Measures

## (VI) Incision Care
4. **Incision Suturing Removal:**
   - Head, face, and neck: 4-5 days
   - Lower abdomen, perineum: 6-7 days
   - Chest, upper abdomen, back, buttocks incisions: 7-9 days
   - Limbs: 10-12 days
   - Tension-reducing sutures: not less than 14 days
   - For the elderly, weak, undernourished, or diabetic patients, the suture removal should be appropriately delayed
   - Intermittent suture removal method can be used; after abdominal suture removal, continue to bandage for 1-2 days
   - If the incision becomes infected, the suture removal should be done earlier.

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
## Nursing Measures

## (VII) Drainage Tube Care
1. **Familiarize with the function and route of the drainage tube, do not connect it incorrectly;**
2. Securely fix it to prevent it from falling off or slipping into the body cavity;
3. Observe and record the color, consistency, and amount of the drainage fluid;
4. Keep the drainage unobstructed, avoid compression or twisting, and use negative pressure aspiration if necessary;
5. Keep the drainage device sterile to prevent contamination, change the dressing at the skin outlet of the drainage tube according to sterile technique, and change the drainage bag daily;
6. Master the indications, time, and methods for removing various types of drainage tubes.

<!-- Slide number: 36 -->
## Nursing Measures

## (VIII) Guidance for Early Activity
1. **Advantages:**
   Increase lung capacity, reduce pulmonary complications, improve overall blood circulation, promote gastrointestinal function recovery and incision healing, prevent pressure sores, and reduce the formation of lower limb venous thrombosis, etc.

2. **Principles:**
   Early activity, strive to get up and move around in a short period of time.

3. **Caution:**
   However, patients with shock, heart failure, severe infection, bleeding, extreme weakness, or those who have undergone joint surgery of the limbs and abdominal hernia repair should not emphasize early activity.

## (IX) Postoperative Complications and Nursing
1. **Postoperative Hemorrhage**
   **Causes:**
   1. Incomplete hemostasis during surgery, uncontrolled oozing from the wound;
   2. Suture line loosening after surgery;
   3. The original spastic small artery end relaxation;
   4. Coagulation mechanism disorders, etc.

   **Manifestations:** Extravasation hemorrhage and intravascular hemorrhage

   **Management:** Prevention, sedation, pressure bandaging, hemostatic agents, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion, and reoperation exploration.

<!-- Slide number: 38 -->
## Nursing Measures

## (IX) Postoperative Complications and Nursing
2. **Incision Infection**
   **Causes:**
       1. Inadequate aseptic operation during surgery; 2. Incomplete hemostasis during surgery, incorrect suture technique, residual blood clots, dead spaces, or foreign bodies in the incision; 3. Poor nutrition or complications such as diabetes, obesity, leading to poor incision healing and increasing the risk of incision infection.

   **Manifestations:** Persistent fever and wound pain 3-4 days after surgery.

   **Management:** Strict aseptic operation, avoid excessive tissue damage, thorough hemostasis, avoid dead spaces in suturing, use antibiotics, enhance resistance, suture removal and drainage, and local dressing changes.

<!-- Slide number: 39 -->
## Nursing Measures

## (IX) Postoperative Complications and Nursing
3. **Incision Rupture**
   **Causes:** 1. Malnutrition; 2. Poor incision suture; 3. Incision infection; 4. Sudden increase in intra-abdominal pressure.

   **Manifestations:** Commonly seen in abdominal incisions, sudden pain and relaxation of the incision when the patient strains, or hearing the sound of suture rupture. A large amount of pale red fluid may be discharged from the incision, and in cases of full-thickness rupture, the bowel or omentum may be exposed.

   **Management:** For mild cases, butterfly gauze can be applied; for visceral prolapse, it should be returned under sterile conditions in the operating room (prohibition of on-site reinsertion) and tension-reducing suture should be performed, along with antibiotics and enhanced nutrition.

## Nursing Measures

## (IX) Postoperative Complications and Nursing
4. **Pulmonary Atresia**
   **Causes:** Increased respiratory secretions blocking the bronchus, postoperative pain, tight bandaging of the chest and abdomen.

   **Manifestations:** Postoperative fever, increased respiratory rate, dull or solid percussion sounds, localized moist rales on auscultation, weakened or absent breath sounds, PaO2↓, PaCO2↑. In cases of secondary infection, T significantly↑, WBC and N↑.

   **Management:** In addition to preventing aspiration and tight bandaging, sputum aspiration, and if necessary, tracheotomy, encourage the patient to take deep breaths and effective coughs at regular intervals, and use antibiotics.

## Postoperative Complications and Nursing

5. Urinary Tract Infection
* **Causes:**
  - Usually due to postoperative indwelling catheter or prolonged bed rest.
* **Manifestations:**
  - Chills and fever, renal area pain, frequent urination, urgency, and hematuria and pus cells in urine examination.
* **Management:**
  - Promptly manage urinary retention, maintain urine通畅, increase fluid intake, pay attention to aseptic operation when inserting a catheter, and use antibiotics.

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->
## Nursing Measures

6. Thrombophlebitis
* **Causes:**
  - Prolonged bed rest, hypercoagulability, repeated vascular puncture and catheter placement, or infusion of drugs with strong irritative properties causing vascular endothelial injury.
* **Manifestations:**
  - Pain and tightness in the calf gastrocnemius muscle, followed by edema of the lower limb; the superficial veins of the lower limb become red and hard with marked tenderness. Often accompanied by fever.
* **Management:**
  - Stop intravenous infusion in the affected limb, elevate the limb and immobilize it, apply a 50% magnesium sulfate compress, strictly prohibit massage (to prevent thrombus detachment leading to pulmonary embolism), and intravenous infusion of low-molecular-weight dextran, compound Danshen solution, defibrinase, etc.

## Evaluation

(1) Whether the patient's postoperative vital signs are stable.
(2) Whether the fluid balance is maintained and there is no electrolyte disturbance.
(3) Whether the patient has no postoperative discomfort.
(4) Whether the patient's nutritional status has improved.
(5) Whether the patient's activity tolerance has increased.
(6) Whether the patient can recite relevant postoperative health knowledge and actively cooperate with treatment and nursing.
(7) Whether the patient's mood is stable and actively cooperates with treatment and nursing.
(8) Whether complications have occurred or have been detected and managed in a timely manner.

## Health Guidance

1. Guide the patient on what to pay attention to in diet and activity after discharge.
2. Guide the patient to master the methods of rehabilitation exercise.
3. Inform the patient of the follow-up appointment and what situations require immediate return to the hospital for examination.

## Summary

The success of surgery depends on whether the preoperative preparation is sufficient. Therefore, medical staff should attach great importance to preoperative preparation, which includes the preparation of medical staff, patients (psychological and physiological), and surgical equipment. Postoperative observation and management are important measures for preventing and treating various postoperative complications, consolidating the efficacy of surgery, and promoting the patient's early recovery. Medical staff should be proficient in postoperative condition observation, promptly detect and correctly manage postoperative discomfort, and prevent complications.
