**I. Surgical Site Infection (SSI)**

SSI ≠ Incisional Site Infection

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->

Surgical Site Infection

Surgical site infection is simply defined as the presence of purulent secretion, abscess, or cellulitis within one month postoperatively. It is categorized into three levels: superficial incisional site infection, deep incisional site infection, and organ (or space) infection.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->

Surgical Site Infections (SSIs) are a major postoperative infection.

The patient's (host) response is an important variable among the factors influencing the infection rate.

Consequences of SSI -- can cause pain, suffering, and deformity in patients, increase medical costs significantly, and even lead to patient death.

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->

According to CDC statistics, the reasons for increased medical costs include:

Extended hospital stay
Re-admission
Reoperation

Antibiotic use
Intensive care

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->

Predisposing Conditions for SSI

Research has shown that:
• Wound contamination is the predisposing condition for infection.
• The risk of SSI increases significantly when the contamination of surgical wounds exceeds 105 organisms per gram of tissue.
• The presence of foreign bodies can cause SSI even with a small number of microorganisms.

Superficial Incision Infection

Infection occurring only in the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the incision 30 days after surgery, and meets one of the following conditions:

1. Purulent fluid in the superficial tissue of the incision
2. Pathogens isolated from the fluid or tissue of the superficial tissue of the incision
3. Symptoms or signs of infection, including local erythema, swelling, fever, pain, and tenderness, with the superficial layer of the incision opened by the surgeon

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->

The following situations do not constitute superficial incisional tissue infection:

1. Pustules at needle holes (limited to minor inflammation and slight secretion at the site of suture passage)
2. Infection at the site of perineal incision or circumcision, or perianal surgical site
3. Infected burn wounds, and escharated II and III degree burn wounds

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->

Deep Incision Infection

Infection occurring within 30 days (1 year after implantation surgery) after surgery, involving deep soft tissue such as fascia and muscle, and meets one of the following conditions:

1. Purulent fluid drained or aspirated from the deep tissue of the incision, but not from the organ cavity part
2. The deep tissue of the incision spontaneously opens or is opened by the surgeon. The patient also has symptoms or signs of infection, including local fever, swelling, and pain
3. Evidence of abscess or other infection in the deep tissue of the incision is found through direct examination, reoperation exploration, pathology, or imaging

<!-- Slide number: 20 -->

<!-- Slide number: 21 -->

Organ/Cavity Infection

Infection occurring within 30 days after surgery in the absence of implants, or within 1 year after surgery with implants, involving the anatomical site (such as organ or cavity) during surgery, and meets one of the following conditions:

1. Purulent fluid drained from the organ or cavity
2. Pathogens isolated from the secretion or tissue
3. Evidence of abscess or other infection in the organ or cavity is found through examination, surgical exploration, pathology, or imaging

All surgical wounds are contaminated by bacteria, but only a small number of wounds actually become infected. Different surgical procedures have different numbers of potential contaminated wounds and bacteria; an increase in the number of bacteria in the wound will increase the infection rate. For different patient populations, the same surgical procedure performed by the same surgeon with the same degree of contamination may result in different infection rates.

Factors Affecting the Wound Microenvironment

<!-- Slide number: 34 -->

• Hemoglobin from red blood cells and blood clots within the wound (i.e., clots) is a rich source of iron – promoting rapid bacterial proliferation

• Loss of viable tissue at the surgical wound site and necrotic tissue caused by electrocautery – providing a sanctuary for bacterial proliferation

• Foreign bodies such as silk sutures increase the likelihood of infection caused by certain microorganisms

• Accumulation of plasma and body fluids in dead spaces within the wound, with bacterial contaminants in these accumulated fluids increasing the possibility of infection

I. SSI Prevention Strategies – Preoperative Measures

	 Patient Preparation
1. Skin Preparation
--Do not shave the hair at the surgical site unless it affects the operation
--Do not use a razor to shave hair (Level II evidence)
--If shaving is necessary, it must be performed outside the operating room
2. Blood Glucose Control
--For cardiac surgery patients (Level I evidence)
--For non-cardiac surgery patients (Level II evidence)
Postoperative blood glucose levels should be controlled immediately; levels should be ≤180 mg/dL

<!-- Slide number: 41 -->

3. Respiratory Preparation
--Encourage patients to quit smoking
Nicotine can delay primary wound healing and may increase the risk of SSI.
(A large prospective study showed that smoking is an independent risk factor for sternal and/or mediastinal SSI following cardiac surgery.)

(Nagacinta T, J Infect Dis 1987)

<!-- Slide number: 42 -->

	 Patient Prophylactic Antibiotic Use (Level I evidence)
--Administer the drug within one hour before incision
--Starting the drug within one hour before incision is also effective
Studies have shown that administration within 0-30 minutes before incision is better than 30-60 minutes before incision
--Vancomycin, fluoroquinolones: administer the drug two hours before incision
--For surgeries with long duration or excessive bleeding, additional doses should be administered at intervals of two and a half half-life periods

--For colorectal surgery: oral combined parenteral antibiotic administration

	 Skin Disinfection
--No contraindications; use alcohol-based disinfectants for skin disinfection before surgery (Level I evidence)
Studies have shown that chlorhexidine-alcohol significantly reduces the incidence of SSI compared to povidone-iodine (9.5% vs 16% [p=0.004] RR=0.59 [95% CI=0.41-0.85])

II. SSI Prevention Strategies – Intraoperative Measures

	 Wearing operating room attire, pants, shoes, and hats; wearing masks
	 Tie the bottom of the shirt inside the washing pants, do not expose the hair, and cover the mouth and nose with a mask
	 Change clothes and shoes when going out

	 Change into clean patient gowns and wear isolation caps for surgical patients

## Environmental Management

1. Room temperature: 20 ~ 25°C, relative humidity: 40 ~ 60%, noise level: 40 ~ 50dB
Average illumination: approximately 500LX

2. Management of surgical personnel entering the room
Computer fingerprint verification, chest card, visitor's badge, and visitors

3. Differentiate between clean and contaminated workflows

4. Differentiate between sterile, emergency, and infection operating rooms

5. Management of operating room doors

## Operating Room Door Management

1. Close doors and windows during surgery
2. Minimize the number of times doors are opened and closed
3. Limit the number of visitors as much as possible
4. Reduce personnel movement
5. When observing surgery, do not stand too high; visitors should be at least 30cm away from surgical personnel, and they should not frequently move around the room

## Cleaning Management

1. Before and after each surgery, and at the end of each day's surgery: Wet cleaning during the operation of the purification air conditioning system
2. After cleaning, continue operation to achieve self-cleaning time
3. Cleaning tools requirements: Non-fibrous fabric; different colors for distinction; disinfect and dry for standby use after use
4. Waste collection, classification, and standardized disposal should be carried out in a timely manner to minimize ground contamination

## Cleaning Management (Continued)

6. After each surgery: The surface of the operating table and within a range of at least 1~1.5m
7. At the end of the day's surgery: Clean and disinfect the exposed ground and surfaces of the operating room
8. Weekly: Carry out a thorough cleaning of the operating department once a week, including wiping and cleaning all surfaces, including ceilings, walls, and floors

## Sterile Management

1. Movement and Positioning
• When changing positions with another surgical personnel, step back first, turn around, and face each other's backs to turn to the other position. When passing in front of the person wearing the surgical gown, they should step aside to avoid collision and contamination.
• Hands should not hang down, cross arms, or be held under the armpits or above the shoulders while standing. They should be placed in front of the chest.
• While waiting for surgery, one can stand on the side of the operating table, avoiding other busy personnel.

## Sterile Management (Continued)

2. During Surgery
• Surgical personnel should stand close to the operating table, facing the table directly, and should not stand completely to the side or turn their backs to the table.
• The area below the operating table surface is considered contaminated. Surgical personnel's hands and instruments should not be placed below this level, otherwise, they are considered contaminated.
3. Instrument Transfer
• Pass instruments at chest height, not too low or too high, not from behind or over the head.
• If necessary, instruments can be passed under the surgeon's arm, but not below the edge of the operating table.
• The surgeon should not randomly extend their arm across the surgical area to retrieve instruments.
• When personnel below the table pass instruments to those on the table, they must use sterile forceps.

## Sterile Management (Continued)

5. Management of Instruments and Items During Surgery
• Unused instruments and items should be placed on the instrument table and covered with sterile towels.
• Sharp instruments and needles should be placed with the tip upwards to prevent piercing sterile dressings and becoming contaminated.
• Items to be inserted into the body cavity should be held with special instruments or sterile dressings, and should not be touched directly with hands.

• Isolation technique for contaminated surgery: Before cutting into hollow organs, use saline pads to protect surrounding tissues; and promptly aspirate any exuded contents. Contaminated instruments and dressings should be placed in the contaminated tray and not reused.

## Sterile Management (Continued)

6. Once sterile items are removed, even if they have not been used, they cannot be returned to the sterile container. They must be re-sterilized before reuse.
• Sterile packages that have not been contaminated for more than 24 hours after opening cannot be used.
• Disposable sterile items should be opened by the circulating nurse and picked up with forceps by the instrument nurse, and should not be torn open directly on the sterile table.
• When using sterile drapes to cover the sterile area, the inside of the drapes is sterile, while the outside and edges are considered contaminated.
• When temporarily opening sterile packages to retrieve items, use sterile forceps to hold or fold the four corners of the package and hold them with the hands, with the instrument nurse retrieving the sterile items.

## Sterile Management (Continued)

4. If surgical items are contaminated, they must be re-disinfected or replaced immediately.
• Sterile dressings within the unrestricted area
• Sterile packages that are damaged or damp
• Sterile packages that fall to the ground
• Uncertainty about the validity and effectiveness of the sterilization time and effect

• If there is a suspicion that sterile items have been contaminated, the establishment of the sterile area should be as close to the time of use as possible to minimize the opportunity for exposure and contamination.

## Sterile Management (Continued)

7. Maintain a dry and sterile sterile area
• Once the sterile towel is wet, it should be replaced immediately or layered.
• Sterile solutions in soft packaging should be used up in one go and not retained.
• Bottled solutions must be retained, and the time should be noted.
• Gloves with a puncture should be replaced immediately.
• Hands that have not been disinfected are prohibited from crossing the sterile area.

## Sterile Management (Continued)

8. Replace masks that become damp in a timely manner
• When surgical personnel cough or sneeze, they should turn their heads away from the sterile area.
• Wipe the sweat of the surgical personnel in a timely manner to avoid dripping onto the operating table.

## Temperature Management

• Maintain body temperature at normal temperature (≧35.5°C) during the perioperative period (Level I evidence)

Intraoperative Hypothermia
• Directly affects the function of neutrophils
• Stimulates subcutaneous vascular constriction and secondary tissue hypoxia, indirectly damaging the function of neutrophils
• Increases blood loss, leading to wound hematoma or blood transfusion

## Patient Warming

30 minutes of preheating indicates that it can reduce infection (Infection Control Today 4/2005)
--Comfort is the most important feeling for patients
--Ensure that all patients are warm at any time
--Invest in and put into use a warming machine in each operating room
--Control of operating room temperature
--Minimize exposed areas
--Use hot lavage solutions
