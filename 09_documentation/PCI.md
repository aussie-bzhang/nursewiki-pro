# Definition
Coronary angiography (CAG), abbreviated as coronary angiography, is a method using a specially designed catheter inserted into the femoral, brachial, or radial artery to reach the root of the aorta, and then inserted into the left and right coronary artery ostia respectively, to inject contrast medium to make the coronary arteries and their main branches visible.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
# Definition
Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) refers to the technique of using percutaneous puncture technology to deliver balloon catheters or other related instruments to relieve coronary artery stenosis or obstruction and reconstruct coronary artery blood flow.

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
# Definition
Percutaneous coronary angioplasty (PTCA): By puncturing the femoral artery or radial artery, catheters, wires, and balloons are sent along the artery to the corresponding stenotic site of the coronary artery, expanded for a few seconds, and the coronary artery stenosis is eliminated.
A coronary stent is a metal mesh support structure attached to a balloon catheter and placed in the human body. After being expanded at the lesion site, it remains permanently in the coronary artery, which can support the vascular wall and prevent it from being blocked again.

# Methods
The performance of cardiac catheterization is carried out under local anesthesia, and the patient remains conscious throughout the process.
The catheter can enter the body through the femoral artery (inguinal) or radial artery (wrist).

# Skin Preparation (Preparation of skin)
Range: A radius of 15-20 cm around the puncture point
      Femoral artery - has the advantages of easy puncture and operation due to its larger diameter and less tendency to spasm.
      Radial artery - the radial artery approach has the advantages of less injury, faster recovery, less pain, and shorter hospital stay.
PS: Pay attention to gentle movements during skin preparation to prevent skin breakage and bleeding, which may increase the risk of infection.

# Preoperative Medication
Aspirin and Plavix each 300mg (loading dose) / or continuous oral administration for more than 6 days
0.9% NS preoperative hydration (establishing venous access, puncture is preferred on the left upper limb, using a catheter needle, for emergency rescue during surgery)
Iodine allergy test: 2 milliliters of the original contrast medium is injected intravenously, and the results are observed 20 minutes later. Before the skin test, it is necessary to closely observe the patient's original symptoms, signs, and severity to facilitate differentiation and accurate judgment
Potassium supplementation as necessary

# Preoperative Training
Defecation training: Perform bed urination training 1-2 days before surgery to prevent urinary difficulties due to unfamiliarity with bed urination during or after surgery.
Practice actions required during surgery: mainly breathing and coughing exercises

# Puncture Point Preparation (Allen Test)
Use the thumbs of both hands to compress the patient's radial and ulnar arteries, have the patient clench their fists and then relax, repeat 3 times, and the patient's palm turns white. Release the ulnar side compression, and the palm recovers color within 10 seconds, indicating a positive Allen test), and the local condition of the puncture site is good.

# Psychological Nursing

Briefly introduce the surgical method and guide the patient on relevant matters during and after surgery, explain the method, purpose, and time of the surgery, emphasize that the patient receives surgery while awake, eliminate the patient's tension, and avoid adverse stimulation

# Other Preparations

Trim nails, clean skin

# Intraoperative Nursing
Closely monitor vital signs, closely observe changes in the patient's condition
Instruct the patient to immediately notify the doctor if they experience palpitations, chest tightness, or other discomfort during the procedure. During balloon dilation, the patient may experience symptoms such as chest tightness and angina, and appropriate comfort and explanation should be provided, and corresponding treatment should be given
Focus on monitoring changes in electrocardiogram and blood pressure during catheter positioning, angiography, balloon dilation, and possible reperfusion arrhythmias, report abnormalities to the doctor in a timely manner, and take effective measures

# Vital Signs Monitoring

Continuous monitoring of ECG, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation for 24 hours to detect changes in heart rate, rhythm, and the occurrence of arrhythmias promptly. Perform a 12-lead ECG immediately, compare with preoperative results, and repeat if symptoms are present.

If hypotension occurs, consider the following:
Vasovagal reflex
Posterior abdominal hemorrhage—lower abdominal pain
Intraoperative bleeding at puncture site—hematoma, absent pulse
Coronary artery rupture or perforation—pericardial tamponade

# Caring for Puncture Sites—Radial Artery

Radial artery puncture: Remove the sheath immediately after surgery.
Haemostasis: Radial artery hemostat
Postoperative 2-hour deflation: 2ml, 2ml, 3ml, 3ml
Observe for bleeding or hematoma.

# Caring for Puncture Sites—Femoral Artery

Femoral artery puncture: Measure activated clotting time (ACT) before removal of the catheter. The catheter can be removed when ACT <150s.
After removal, apply pressure to the puncture site for 30 minutes, then apply compression bandaging, and apply a 1~1.5 kg sandbag for 6~8 hours.

Regularly observe the wound for bleeding and the dorsalis pedis pulse.

# Diet and Lifestyle Care

Drink 1500-2000ml of water in divided doses (to promote the excretion of contrast medium)
If there is no nausea or vomiting after surgery, patients can start eating, and it is advisable to eat light, easily digestible foods without overeating.
Instruct patients to change their previous poor lifestyle and dietary habits, adhere to medication, and prevent restenosis of the coronary artery and stent. Carefully explain the purpose of drug treatment to the patient and family, explain its importance and potential risks, and teach patients to understand the symptoms and signs of bleeding. If bleeding or chest pain, chest tightness, and other conditions occur, they should seek medical attention promptly.

# Activity Guidance

Postoperative immobilization of the patient's "exercise" is beneficial for the recovery of the condition and to reduce the occurrence of complications. The non-surgical limb can be freely moved when the surgical limb is immobilized. After the sandbag pressure is removed from the surgical limb, movement can be performed to prevent thrombosis. The principle of movement is to keep the surgical limb straight. Use ankle movement.
Post-immobilization exercise: Gradually increase the amount of activity, move slowly when getting up and squatting, and do not exert force suddenly. Avoid lifting heavy objects within one week after surgery to prevent bleeding at the puncture site, and resume daily life and light physical activities one week later.

# Observation and Nursing of Postoperative Complications

Hematoma: The most common complication at the artery puncture site, which may be due to leakage of blood from around the vascular sheath or improper pressure on the artery after removal, leading to external leakage of blood. Some hematomas are related to the patient moving the lower limb too early or the sandbag shifting. Closely observe the wound for bleeding, and re-bandage if bleeding occurs. Local thrombi formed by hematomas disappear completely in about two weeks.
Vasovagal reflex:
    Causes: Stimulation of the vessel by catheter removal; pressure technique after catheter removal; patient's constitution and mental state.
    Characteristics: Sudden drop in blood pressure, rapid decrease in heart rate.
    Time: Most occur within 10 minutes after catheter removal, and some occur during femoral artery puncture and catheter removal.
    Management: Instruct the patient not to become overly anxious or worried, administer intravenous fluid therapy quickly, and, if necessary, administer atropine intravenously as prescribed.

# Observation and Nursing of Postoperative Complications

Lower limb venous thrombosis: Pay attention to the dorsalis pedis pulse, skin color, temperature, and sensation changes of both lower limbs after getting out of bed. Observe for pain or limping in the limbs after ambulation, and notify the doctor immediately if any abnormalities are found. It usually occurs within 24-48 hours after surgery. Therefore, pay attention to limb movement within 24 hours after the patient's surgery.
Urinary retention: Caused by the patient's unfamiliarity with bed defecation, preoperative training of bed defecation should be provided, and psychological counseling should be given. If necessary, catheterization is performed.

# Postoperative Health Guidance

Follow the doctor's instructions to take antiplatelet aggregation drugs, do not reduce or stop the medication arbitrarily, to prevent complications such as thrombosis and embolism leading to vascular occlusion and acute myocardial infarction. Regularly monitor changes in platelet count and coagulation time.
Quit smoking and limit alcohol consumption, eat a light diet, eat more vegetables and fruits, and eat less high cholesterol and high-fat foods.
Live a regular life, avoid overwork, and maintain good emotions.
Instruct the patient to continue taking medication as prescribed after discharge to consolidate the efficacy of coronary intervention therapy. Regularly follow up in the outpatient department.
