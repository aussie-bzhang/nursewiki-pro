Relationship between Intervertebral Disc and Nerve Root
       The L3 and L4 nerve roots exit the corresponding vertebral body at the upper 1/3 or middle 1/3 level, closely adhering to the pedicle and entering the intervertebral foramen. During their course within the spinal canal, they do not come into contact with the intervertebral disc of the same level.
       The L5 nerve root exits the dura mater at the level of the L4/5 intervertebral disc or above its superior margin, extending downward and outward, crossing the posterior superior aspect of the L5 vertebral body and wrapping around the pedicle to enter the L5/S1 intervertebral foramen.
       The S1 nerve root originates from the superior margin of the L5/S1 intervertebral disc or at the lower 1/3 level of the L5 vertebral body, extending downward and outward, crossing the outer 1/3 of the L5/S1 intervertebral disc, and wrapping around the sacral arch root to enter the vertebral foramen.

Anatomy and Pathological Characteristics of Lumbar Disc Herniation
The above anatomical structures determine that lumbar disc herniation at the L4/5 and L5/S1 levels has the highest incidence, and the protrusion is mostly located at the posterior part of the intervertebral disc, lateral to the posterior longitudinal ligament. The main part of the protruding material compresses the nerve root at this location or the next segment of the nerve root about to exit the dura mater. If the protruding material is large or protrudes inward, it can also compress the next nerve root within the dura mater, causing simultaneous compression of two nerve roots.

Anatomy and Pathological Characteristics of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Generally, L3/4 intervertebral disc herniation compresses the L4 nerve root, L4/5 intervertebral disc herniation compresses the L5 nerve root, and L5/S1 intervertebral disc herniation compresses the S1 nerve root. However, if the lumbar disc herniation is located in the posterior central part or the annulus fibrosus is completely ruptured (i.e., central herniation), the nucleus pulposus fragments may fall into the spinal canal (i.e., rupture or free herniation), causing widespread compression of the nerve root and cauda equina.

Etiology of Lumbar Disc Herniation
It is generally believed that lumbar disc herniation occurs on the basis of intervertebral disc degeneration, while trauma is often an important cause of the disease. In daily life, the lumbar intervertebral disc repeatedly bears compressive, flexion, and torsional loads, which are prone to produce fissures from the inside to the outside of the annulus fibrosus at the location where the intervertebral disc bears the greatest stress, i.e., the posterior part of the annulus fibrosus. This change accumulates and gradually worsens, with the fissure continuously widening, causing the annulus fibrosus at this location to gradually become weak. On this basis, due to a relatively severe trauma, or repeated mild trauma, or even some daily activities that increase the pressure on the intervertebral disc, can promote the further rupture of the degenerated and accumulated injury of the annulus fibrosus. The degenerated nucleus pulposus tissue protrudes from the weak or ruptured part of the annulus fibrosus. The injury itself can cause lumbar pain, while the protruding material compressing the nerve root or cauda equina can cause radicular pain. Therefore, symptoms and signs of lumbar pain and radicular pain in the lower extremities, as well as nerve function impairment, occur.

#

Pathological Changes of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Early stage of protrusion: The nucleus pulposus becomes fragmented due to degeneration and injury, or presents as scar-like connective tissue; the degenerated annulus fibrosus becomes thin and soft or produces fissures due to repeated injury.
Intervertebral disc protrusion: When the intervertebral disc pressure increases due to trauma or normal activities, the nucleus pulposus protrudes from the weak or ruptured part of the annulus fibrosus.
       The pathological morphology of nucleus pulposus protrusion can be divided into three types:
        Elevation type; Rupture type; Free type
Late stage of protrusion: In cases with a long course, various secondary pathological changes occur in the intervertebral disc itself and adjacent structures: fibrosis or calcification of the protruding intervertebral disc material; overall degeneration of the intervertebral disc; damage to the nerve root and cauda equina; thickening of the ligamentum flavum; degeneration and hyperplasia of the intervertebral joint; secondary spinal canal stenosis.

Classification of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Classification according to the direction and location of protrusion
1. Lateral type protrusion:
1) Root shoulder type: The nucleus pulposus protrudes in the anterior external part of the nerve root. Clinical manifestations include radicular radiation pain, the spine bends to the healthy side, and the protrusion is towards the affected side.
2) Root axillary type: The nucleus pulposus protrudes in the anterior internal part of the nerve root. Clinical manifestations include radicular radiation pain, the spine bends to the affected side, and the protrusion is towards the healthy side.
3) Root anterior type: The nucleus pulposus protrudes in the anterior part of the nerve root. Clinical manifestations include severe radicular radiation pain, disappearance of the physiological lumbar lordosis, limited anterior and posterior movements, and no deformity.
4) Extreme lateral type: The nucleus pulposus protrudes at the intervertebral foramen and its lateral part. Clinical manifestations include leg pain greater than back pain, little relation to abdominal pressure, marked intermittent claudication, no cauda equina involvement, and dysfunction of the motor and sensory innervation of the upper segmental nerve root or spinal nerve.

# Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Most cases of lumbar disc herniation can be correctly diagnosed based on the characteristics of the medical history, symptoms and signs, and imaging findings, but attention should be paid to clarify the following issues:
   1) Determine whether the lumbago and leg pain are caused by lumbar disc herniation, and pay attention to differential diagnosis.
   2) Determine the level of lumbar disc herniation and the exact location.
   3) Determine the type of lumbar disc herniation.
   4) Pay attention to the presence of any associated diseases.

# Physical Examination in the Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Straight Leg Raising Test: Compared with the healthy side, if the straight leg raising is limited and radiating pain below the calf appears, it is positive, with a positive rate of about 87%. Lumbar disc herniation at L3/4, L4/5, and L5/S1 can cause a positive straight leg raising test, while lumbar disc herniation at L1/2 and L2/3 can also cause a positive straight leg raising test. However, a negative straight leg raising test does not rule out the presence of lumbar disc herniation and lumbar spinal stenosis.
Straight Leg Raising Strengthening Test: At the same height as the above straight leg raising test, the ankle joint is actively extended to further stretch the involved nerve root, and if the radicular radiation pain is more severe, it is positive.
Healthy Leg Raising Test: If the healthy lower limb is raised and it causes radiating pain in the affected limb, it is positive. When the intervertebral disc herniation mass is located in the anterior-inferior aspect of the nerve root (axial type), it can further compress the involved nerve root and cause radiating pain in the affected limb. If the intervertebral disc herniation mass is located in the anterior-superior aspect of the nerve root (shoulder type), this test is negative.

# Physical Examination in the Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Lying Supine and Arching Test: The mechanism is that the intracranial cerebrospinal fluid and intraspinal pressure increase, further compressing the nerve roots, thus causing radiating lower limb pain.
Femoral Nerve Stretch Test: The patient lies prone, with the hip and knee joints fully extended, and the lower limb is raised to hyperextend the hip joint. If there is radiating pain in the anterior aspect of the affected limb, it is positive. This test is positive for lumbar disc herniation at L2/3 and L3/4, but negative for lumbar disc herniation at L4/5 and L5/S1.

# Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation - Special Examinations
X-ray examination: Generally, routine anteroposterior and lateral lumbar spine X-rays are required. For those suspected of having spondylolysis, additional left and right oblique views are needed.
   Some patients may show no abnormal changes, while others may exhibit some non-specific changes.
   1) Changes in the lumbar spine shape: In the anteroposterior view, there may be lateral curvature deformity, and in the lateral view, the lumbar physiological lordosis may be reduced or disappear, with severe cases even showing kyphosis.
   2) Changes in the width of the intervertebral spaces: In the anteroposterior view, there may be uneven width of the intervertebral spaces, and in the lateral view, the intervertebral spaces may appear narrow anteriorly and wide posteriorly, or uniform in width from front to back. Sometimes, there may be slight displacement of the posterior margins of the upper and lower vertebral bodies.
   3) Osteophytes at the anterior and posterior superior and inferior margins of the vertebral bodies, presenting as lip-like changes.

# Differential Diagnosis of Symptoms of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Differential diagnosis of radicular pain, dry pain, and plexus pain
1. Radicular pain: Caused by lesions within the spinal canal and nerve root canals, commonly seen in diseases such as lumbar disc herniation, intraspinal space-occupying lesions, and arachnoid adhesions.
2. Dry pain: The clinical manifestations vary depending on the affected nerve trunk. On physical examination, there may be tenderness at the exit of the nerve trunk and along the course of the nerve trunk. Lower limb movement often exacerbates pain, with a wider range of involvement compared to radicular pain.
3. Plexus pain: Caused by stimulation or compression of the sacral plexus within the pelvis due to factors such as pelvic inflammation or intrapelvic space-occupying lesions. A series of neurological symptoms appear due to stimulation or compression of the nerve trunks emanating from the sacral plexus. The symptoms are complex, with multiple trunks involved simultaneously, a wide range of involvement, and a sensation of "comfort" upon tapping over the lumbosacral region. It is more common in female patients and requires exclusion of gynecological and obstetric diseases.

# Differential Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
I. Acute lumbar sprain
       Most patients have a history of acute lumbar sprain, which can cause various symptoms and functional disorders, as well as sudden onset of acute pain. They often adopt a forced posture, with the spine stiffened or laterally curved due to protective muscle tension. Pain may radiate to the buttocks. Lumbar pain may be induced by flexion of the hip and knee, and the straight leg raise test may be positive, but there is no sciatic nerve traction pain, and the straight leg raise strengthening test is negative.

# Differential Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
II. Chronic lumbar strain
       It can be caused by inadequate and timely treatment after acute lumbar sprain or long-term cumulative lumbar tissue damage. It is commonly manifested as lumbosacral soreness or dull pain, which worsens after fatigue, but improves after rest, changing body position, and local massage. It is difficult to maintain a bent posture, and severe pain may radiate to the buttocks and posterior side of the thigh. The most common tender point is at the attachment of the erector spinae muscle at the lumbosacral region. Deep pressure pain is present at the paravertebral, interspinous, and transverse process of the third lumbar vertebra. There may be tender points at the origin of the gluteal muscles and the buttocks. The straight leg raise test shows no radiation pain.

# Differential Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
III. Degenerative lumbar osteoarthritis
       It is mainly characterized by degenerative changes in the lumbar spine, with widespread bone and joint hyperplastic changes, and secondary clinical symptoms and signs. The clinical manifestations include marked stiffness or soreness in the morning, which gradually diminishes after activity. However, pain may worsen after prolonged activity. Rest and local massage can alleviate symptoms. There are usually no obvious tender points, and there is a comfortable sensation after local pressure. In patients with severe degeneration, the articular surfaces of the small joints are asymmetrical, and the incidence of lumbar disc degeneration in that segment is significantly increased, leading to osteophytes that compress the nerve roots, or due to lumbar instability and the convergence of small joint hyperplasia, stimulating the nerve roots, resulting in radicular pain in the lower extremities, mainly in the anterior and lateral aspect of the thigh. Sometimes, it may manifest as radicular pain, and it is necessary to differentiate from lumbar disc herniation. If necessary, combine with imaging examinations.

# Differential Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
IV. Third Lumbar Transverse Process Syndrome
      It is an extraspinal lumbar canal lesion. The soft tissue at the tip of the third lumbar transverse process is damaged due to injury, causing a series of pathological changes and leading to lumbar or lumbosacral pain. It is more common in young and middle-aged adults with weak lumbar and back muscles, and is more common in males. It has a history of trauma and poor working posture for a long time.
       The main symptoms are pain in the lumbar and gluteal regions, which worsens with activity. During prone position examination, there may be mild spasm and tenderness in one or both paravertebral erector spinae muscles. A hard nodule and string-like mass can be palpated at the end of the third lumbar transverse process, with marked tenderness. Sometimes, string-like masses and tenderness can be palpated at the posterior border of the gluteus medius or the superior border of the gluteus maximus. The straight leg raise test is negative, and there are no symptoms of nerve root irritation. Laboratory and imaging examinations show no special abnormalities.

# Differential Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Five, Spondylolysis and Spondylolisthesis of the Lumbar Vertebrae
       Spondylolysis refers to the defect or fracture of the pedicle between the superior and inferior articular processes of the lumbar vertebrae, causing the pedicle to lose its complete bony connection, also known as pedicle non-union. On the basis of spondylolysis, the vertebral body may produce anterior displacement, also known as true spondylolisthesis. If the vertebral body slips due to a complete pedicle, it is called pseudospondylolisthesis.
       When the pedicle is fractured, the ends of the pedicle move, forming a pseudojoint. Due to repeated movement and friction, a large amount of fibrocartilaginous bone callus is produced at the ends, and these proliferative fibrocartilaginous tissues can cause nerve root adhesions, resulting in lumbar and leg pain, and can cause nerve root compression, leading to radicular pain.
       Key points for differential diagnosis with lumbar disc herniation: ① Spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis generally have a longer course, with no obvious exacerbation or remission periods. ② The impact on the nerve roots is less than that of lumbar disc herniation. ③ X-ray examination can clearly diagnose the condition and determine the degree of slippage, and a lumbar dynamic X-ray film can be taken to determine the stability of the vertebral body structure. If necessary, CT and MRI examinations can be combined to make a judgment.

Seven, Gluteal Epineuritis
       Gluteal epineuritis refers to the compression of the gluteal epineurium at the exit of the bony fibrous canal or fascial exit, causing lumbar and gluteal pain and leg pain. The gluteal epineurium originates from the lateral branches of the posterior rami of the thoracic 11th to lumbar 1st nerves. Analysis of the cause: ① Overly large stripping during surgery, causing injury to the muscles and tendons attached to the transverse process, resulting in injury to the lateral branch of the posterior ramus of the spinal nerve. ② Bleeding during surgery, inflammatory reaction can stimulate and compress the nerve. ③ Swelling and ischemia of the nerve itself.

# Differential Diagnosis of Lumbar Disc Herniation
Thirteen, Myelitis
Clinical signs may include:
1. Motor impairment: Mainly manifested as upper motor neuron paralysis below the level of the lesion segment. However, in patients with acute onset, early transient flaccid paralysis may occur, known as spinal shock. After several days to several weeks, the tendons may gradually become hyperactive, muscle tone may increase, and pathological reflexes may appear. 
2. Sensory impairment: Sensory loss or reduction below the level of the lesion segment. Both deep and superficial sensations are affected to varying degrees, but the severity on both sides may not be symmetrical. If only one side of the spinal cord is involved, the pain and temperature sensation below the level of the lesion may be lost on the contralateral limb, while the deep sensation on the same side may be lost. In the area where the sensation is normal and the sensation is lost, there is often a hyperalgesic area.
3. Autonomic nervous system symptoms: In the acute phase, there may be urinary retention or constipation. In patients with spinal shock, urinary incontinence may gradually appear, and some cases may eventually become autonomous bladder. With different levels of damage, other autonomic nervous system dysfunction may occur.

Herpes zoster is an acute inflammatory skin disease caused by the varicella-zoster virus, known as "Chuan Shou Huo Long" or "Chuan Shou Huo Dan" in traditional Chinese medicine. It is commonly known as "She Dan" or "Spider疮". Its main feature is clustered blisters, distributed in a group along one side of the peripheral nerve, often accompanied by marked neuralgia. Differential diagnosis can be made by fully exposing the painful area during the examination, but it is easy to miss the diagnosis in the early stage of onset and atypical herpes manifestation.
