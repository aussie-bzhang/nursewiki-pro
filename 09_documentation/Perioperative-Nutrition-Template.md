How do humans obtain these nutrients?

Enteral Nutrition (EN): This is a nutritional support method that provides nutrients and other various nutrients required for metabolism through the gastrointestinal tract.

Parenteral Nutrition (PN): This is intravenous nutrition, referring to the nutritional support method of supplementing nutrition and body fluids through the venous system.

Enteral Nutrition (EN)
Refers to the administration of elemental preparations through nasogastric (nasoduodenal) or gastrostomy/jejunostomy tubes, including oral intake. The feasibility of EN mainly depends on whether the small intestine has the function to absorb various nutrients.

Nasogastric tube, nasoduodenal tube, nasojejunal tube, and gastrojejunal stoma tube.

1. Gastrointestinal obstruction
2. Gastrointestinal absorption dysfunction

  2) Small bowel disease
  3) Radiation enteritis
  4) Severe diarrhea
  5) Refractory vomiting

6. Hypermetabolic state, such as severe infection, burns, trauma
7. Inflammatory bowel disease
8. Perioperative period
9. Severe pregnancy-induced vomiting or anorexia nervosa

Complications of PN
PN is different from EN and is a mandatory nutritional support method, which is different from the physiological process of normal oral intake, so it is more prone to various complications.
1. Infection-related complications  Long-term PN leads to dysfunction of the intestinal mucosal barrier under stress conditions, and bacteria and endotoxins can penetrate the mucosal barrier and enter the body, causing the body to have a systemic inflammatory response again.
2. Metabolic complications  Such as: abnormal amino acid metabolism, electrolyte disturbance, liver and biliary system damage, etc.

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->

Complications of PN
3. Gastrointestinal complications  Long-term fasting and PN treatment can destroy the normal structure and function of the intestinal mucosa, leading to atrophy of the intestinal mucosal epithelial villi, thinning, flattening of the folds, thinning of the intestinal wall, thus affecting the intestinal barrier structure, reducing the function, and easily causing bacterial translocation and intestinal infection.
4. Complications related to venous puncture and catheter placement  Such as: catheter thrombosis, venous thrombosis, thrombophlebitis.
Basic principle: As long as gastrointestinal function allows, try to use enteral nutrition.

How can clinical medical staff ensure normal nutritional absorption in patients?
Under normal circumstances, after patients receive deep sedation or general anesthesia, their protective cough reflex and swallowing reflex will also weaken or disappear. Reflux of gastric contents can cause respiratory tract obstruction and aspiration pneumonia, which can even lead to asphyxiation death. Therefore, medical staff will require patients to fast from food and water before surgery. So, how long should the fasting from food and water be appropriate?
Traditional surgery requires fasting from food for more than 10 hours and from water for more than 8 hours.

Evidence for preoperative fasting
The expert consensus (2017) recommends that patients with no gastrointestinal motility disorders should fast from solid food for 6 hours before surgery and from clear fluids for 2 hours before surgery. If the patient has no history of diabetes, it is recommended to drink 400 ml of a beverage containing 12.5% carbohydrates 2 hours before surgery, which can alleviate hunger, thirst, and anxiety.

Consensus

Scientific evidence

① Reducing the fasting time to 2 hours before surgery does not increase the probability of esophageal reflux during anesthesia induction or extubation, and preoperative oral glucose water can alleviate insulin resistance and protein catabolism in patients after intestinal surgery. ② It can reduce postoperative insulin sensitivity; ③ It can reduce the stress response caused by surgery.

Literature

Chinese Expert Consensus and Guideline for Pathway Management of Accelerated Recovery Surgery (2018) recommends oral fluid diet 2 hours before surgery: clear water, sugar water, clear juice, clear tea, black coffee (without milk). The fasting time is shortened to 6 hours before surgery.

Preoperative Dietary Guidance
(1) Try to improve and correct the patient's malnutrition status
1. Understand the patient's dietary habits and encourage the intake of nutrient-rich and easily digestible foods;
2. Correct anemia, water, electrolyte, and acid-base imbalances;
3. Encourage normal evening meals unless otherwise instructed;
4. Correct any gastrointestinal abnormalities promptly to promote eating and nutritional absorption;
5. Provide nutritional supplements as necessary to improve nutritional status, but be aware of gastrointestinal complications of enteral nutrition and manage them promptly.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
Preoperative Diet
(2) Try to shorten the fasting and water intake time
1. No drinking 2 hours before surgery
   Typically, 12.5% carbohydrate solution 800ml is administered orally 10 hours before surgery (as per physician's instructions); drink ≤400ml 2 hours before surgery, 200ml for diabetic patients, and check blood glucose;
2. Fasting time is shortened to 6 hours before surgery (starchy solid foods can be consumed before this, but fried, fatty, and meat products require a longer fasting time).
3. In special cases where the surgery time cannot be determined, carbohydrates and electrolytes can be supplemented parenterally.
Potassium Metabolism Characteristics: Fast absorption in the intestines, slow excretion by the kidneys, slow entry into cells, more intake leads to more excretion, less intake leads to less excretion, and no intake still leads to excretion.

What to Note about Parenteral Nutrition?
1. Parenteral nutrition should not be performed for a long time unless necessary;
2. Monitor electrolytes and blood glucose levels regularly;
3. Assess the patient's tolerance to enteral nutrition daily and provide timely enteral nutrition support as needed;
4. Monitor trace elements as necessary.
