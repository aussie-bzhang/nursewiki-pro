# Preoperative Preparation for Primary Hypercortisolism

Comprehensive preoperative assessment, evaluation of cardiac function
Control blood pressure, improve cardiac function
Control blood glucose
Correct acid-base imbalance and electrolyte disturbance
X-ray or bone density measurement of the skeletal system, evaluate osteoporosis and possible fractures
Note that a few patients may have mental and psychological disorders

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
# Primary Hypercortisolism
Substitution Therapy with Glucocorticoids

Principles of administration: ①Administer dexamethasone or hydrocortisone acetate intramuscularly preoperatively; ②Intravenous hydrocortisone on the day of surgery; ③During the period of fasting after surgery, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, or hydrocortisone acetate can be administered intravenously or intramuscularly, and oral prednisone can be substituted after eating. ④Gradually reduce the dose of corticosteroids postoperatively until discontinuation. In case of disease, physiological stress factors, or symptoms of adrenal insufficiency, increase the dose by 1/2 or 1 times the original dose, and intravenous hydrocortisone can be used if symptoms are severe.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# Primary Hypercortisolism
Substitution Therapy with Glucocorticoids

Example of administration schedule: ①2mg of dexamethasone intramuscularly 1 day before surgery, and 2mg of dexamethasone intramuscularly on the day of surgery; ②100-200mg of hydrocortisone intravenously during surgery; ③100-200mg of hydrocortisone intravenously on the day after surgery; ④Starting with 2mg of dexamethasone intramuscularly every 6 hours on the first day after surgery, gradually reducing to 2mg every 12 hours, then changing to prednisone 20-25mg/day, and gradually reducing to 10-15mg before discharge. After discharge, reduce by 2.5mg every 4 weeks. ⑤Monitor plasma cortisol and ACTH, and confirm the recovery of adrenal cortical function to normal before discontinuation, which usually takes 6-8 months.

# Common Glucocorticoids Classification and Equivalence Conversion

Prednisone (Prednisolone) and hydrocortisone need to be converted into prednisolone and hydrocortisone in the liver after entering the body to exert their effects.

Adrenal Crisis

Also known as acute adrenal insufficiency

——Electrolyte disturbance and acute peripheral circulatory failure due to insufficient sugar and salt corticosteroids

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# Adrenal Crisis  Clinical Manifestations

Mostly occur within 24-48 hours after surgery, and may appear within a few hours
Elevated or decreased body temperature;
Fatigue, weakness, malaise, drowsiness;
Restlessness, confusion, or coma;
Heart rate increased, up to 160bpm or more;
In case of electrolyte disturbance, arrhythmia or cardiac arrest may occur;
Cold extremities, difficulty breathing, hypotension, and other shock symptoms;

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
# Adrenal Crisis  Management

Rapidly judge based on clinical manifestations, symptoms, and signs;
No need to wait for laboratory results;
In the first 1-2 hours, rapidly administer 100-200mg of hydrocortisone intravenously, reaching 500-600mg within 5-6 hours, and 300mg on the 2-3rd day, then reduce by 100mg per day;
Correct water and electrolyte disturbance;
Prevent and treat hypoglycemia (be aware of iatrogenic hypoglycemia caused by excessive insulin);
Dexamethasone and betamethasone have weak mineralocorticoid effects and are not suitable for substitution.
Keywords: Rapid!

# Preoperative Management of Primary Hyperaldosteronism

Attention to the assessment of the heart, kidney, brain, and vascular systems
Correct hypertension and hypokalemia
For patients with normal renal function, recommend spironolactone preoperative preparation, 100-400mg, twice or four times a day. Caution should be exercised in patients with renal insufficiency (hyperkalemia!)
If undergoing subtotal or total adrenalectomy, corticosteroids should be supplemented preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoperatively.

# Postoperative Management of Primary Hyperaldosteronism

Monitor blood electrolyte levels;
Discontinue potassium salts, spironolactone, and antihypertensive drugs on the first day after surgery, and adjust according to actual conditions if blood pressure fluctuates.
In the first few weeks after surgery, a diet rich in sodium is recommended,

### Notes:
to avoid high potassium due to long-term suppression of the contralateral adrenal gland, insufficient aldosterone secretion

# Pheochromocytoma  Preoperative
Importance and objectives of drug preparation

Comprehensive preoperative preparation is the key to surgical success. Before the routine use of alpha-receptor blockers, the mortality rate of pheochromocytoma surgery was 24%-50%.
Block the excessive action of CA, maintain normal blood pressure, heart rate/rhythm, and improve the function of the heart and other organs;
Correct the deficiency of effective blood volume;
Prevent the release of a large amount of CA induced by surgery and anesthesia, which causes blood pressure fluctuations, and reduce serious complications such as acute pulmonary edema and heart failure.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
# Pheochromocytoma  Preoperative
Control of Hypertension

1. Alpha-receptor blockers (recommended)
The most commonly used is the long-acting non-selective alpha-receptor blocker phentolamine (10mg qd/bid, increasing by 10-20mg every 2-3 days); alpha1-receptor blockers: prazosin (2-5mg, 2-3 times a day), terazosin (2-5mg/day), doxazosin (2-16mg/day).
2. Calcium channel blockers
They can control blood pressure, arrhythmia, prevent coronary spasm, and improve cardiac function, but they will not cause hypotension. However, they cannot improve the hemodynamic changes caused by catecholamines when used alone.

### Notes:
But note that these α-adrenergic receptor blockers have an incomplete blocking effect; during medication, increasing the intake of salt-containing fluids in the diet can help reduce the occurrence of orthostatic hypotension and aid in volume expansion. Usually, calcium channel blockers are only used as a supplement to α-adrenergic blockers, which can reduce the dosage of the former and decrease the occurrence of orthostatic hypotension.

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
# Pheochromocytoma   Preoperative

Control of arrhythmias
Tachycardia (>100-120 beats/min) or supraventricular arrhythmias, add β-adrenergic blockers. It is recommended to use β1-receptor blockers: atenolol, metoprolol (Betacore).

Management of hypertensive crisis
Phentolamine (Regitine), sodium nitroprusside, or nicardipine (Perindopril) can be administered intravenously.

### Notes:
Non-selective β-adrenergic blockers must be used 2-3 days after α-adrenergic blockers, otherwise, they may block the vasodilatory effect of β2 receptors, which can induce life-threatening complications such as hypertensive crisis, pulmonary edema, and myocardial infarction.

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
# Pheochromocytoma   Preoperative
Timing and standards for drug preparation

Recommended 7-10 days, 4-6 weeks for frequent episodes
Ideal blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, ideal heart rate <80-90 beats/min
No paroxysmal hypertension, palpitations, or sweating
Weight tends to increase, HCT <45%
Mild nasal congestion, nail bed redness, disappearance of warmth or coldness in the extremities
For patients with no obvious hypertension or atypical symptoms, it is still recommended to perform preoperative CA blocking treatment.
The effect of volume expansion is not clear.

### Notes:
There is currently no evidence to indicate that preoperative blood transfusion and volume expansion can reduce the risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications, especially in patients with congestive heart failure and renal insufficiency, caution should be exercised.

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
# Pheochromocytoma   Intraoperative

Phentolamine, dopamine, adrenaline, hydrocortisone are on standby at all times!
Radial artery catheterization, central venous (right internal jugular vein) catheterization for dynamic monitoring of arterial pressure and central venous pressure!
At least two venous access lines should be established;
During the operation, when touching the adrenal lesion, pay close attention to changes in heart rate and blood pressure.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Pheochromocytoma   Postoperative

ICU monitoring for 24-48 hours, continuous ECG, arterial pressure, and CVP monitoring
Postoperative hypertension, hypotension, and hypoglycemia are common, routine and moderate volume expansion and 5% GS supplementation should be maintained to keep a positive balance
If there is refractory hypotension that is difficult to correct, pay attention to whether there is a deficiency of corticosteroids.
