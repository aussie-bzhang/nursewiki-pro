Lumbar Disc Herniation
                     Sharing Information

<!-- Slide number: 2 -->
Concept
Lumbar disc herniation refers to a condition where, due to aging, wear and tear, degeneration, and external forces, the annulus fibrosus ruptures, causing the nucleus pulposus to protrude or extrude from the rupture site, compressing the lumbar nerve roots or cauda equina, stimulating the corresponding lumbar intervertebral joints and accessory tissues, and resulting in a series of radicular symptoms such as lumbar and leg pain, numbness, and other neurological signs. Lumbar disc herniation has the highest incidence in the lumbar 4-5 and lumbar 5-sacral 1 regions, accounting for about 95%.

Let's Get to Know the Lumbar Spine

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Lumbar spine (L) is located at the lower part of the spine. It has the functions of movement, load-bearing, and protection. It connects the thoracic spine above and the sacrum below, with particularly important load-bearing and stability functions. The anterior part of the lumbar spine is connected by vertebral bodies with intervertebral discs and longitudinal ligaments; the posterior part is composed of vertebral arches, vertebral plates, transverse processes, and spinous processes. They are connected by joints, ligaments, and muscles, among others. The anterior and posterior structures of the lumbar spine enclose the vertebral foramen, and the vertebral segments are sequentially connected to form the vertebral canal, which contains the spinal cord and nerves.

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
What is an intervertebral disc? An intervertebral disc is a connecting structure between adjacent vertebral bodies, including the external cartilage plate, annulus fibrosus, and the internal nucleus pulposus tissue.

Composition of the intervertebral disc? (1) Annulus fibrosus: It is a fibrocartilaginous tissue at the periphery of the intervertebral disc, which is hard and elastic. The annulus fibrosus is thicker in front, so the nucleus pulposus is posterior, and it is more common for it to protrude or extrude posteriorly. (2) Nucleus pulposus: It is a white, gelatinous-like substance containing chondrocytes and fibroblasts, containing a lot of water to regulate the pressure of the intervertebral disc. With age, the water content decreases. For example, in childhood, the water content can be over 80%, while in old age, it can be below 70%. The nucleus pulposus is surrounded by the annulus fibrosus, like a ball bearing, and the vertebral bodies roll on it above, so that the pressure can be transmitted to the annulus fibrosus, therefore, the intervertebral disc acts as an elastic pad, which can cushion external forces. (3) Cartilage plate: It is hyaline cartilage, forming the upper and lower walls of the intervertebral disc, connecting with the cancellous bone of the vertebral bodies, and merging with the annulus fibrosus, sealing the nucleus pulposus within, so that when the cartilage plate is intact, the nucleus pulposus is neither easy to protrude into the cancellous bone of the upper and lower vertebral bodies nor to protrude posteriorly.

Comparison of Normal and Degenerative Intervertebral Disc Height

How many intervertebral discs does the human body have? There are only 23 intervertebral discs in the human body. They are located between two vertebral bodies. The lumbar intervertebral discs are thick, about 9 millimeters. There are lumbar intervertebral discs between lumbar 1 and the sacrum. What people often refer to as intervertebral disc herniation actually refers to lumbar intervertebral disc herniation. In fact, there are intervertebral discs between the cervical and thoracic spines, which can also herniate, but the symptoms, signs, and treatment methods are different.

Clinical Manifestations
Patients often present with lumbar pain, with mild cases only feeling acid and discomfort in the lumbar region, and severe cases feeling like a knife cut or needle pricking, worse at night, unable to travel far, and pain exacerbated by walking.
Pain is exacerbated when abdominal pressure is increased, such as bending over, extending the knees, sitting up, coughing, sneezing, and straining during defecation.
Radioactive lower limb pain and numbness may occur simultaneously.
A few patients have urinary and fecal dysfunction.

Causes
1. Intervertebral disc degeneration
2. The effect of external forces
3. The anatomical weaknesses of the intervertebral disc itself
The triggering factors for lumbar disc herniation include:
1. Sudden weight-bearing or a sprain is the main cause of annular fibrosis rupture.
2. Lumbar trauma
3. Incorrect posture
4. Increased abdominal pressure
5. Exposure to cold

Pathology and Classification
Classification according to the position of intervertebral disc protrusion:
1. Lateral posterior type
2. Central type
Classification according to pathological changes and CT, MRI findings:
1. Bulging type: partial rupture of the annulus fibrosus, superficially intact, the nucleus pulposus protrudes locally into the vertebral canal due to pressure.
2. Protruding type: complete rupture of the annulus fibrosus, the nucleus pulposus protrudes into the vertebral canal, covered only by the posterior longitudinal ligament or a layer of fibrous membrane, with an uneven surface.
3. Extruding type: the ruptured, protruding intervertebral disc tissue or fragments fall into the vertebral canal or become completely free.

Special Signs
(1) Straight Leg Raising Test and Strengthening Test The patient lies on their back, extends their knees, and the affected limb is passively raised. Normally, the nerve roots have a 4mm sliding degree, and the lower limb is raised to 60°～70° before feeling discomfort in the popliteal fossa. In patients with lumbar disc herniation, the nerve roots are compressed or adhered, causing a reduction or disappearance of the sliding degree, and pain radiating to the sciatic nerve occurs when the limb is raised within 60°, known as a positive straight leg raising test. In positive patients, slowly lower the height of the limb until the radiation pain disappears, and then passively flex the ankle joint on the affected side to induce radiation pain again, known as a positive strengthening test. Sometimes, due to a large nucleus pulposus, raising the healthy limb can also pull the dura mater and induce radiation pain in the affected side of the sciatic nerve.

How to Prevent and Prevent Recurrence
1. Pay attention to adequate bed rest, preferably lying on a hard board bed or a bed with thin soft padding. The purpose is to alleviate the pressure of body weight on the intervertebral disc and reduce pain.
2. Pay attention to maintaining warmth in the lower back, avoid engaging in overloading work, such as bending over to lift heavy objects, prolonged bending over work, and avoid sudden squatting, sudden bending over, and sudden turning movements.
3. Pay attention to posture in daily life, avoid maintaining a single posture for a long time, especially sitting. Sitting on a low stool places a large amount of stress on the lower back, which is not suitable for those with lower back pain.
4. Strengthen muscle exercises consistently. Exercises for strengthening the lumbar and back muscles include three-point support, five-point support, and flying swan pose, etc. Swimming is particularly effective for those with lower back pain. Other exercises such as physical exercises, Tai Chi, and jogging are also beneficial for the rehabilitation of lumbar and back muscles.
